## 4.1 Frames

Our decisions, big and small, are often influenced by the way information is presented to us. If the way the information has been presented is appealing to us, then the bias is positive. If we do not like it, then the bias is negative.

Framing is largely the job of marketing. The goal of marketers is to create bias which favours their companies’ products. So, when selling a product, a company promotes the positive features and benefits. Often the less attractive features are left out of promotional material, or if they are legally required, they are put into small print. Again, the effect of the way information is presented is the ‘framing effect’.

When it comes to problem solving and decision making, we have to be aware of how questions and information are framed. We need to keep an eye out for any implied limitations or constraints, assumed information, and be aware of our own biases and preferences.

Now that we are aware of the potential negative impact of framing, we can use it to create a positive impact. A decision frame is simply a set of criteria which can be used to support decision-making. Earlier, we looked at SCAMPER as a constructive way to think differently about problems to find creative solutions. In choosing SCAMPER to create solutions, we also applied specific limitations and constraints at each step. For example, with the first stage, Substitute, we could only look at replacing and substituting elements of the product or situation. This would be a problem if we had stopped after the first or second step, because the options would have been very limited. However, by doing each step in SCAMPER, we are forced to consider all aspects, and in doing so we come up with a broader range of options.

Choosing a frame to help you with decision-making can be a challenge. You should choose one which is best suited to the situation.

## 4.2 Pro’s and Con’s

Pros and cons are arguments for (pros) and against (cons) a proposal. When you prepare a list of pros and cons, you are simply preparing a list of good points and bad points.

As a frame, a pros and cons list can be a simple yet effective way of quickly evaluating options. When the best decision is not clear, and a decision needs to be made quickly, or when the impact of the decision is not so important or wide-ranging, a pros and cons list can be an effective process for reaching a considered decision.

However, it also has limitations. It assumes that everything can be put into either good or bad categories. The evaluation that occurs when creating a pros and cons list is often very subjective; what is good for one person may be bad for another. Finally, the decision is often made based on how many pros there are in the list versus how many cons there are. However, this assumes that the scope and impact of each item is equal. Usually this is not the case.

## 4.3 Weighted Criteria Matrix

To help clarify the value of options, it can be useful to evaluate options based on a set of criteria and their pre-determined value. This is often referred to as a weighted criteria matrix.

1.  Identify the criteria. The criteria are the factors which are important for your organization within the context of the situation. For example, when hiring a new employee, the criteria could be ‘education’, ‘qualifications’, ‘skills’ and ‘character’.

2.  Weigh the criteria. Give each of the criteria a weight (a value) which reflects its importance in the decision-making process.

3.  Score each option against each criterion. Use a consistent method for evaluating your options. For example, you could assign each option a score between 1 and 5 for every criterion.

4.  Tally the results. Multiply the weight by the score for each option, and then total the scores.

In this example, a weighted criteria matrix is used to help decide which candidate to hire. Candidates have already been interviewed and the list was narrowed down to three potential hires.
