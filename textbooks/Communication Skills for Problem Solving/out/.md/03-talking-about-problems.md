## 2.1 Defining the Problem

Even before we start to solve a problem, we first need to understand it. That means understanding;

1.  The nature of the problem:

<!-- -->

1.  What do you know about the problem?

2.  How did it come about?

3.  What information is missing, and how can you get it?

<!-- -->

2.  The impact the problem has had, continues to have, or will have:

<!-- -->

1.  Who and what has been affected, or will be affected by the problem if there is no intervention?

2.  What has the problem cost your organization, your customers, your partners?

<!-- -->

3.  The scope of the problem:

<!-- -->

1.  How wide-ranging is the problem?

2.  Is it organization-wide, or is it localized?

3.  Does it affect the entire product range, or just one line?

4.  Is it market-wide, or isolated to one segment?

When answering the above questions, we have to keep in mind the concept of bounded rationality. Decision makers are limited by their own knowledge, values, attitudes and skills. Efforts to mitigate those limits at some point stop producing results. For example, extensive research into the cause of a problem can soon become costly and, given the problem, too slow. The solution needs to be framed within the context and with a reasonable understanding of the constraints.

Good problem definitions connect the specifics of the context of the problem, and the future desired state, in a way that facilitates idea generation. More simply, we make a connection between the current situation (the problem) and how we want it to be (the ideal). From this, we should be able to come up with ideas to create solutions.

When sharing a problem, make sure to include the following:

## 2.2 Finding the Cause of a Problem

One way to solve a problem is to remove its cause. However, this can be difficult when a problem has more than one cause. Therefore, it is important to try and identify the cause or causes and deal with them one by one.

### The 5 Why’s for Root-Cause Analysis

The 5 Why’s method is part of the Toyota Production System which was developed in the 1930s by Sakichi Toyoda, a Japanese inventor and industrialist, and founder of Toyota Industries. The technique became an integral part of the Lean management philosophy and was popularized in the 1970s.

“The basis of Toyota’s scientific approach is to ask why five times whenever we find a problem. By repeating why five times, the nature of the problem as well as its solution becomes clear.“ Taiichi Ohno

One of the key factors for successful implementation of the technique is to make an informed decision. This means that the decision-making process should be based on an insightful understanding of what is actually happening on the work floor.

You'll know that you've revealed the root cause of the problem when asking "why" produces no more useful responses, and you can go no further. An appropriate counter-measure or process change should then become evident.

### Fishbone Diagrams

A fishbone diagram, such as the one below, can be useful for analyzing complex problems with many possible causes. Also referred to as the Ishikawa diagram or a cause and effect diagram, it was invented by Kaoru Ishikawa, who pioneered quality management techniques in Japan in the 1960s. It is commonly known as a fishbone diagram because of its shape.

The 'fish head' represents the main problem. The potential causes of the problem, usually derived from brainstorming sessions or research, are indicated in the 'fish bones' of the diagram.

<img src="assets/media/image4.webp" style="width:4.33071in;height:3.14409in" />

The first step is to identify the problem, and then the possible causes. Then, go an extra step and identify potential sub-causes. This will give a deeper understanding of the context surrounding the problem.

CRITICAL THINKING

Think of a problem you solved recently. Draw a fishbone diagram similar to the one above. Clearly label (a) the problem, (b) it’s causes, and (c) any sub-causes. Then, explain the problem to a partner.
