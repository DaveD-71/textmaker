# 13 Asking Questions

> *Skills for asking questions*

- **About Questions**

> Questions are the foundation of thinking and understanding. They are the natural product of an active mind; the scientific examination of information; and the keys that open the doors to comprehension.
>
> Questions are an essential part of thinking, communication and meetings.

- **Role of the Participants**

> Good meetings have many questions.
>
> Participants in meetings have an obligation to ask questions during the question period. Asking questions is their contribution to the success of the meeting.
>
> “No questions” indicates inattention, disinterest, or an inability to participate in the discussion.

- **Creating Questions**

> Questions are created from our *curiosity* and *analyzing* information with the *Wh* Questions: *who, where, what, when, why, and how.*
>
> Ask yourself the *Wh* questions about information. If you can’t give yourself an answer then that becomes a question for the speaker.
>
> There are basically three types of questions:

- Type 1 Clarify and confirm information.

- Type 2 Ask for more information.

- Type 3 Challenge or test ideas and information. Testing ideas and information is very important for making good decisions.

> There are examples of these questions in Unit 16: Expressions for Meetings.

- **Preparing your Questions**

  1.  **Before the meeting**

> Before the meeting think about the issues and prepare questions. Ask yourself -

- What do you know about the issue?

- What do you think about the issue?

- What do you <u>want to know</u> or <u>need to know</u> about the issue?

  1.  **Think in the language of the meeting**

> Before and during the meeting, think in the same language as the meeting. If the meeting is in English, then think in English. Don’t try to translate.

- Translation is difficult, takes time, and the results are poor. Don’t try to do it!

- Thinking in Japanese and translating is a barrier to understanding.

  1.  **Listen during the meeting**

> During the meeting, listen carefully to the speaker, take notes, and think about your questions.

- Did the speaker answer your prepared questions? If not, ask your questions.

- What new information did you hear? Ask for clarification or more information.

- If the speaker gave information that you did not catch, ask the speaker to repeat the information.

  1.  **Use “Creative negativity”**

> Think with *creative negativity* about the presentation and proposal.

- Don’t automatically agree with the speaker.

- Even if you agree with the speaker, creative negativity will help create questions that will actually support the speaker’s position.

  1.  **Don’t wait!**

> Try to ask your first question at the beginning of the Q&A session.

- Don’t wait. Try to be the first person to ask a question. You can ask more questions later.

- **The Language and Patterns of Questions**

  1.  **Fixed expressions**

> Use the fixed expressions and patterns for asking questions.

- Using fixed expressions and patterns makes it easier for you to ask questions and easier for the speaker to understand and answer your questions.

- You will find expressions for asking questions in Unit 16: Expressions for Meetings.

- During the Q&A session never say, “May I ask a question?” The purpose of the Q&A session is to ask questions: there is no need to ask permission!

  1.  **Question patterns**

> There is a common pattern to asking questions that is simple and effective:
>
> First identify the issue, then ask the question.

- <u>You proposed</u> increasing surveillance in the high-risk areas. <u>I’d like to ask about the</u> cost. <u>How would</u> the cost be shared?

  1.  **Asking two questions**

> In the Q&A session you can ask more than one question at a time but no more than two. Asking more than two questions at a time takes too much time.
>
> If you have two questions, don’t ask the two questions together. It is confusing.
>
> Begin by announcing you have two questions then ask your first question. Listen to the reply, then ask your second question. For example:
>
> P I’d like to ask two questions. My first question is…
>
> S (Response)
>
> P Thank you. And my second question is…
>
> S (Response)
>
> P Thank you.

- **Taking Your Turn in Q&A: Raise your hand?**

> The protocol for asking questions during the question period in a conference or discussion varies.

- During the question period in a large conference, it is necessary to raise your hand in order for the chairperson to see you, or you may have to go to a central microphone and wait your turn.

- In a small discussion group it is not usually necessary to raise your hand or wait for the chairperson to call on you to speak. Simply wait your turn and speak up at the right time.

<!-- -->

- If you have difficulty getting your turn, simply look at the chairperson and raise your hand just enough to be seen (it is not necessary to raise your hand above your shoulder.) The chair will see you and call on you.

- **The Chairperson & Questions**

> In a conference the chairperson manages the proceedings but does not usually ask questions during the Q&A session. There are two exceptions:

1.  At the beginning of the question period, if there are no questions from the audience, the chairperson must ask questions.

- This is the <u>only</u> condition in which the chair asks questions before the audience members. In this situation the chair says, “While you’re thinking, I’d like to ask a question. …”

  1.  At the end of the question period if there are no more questions and if there is time, the chair may ask a question. The chairperson <u>must</u> take all the questions from the audience before asking questions of his or her own.
