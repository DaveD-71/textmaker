- **Social Protocol of Communication**

> Effective communication follows accepted protocols to achieve success. The social protocol of communication consists of the patterns and language suitable for the situation.
>
> Following appropriate social protocol helps to create good communication.
>
> Protocol consists of actions, language, and timing:
>
> 1\. What to do and what to say.
>
> 2\. When to do it and when to say it.

- **Introducing Yourself – Cultural Differences**

> Meeting new people is a common experience. The patterns of introductions vary from culture to culture. The differences in form and procedure are greater than simply bowing or shaking hands, however, the purpose of introductions is exactly the same: to begin a new relationship on a firm foundation of mutual respect and good communication.
>
> For example, in the protocol of Japanese introductions, people usually introduce themselves individually by saying their own name and presenting a business card. In western culture, however, people are usually introduced by another person.
>
> Read and study the Model Introductions below. Notice the patterns and language: who says what, and when.

- **Small Talk**

> Participating in small talk is an important social skill. It is an exchange of brief, linked comments and questions on a neutral subject.
>
> Small talk is not primarily about exchanging information. It is mostly about connecting with others and showing interest in them though simple questions, responses and comments.

- **Language Note: Greetings**

> The greeting, “Nice to meet you”, is a simple common expression however, it is often misused!

- In *“Nice to meet you”*, “meet” means to exchange names. If you have not exchanged names with someone, you have not met.

- “Nice to meet you” is not a greeting like “Hello.” Do not say, “Nice to meet you.” before exchanging names.

- A common greeting for people you know is, “Nice to <u>see</u> you again.”

- Avoid saying, “Nice to ~~<u>meet</u>~~ you again”. It is not a common greeting.

- **Vocabulary for introducing others**

> Other expressions for third party introductions:

- I’d like to introduce (you to) Mr. Ozora.

- May I introduce (you to) Mr. Ozora?

- Let me introduce (you to) Mr. Ozora.

- Have you met Mr. Ozora?

- I don’t think you’ve met Mr. Ozora.

- **About Shaking Hands**

> Shaking hands is a common part of international business. It is used for greetings and saying good-bye, and for signifying agreement. As a courtesy, people usually stand up to shake hands.

- The handshake is simple and it should be done well. Practice these steps:

  1.  <img src="assets/media/image13.png" style="width:1.11181in;height:0.62361in" />Extend your open hand and make eye contact.

  2.  Let your hand meet your counterpart’s at the base of the thumbs.

  3.  Close your hand gently but firmly, and smile.

  4.  Shake three or four times – 1, 2, 3, (4), let go and…

  5.  Open your hand and bring it back to your side.

- Styles of handshakes vary around the world:

  - In North America, Australia, and New Zealand people like firm handshakes. Almost everywhere else – Europe, South America, Africa and Asia – people prefer a softer handshake.

  - Except in North America, men do not offer to shake hands with women. In most cultures it is the woman who initiates the handshake.

- Worried about sweaty hands?

> Here’s a tip: Carry your handkerchief in your right hand before shaking hands. Move the handkerchief to your left hand just before shaking hands.
