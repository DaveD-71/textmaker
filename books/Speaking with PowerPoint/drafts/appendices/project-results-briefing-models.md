# Project results briefing models

These models show how a presenter can report project results, explain simple evidence, and recommend next steps. The data is fictional for practice. Do not replace it with real client, account, trade, applicant, or internal control information.

Use these models when you need to practise:

- results-evidence-next steps structure
- chart and data explanation
- cautious claims
- online or recorded delivery adaptation
- evidence-based Q&A
- a final recommendation or action request

## Model 1: Business-client / finance and trading operations

### Scenario brief

Seika Capital Operations tested a new exception resolution process for trade confirmations. The old process used separate email threads and manual handoff messages, so some exceptions were not owned clearly before the end-of-day preparation window.

The pilot used a shared exception log and a fixed daily checkpoint. After four weeks, the presenter reports the results and recommends expanding the process to two more desks.

All organization names and data in this model are fictional for practice.

### Audience and purpose

Audience:

- operations leadership
- risk/control representative
- settlement team lead
- client-service manager

Purpose: report pilot results and request approval for controlled expansion.

The audience needs to decide whether the result is strong enough to expand the process, and what safeguards are needed before expansion.

### Core message

The pilot reduced late confirmations and made ownership clearer, so we should expand it to two more desks with two small controls: a morning log check and one owner for duplicate entries.

### Outline

1. Pilot objective: reduce delays caused by unclear handoffs.
2. Results: late confirmations and rework time both fell.
3. What we learned: ownership improved, but morning updates need tightening.
4. Recommendation: expand to two more desks with minor controls.
5. Action: approve the expansion and review the result after six weeks.

### Delivery context profile

Delivery format: internal project-results briefing.

Time: 8 minutes plus 5 minutes for Q&A.

Materials:

- one before/after chart
- one "what changed" table
- one recommendation summary
- optional backup table with volume-adjusted results

Delivery adaptation:

- In person: show the before/after chart first, then use the table only when explaining the reason for the recommendation.
- Online: enlarge the chart labels and pause after the main result before moving to the details.
- Async or recorded: add one sentence before the chart: "The key result is the reduction in late confirmations; the table after the chart explains the two remaining issues."

Confidentiality check: do not include real desk names, client names, trade IDs, account information, ticket numbers, market predictions, control findings, or regulatory advice.

### Sample opening

Good morning. Today I will report the results of the exception resolution pilot and recommend the next step. The pilot had one main goal: to reduce late trade confirmations caused by unclear handoffs. The result is positive, but not perfect. Late confirmations fell from 38 to 27, and average rework time fell from 22 minutes to 16 minutes. Based on this, I recommend expanding the process to two more desks with two small controls before we scale it further.

### Chart and data artifact

**Fictional practice data: Seika Capital Operations exception resolution pilot**

| Measure | Before pilot | After pilot | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Late confirmations | 38 | 27 | down 29% |
| Average rework time per exception | 22 minutes | 16 minutes | down 6 minutes |
| Users who said ownership was clearer | not measured | 82% | new measure |
| Duplicate log entries | not tracked | 9 cases | issue to repair |

Suggested chart: a before/after bar chart with two bars for late confirmations and two bars for average rework time. Use the title: **Pilot reduced late confirmations and rework time**.

Chart explanation:

The chart shows two changes after the pilot. First, late confirmations fell from 38 to 27. That is a reduction of about 29%. Second, average rework time fell from 22 minutes to 16 minutes. This does not prove that the new process caused every improvement, because volume and staffing can also affect the result. However, the user feedback and the lower rework time both suggest that ownership became clearer.

### Language-analysis excerpt

> The result is positive, but not perfect. Late confirmations fell from 38 to 27, and average rework time fell from 22 minutes to 16 minutes. This suggests that the shared log helped teams see ownership earlier. However, we should not expand without controls. I recommend adding a morning log check and naming one owner for duplicate entries before we include two more desks.

Language points:

- "positive, but not perfect" gives a balanced evaluation.
- "fell from...to..." explains the data clearly.
- "suggests" is cautious; it does not overclaim.
- "we should not expand without controls" acknowledges risk.
- "I recommend..." gives a clear final action.

### Q&A pairs

| Function | Question | Model answer |
|---|---|---|
| Clarify the question | Are you saying the pilot solved the delay problem? | If I understand correctly, you are asking whether the problem is fully solved. No, not fully. The result improved, but we still need to fix morning updates and duplicate log entries. |
| Answer directly | Is the improvement large enough to expand? | Yes, I think it is large enough for a controlled expansion. Late confirmations fell by about 29%, and rework time also fell. I am not recommending full rollout yet. |
| Bridge to evidence | Could this be because volume was lower during the pilot? | That is possible, so we checked the result against average daily volume. The improvement was still visible, although the exact percentage was smaller. That is why I recommend expansion, not full rollout. |
| Acknowledge risk or limitation | Does the shared log create a control risk? | It could create a risk if ownership is unclear. To reduce that risk, each exception needs one named owner, and duplicate entries must be checked each morning. |
| Defer safely | Can you confirm whether this meets all control requirements? | I cannot confirm the full control position today. I can say that the pilot did not change the approval process. I will confirm the control review result with the risk/control team before expansion. |
| Confirm follow-up or next action | What do you need from us today? | Today I need approval to expand the pilot to two more desks for six weeks, with the two controls shown on the final slide. I will report the next result after the six-week review. |

## Model 2: Government-agency administrative project results

### Scenario brief

Midori Ward Administrative Services Office tested a new intake checklist for application forms. Before the trial, many forms were returned because attachments were missing or key fields were incomplete. Returned applications created delays, repeat inquiries, and extra staff work.

The trial used a short front-counter checklist and a shared FAQ sheet. After one month, the presenter reports the results and recommends expanding the checklist to two more high-volume application types.

All organization names and data in this model are fictional for practice.

### Audience and purpose

Audience:

- department leadership
- section manager
- counter-service staff
- document-review staff

Purpose: report trial results and request approval to expand the checklist.

The audience needs to decide whether the checklist should be used with more application types, and who will own updates when requirements change.

### Core message

The intake checklist reduced preventable returned applications, so we should expand it to two more high-volume application types and assign one owner for checklist updates.

### Outline

1. Trial objective: reduce returns caused by preventable intake errors.
2. Results: returned applications and repeat inquiries both fell.
3. What we learned: the checklist works best with short staff reminders.
4. Recommendation: expand to two more application types.
5. Action: approve expansion and assign update ownership.

### Delivery context profile

Delivery format: internal administrative results briefing.

Time: 8 minutes plus 5 minutes for Q&A.

Materials:

- one before/after chart
- one short process note showing where the checklist is used
- one expansion recommendation
- optional backup table with staff feedback

Delivery adaptation:

- In person: point to the intake step where the checklist is used, then explain the result chart.
- Online: show the process note and chart on separate screens or pages so the labels remain readable.
- Async or recorded: use a short storyboard: problem, checklist step, result chart, recommendation, next action.

Accessibility check: if the checklist later becomes staff-facing or public-facing guidance, the language should be plain, readable, and usable by staff who rotate into the counter role.

### Sample opening

Good afternoon. I will report the results of the intake checklist trial and recommend the next step. The trial focused on one practical problem: too many applications were returned because preventable errors were found after formal review. During the trial, returned applications fell from 142 to 111, and repeat inquiries fell from 86 to 68. The result suggests that the checklist helped, so I recommend expanding it to two more application types and assigning one owner for updates.

### Chart and data artifact

**Fictional practice data: Midori Ward intake checklist trial**

| Measure | Before trial | After trial | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Returned applications | 142 | 111 | down 22% |
| Repeat inquiries | 86 | 68 | down 21% |
| Attachment-related returns | 77 | 58 | down 24% |
| Added counter time | not measured | about 40 seconds per intake | issue to monitor |

Suggested chart: a before/after bar chart with two bars for returned applications and two bars for repeat inquiries. Use the title: **Checklist trial reduced returns and repeat inquiries**.

Chart explanation:

The chart shows that returned applications fell from 142 to 111 after the checklist trial. Repeat inquiries also fell, from 86 to 68. This is a useful improvement, but we should be careful. The trial was only one month, and staff reported that the checklist added about 40 seconds to each intake. For that reason, the next step should be expansion to two application types, not full office-wide rollout.

### Language-analysis excerpt

> The checklist appears to have reduced preventable errors at intake. Returned applications fell by 22%, and repeat inquiries fell by 21%. At the same time, staff reported that the checklist added about 40 seconds to each intake. For this reason, I recommend a limited expansion with short staff reminders and one update owner.

Language points:

- "appears to have reduced" is cautious and suitable for a short trial.
- "fell by..." gives a clear percentage change.
- "at the same time" introduces a limitation.
- "For this reason" connects the evidence to the recommendation.
- "limited expansion" avoids overclaiming from one trial.

### Q&A pairs

| Function | Question | Model answer |
|---|---|---|
| Clarify the question | Do you mean the checklist should be used for every application type? | Not yet. My recommendation is limited expansion to two more high-volume application types. After that, we should review the result again. |
| Answer directly | Did the checklist reduce returned applications? | Yes. In the trial, returned applications fell from 142 to 111. That is a reduction of about 22%. |
| Bridge to evidence | Why do you think the checklist caused the improvement? | The main improvement was in attachment-related returns, which the checklist directly targeted. That does not prove every change came from the checklist, but the link is strong enough to justify the next trial. |
| Acknowledge risk or limitation | Will this make the counter line longer? | It may add some time. Staff reported about 40 seconds per intake. That is why we should monitor waiting time during the expansion, not only returned applications. |
| Defer safely | Can you confirm the exact training time needed for rotating staff? | I cannot confirm the exact training time today. My estimate is a short reminder session, but I will check with the counter-service supervisor and include a training plan before the expansion starts. |
| Confirm follow-up or next action | Who should update the checklist when requirements change? | I recommend assigning one update owner in the document-review team, with counter-service staff sending change requests. If you approve the expansion, I will draft that ownership rule this week. |

## Comparison note for teachers

The two variants teach the same presentation skill, but the workplace pressures are different.

| Teaching point | Finance/trading operations variant | Government administrative variant |
|---|---|---|
| Main decision | Expand an exception resolution process to two more desks | Expand an intake checklist to two more application types |
| Evidence type | Late confirmations, rework time, user ownership feedback | Returned applications, repeat inquiries, attachment-related returns |
| Main risk | Control risk, duplicate log entries, volume effects | Waiting time, rotating staff, checklist update ownership |
| Sensitive information risk | Client names, account data, trade IDs, desk names, control findings | Applicant information, form details, staff records, public-facing guidance |
| Natural visual | Before/after bar chart plus "what changed" table | Before/after bar chart plus process note |
| Q&A pressure | Is the result large enough and controlled enough to expand? | Does the result justify expansion without slowing service? |

Neither model should be used for investment advice, market prediction, legal advice, policy argument, or political advocacy. Both models are about operational results and next steps.
