# Teacher Notes

Printable teacher notes for the 2026 learner-facing draft.

These notes are separated from the student textbook. Unit and appendix drafts should remain learner-facing.

## Course Planning and Unit Timing

Use these timings for course planning unless the client schedule requires a different pace.

| Unit | Expected teaching time | Notes |
|---|---:|---|
| Units 1-3 | 75-90 minutes each | Core planning units. Allow extra time if learners need support choosing a presentation topic. |
| Units 4-10 | 90-120 minutes each | Skill-building units with language practice, visuals/documents, delivery work, or Q&A practice. |
| Unit 11 | 90-120 minutes | Rehearsal and peer feedback. Larger classes may need more time. |
| Unit 12 | 45-60 minutes for 1-to-1; about 10-12 minutes per learner in groups, plus quiz/review time | Final presentation, Q&A, textbook wrap-up quiz, reflection, transition time, and brief feedback. |

Unit 12 timing depends on class size. In 1-to-1 lessons, the final presentation itself may be short, so use the textbook wrap-up quiz and reflection discussion to consolidate learning across the course. For group planning, six learners usually need about 60-75 minutes, eight learners need about 80-100 minutes, and ten learners need about 100-120 minutes before extended quiz discussion. Adjust if the teacher gives longer Q&A, detailed feedback, or individual scoring comments.

## General Teaching Notes

The student textbook, including appendices, should be completely learner-facing. Keep teacher guidance, answer keys, timing options, extension ideas, and review cautions in this document rather than in the learner manuscript.

The printed textbook should be role-agnostic, but classroom delivery does not have to be. Most classes are for one client or one type of client, so teachers should adapt examples, discussion questions, vocabulary support, and practice contexts to the learners' actual work roles, company, organization, and confidentiality limits.

When specialist review is used, run the Business Presentation Specialist first, integrate business/context changes, and then run the Language Editor last. The final language pass matters most because the goal is English development. It should check first-use definitions, glossary needs, B1-B2 load, Japanese-learner support, spoken naturalness, and specialized workplace terms.

Watch recurring business/process terms that may need explanation before learners use them: `handoff`, `exception`, `pre-read`, `follow-up handout`, `takeaway document`, `fallback option`, `asynchronous`, `sanitized`, `accessibility`, and `confidentiality`.

Terminology note: use `leave behind` without a hyphen only as the phrasal verb, as in "Please do not leave important data behind." Some business writers use `leave-behind` as a compound noun for material given after a meeting, but this is not transparent for B1-B2 learners. Prefer `follow-up handout`, `takeaway document`, or `supporting document` in learner-facing text unless the industry term is explicitly taught.

The appendix models are for learner comparison and practice. Do not ask learners to copy scripts word for word. Use the models to notice structure, phrase choices, visual choices, Q&A handling, and how the business-client and government-agency variants demonstrate comparable skills in different contexts.

## Unit 1: Audience, Purpose, and Workplace Context

Suggested answers for Practice 1: 1B, 2A, 3C.

For Practice 2, accept any answer that moves from a topic to a listener outcome. Check that learners do not write only "I will explain..." without a result for the audience.

For B1 learners, allow sentence frames and short notes. For B2 learners, ask for a clearer business action, such as approve, compare, prepare, adopt, revise, or follow up.

Keep the printed Unit 1 text focused on the transferable skill: identifying the audience, purpose, and audience outcome. In class, adapt examples to the actual client group. For example, a banking class can use internal approval, risk-control, or client-service examples; a trading-company class can use procurement, supplier, or shipment-reporting examples; a government-agency class can use administrative service, reporting, or coordination examples.

## Unit 2: Message, Objective, and Relevance

In Practice 1, sentences 1, 3, and 5 are topics. Sentences 2 and 4 are messages.

For the AI critical-literacy task, keep the discussion short and focused on responsibility. The sample is intentionally vague. Learners should not be encouraged to use AI as a drafting shortcut for assessed work.

For B1 learners, accept openings that use the frame accurately. For B2 learners, push for a more precise action request and less repeated wording.

## Unit 3: Structure and Flow

Suggested answers for Practice 1: 1B, 2C, 3D, 4A.

For Practice 2, accept several possible structures if the sequence is logical. The key requirement is that learners move from a loose list to a purposeful flow.

Practice 3 now uses a planning map grouped into Introduction, Body, and Conclusion. Reinforce that the conclusion should summarize key points and state the action, decision, or close. Backup details should be prepared for Q&A, appendices, or follow-up documents, not introduced as new content in the conclusion.

For B1 learners, allow a simple three- or four-part outline with sentence frames. For B2 learners, ask them to justify why the chosen structure fits the audience and purpose.

Business-client and government-agency models can be used for comparison, but the main task should remain transferable across workplace contexts.

## Unit 4: Business English for Signposting

Accept different register choices if learners can explain the audience and situation.

Correct false absolute rules carefully. `At first`, `then`, `let's`, and `that's all` are not always grammatically wrong, but they may be weak or unsuitable in some presentation contexts.

The learner draft now includes limitation/risk signposting phrases. Use these to show that professional signposting is not only sequence language; it also helps listeners understand caution, evidence limits, and why a limited next step may be better than a full rollout.

The spoken drill after Practice 1 should be used actively. Learners should mark thought groups and stress, then say the repaired phrases aloud. Do not let the phrase-repair task remain only a writing exercise.

For Japanese learners, watch for article and plural omissions in slide or outline text, and for sentence-by-sentence translation that creates long, indirect signposting.

Keep the printed unit from becoming one client-specific case. In class, however, adapt signposting examples to the learners' work context, such as banking/leasing operations, general trading-company coordination, manufacturing reporting, or government-agency administration.

## Unit 5: Clear Visual Communication

Accept slides, PDFs, document sections, dashboard mockups, or other visual formats if the learner can justify the choice.

Keep the printed main task role-agnostic. In class, teachers may narrow the task to the client context, such as a banking/leasing service update, a trading-company supplier process, a manufacturing workflow, or a government-agency program or service launch.

The learner draft now defines `accessibility` before the checklist. Treat accessibility as a practical presentation-quality issue, not as a separate technical topic.

Check slide text for missing articles, missing plurals, and over-compressed noun phrases.

Use the corrected title examples to connect visual design with English accuracy. A good takeaway title should be readable, grammatical, and message-led.

The optional AI task is critical literacy only. Do not let it become a slide-generation workflow for assessed work.

Spoken output is required in this unit. Learners should explain the visual aloud, not only submit the visual.

## Unit 6: Data, Charts, and Evidence

Use fictional data unless a verified source is already available and appropriate.

For banking/leasing or general trading-company examples, keep the focus on operations, reporting, workflow, service quality, procurement, supplier coordination, shipment reporting, or control escalation. Do not allow securities trading recommendations, securities-market predictions, real identifying client/account/order/shipment/transaction details, or legal or regulatory advice.

For government-agency examples, keep the focus on administrative service, process, reporting, coordination, or service delivery.

Spoken output is required in this unit. Learners should explain a chart in 60-90 seconds.

Check for overclaims such as "prove," "guarantee," and "solve." Encourage cautious claims when the evidence is limited.

## Unit 7: Tool-Neutral Slide and Document Workflow

Keep the focus on decision-making, not software features. Accept slides, PDFs, documents, dashboards, worksheets, or other formats if the learner can justify the choice.

For banking/leasing or general trading-company examples, require fictional or sanitized workflow, reporting, procurement, supplier-status, shipment, or order data. Redirect learners away from investment advice, securities-market predictions, real identifying client/account/order/shipment/transaction details, and regulatory interpretation.

For government-agency examples, keep the context administrative and avoid political or policy advocacy.

## Unit 8: Delivery: Voice, Presence, Movement, and Notes

For Japanese learners, word stress and thought groups may need more attention than individual sounds.

Avoid telling learners only to speak slowly. Instead, have them pause between thought groups and stress content words.

In online or hybrid practice, include camera, microphone, screen-share, and remote-audience checks.

Keep peer feedback focused on intelligibility and message control, not personality.

## Unit 9: Online, Hybrid, and Async Delivery

In this unit, learners should adapt one selected mode, not compare every possible platform in detail.

Keep tool references general and avoid step-by-step interface instructions.

For banking/leasing or general trading-company examples, use fictional or sanitized project-results data only. Sanitized shipment, order, procurement, supplier-status, and workflow examples are acceptable. Avoid securities-market predictions, securities trading recommendations, real identifying customer/account/order/shipment/transaction details, and legal or regulatory advice.

For government-agency examples, keep the context administrative and avoid flags, seals, emblems, or political-policy framing.

Async work can be a storyboard if recording is not practical.

## Unit 10: Q&A, Challenge Handling, and Interaction

Keep role plays professional. Do not reward aggressive questioning. The aim is controlled challenge handling, not debate performance.

For banking/leasing or general trading-company variants, check that learners do not give securities trading recommendations, securities-market predictions, real account details, or legal/regulatory interpretations.

For government-agency variants, keep questions administrative and service-focused.

Suggested quick feedback codes:

- D = answered directly
- C = clarified before answering
- E = used evidence
- L = limited an over-strong claim
- F = confirmed follow-up

## Unit 11: Final Rehearsal and Peer Feedback

Separate peer feedback from formal assessment. Learners should feel able to identify problems honestly during rehearsal.

Give each group a limited feedback target if time is short. For example, one group can focus on opening and structure, another on visuals and evidence, and another on delivery and Q&A.

Require specific evidence in peer comments so feedback does not become personal preference.

## Unit 12: Final Presentation and Reflection

Confirm time limits, Q&A requirements, allowed materials, and submission format before learners present.

The current course default is 5-7 minutes plus required Q&A, but client or class conditions may require a shorter or longer local format.

If learners present online or asynchronously, keep the same rubric categories but adjust the delivery evidence: camera, microphone, captions or transcript awareness, screen sharing, and response format may replace room movement or physical eye contact.

For final assessment, separate English control from presentation effectiveness. A learner may have a clear structure with language problems, or accurate English with weak audience fit. Use the rubric to make both visible.

Use the textbook wrap-up quiz to extend 1-to-1 Unit 12 lessons and to consolidate learning after final presentations. It can be done before the final presentation as a warm-up, after the final presentation as review, or partly as homework.

Textbook wrap-up quiz answer key:

Practice 3, Part A:

1. audience outcome
2. core message
3. problem - cause - solution - action
4. takeaway title
5. pre-read
6. safe deferral

Practice 3, Part B:

1. Suggested answer: "I will use a short slide pack because the audience needs to compare the two options quickly."
2. Suggested answer: "This chart shows a decrease from 38 late handoffs to 27, but it does not prove a full-year result."
3. Suggested answer: "I do not have the exact number here, so I will check and follow up."
4. Suggested answer: "For these reasons, I recommend starting with a four-week pilot."

Practice 3, Part C:

1. The main message is late and unclear. A stronger opening should state the purpose and action request early.
2. The structure is mostly a weak list. A better structure is `Problem - cause - solution - action`.
3. The speaker should remove or explain specialist terms such as `handoff`, `exception log`, and `ownership`, depending on the audience.

## Appendix: Process Improvement Briefing Models

Both variants teach the same presentation skill: identify a repeated process problem, recommend a limited pilot or trial, explain the expected benefit, and ask for a specific decision.

The business-client version focuses on operational handoff risk in banking/leasing or general trading-company operations. Sanitized shipment, order, procurement, supplier-status, and workflow examples are acceptable. Keep it away from investment advice, securities-market prediction, stock ticker symbols, real firm names, real identifying customer/account/order/shipment/transaction data, and legal or regulatory advice.

The government-agency version focuses on administrative intake quality and service consistency. Keep it away from politics, public-policy advocacy, legislation, budget campaigning, and official symbols such as flags, seals, emblems, and crests.

Both models use simple fictional evidence because a process-improvement briefing needs enough support to justify a pilot. Do not add heavy chart teaching here unless a unit specifically asks learners to compare this model with a data-focused project results briefing.

## Appendix: Product, Service, or Program Launch Models

If AI is mentioned with these models, use it only for checking and critique. For example, learners can review a short flawed AI-style launch opening and identify generic claims, missing limits, confidentiality risk, or unclear audience benefit.

Do not ask learners to generate their final script or final visuals with AI. The presentation, language, visuals, Q&A preparation, and final delivery must remain learner-owned.

## Appendix: Project Results Briefing Models

The two variants teach the same presentation skill, but the workplace pressures are different.

| Teaching point | Banking/leasing or general trading-company operations variant | Government administrative variant |
|---|---|---|
| Main decision | Expand an exception resolution process to two more desks. | Expand an intake checklist to two more application types. |
| Evidence type | Late document handoffs, rework time, ownership feedback. | Returned applications, repeat inquiries, attachment-related returns. |
| Main limitation | Volume effects and duplicate log entries. | Short trial period and added counter time. |
| Sensitive information risk | Client names, account or transaction data, shipment IDs, desk names, internal control findings. | Applicant information, ID fields, case numbers, staff records, public-facing guidance. |
| Natural visual | Before/after bar chart plus "what changed" table. | Process Note plus before/after bar chart. |
| Q&A pressure | Is the result large enough and controlled enough to expand? | Does the result justify expansion without slowing service? |

Neither model should be used for investment advice, securities-market prediction, legal advice, policy argument, or political advocacy. Both models are about operational results, evidence, limitations, and next steps.
