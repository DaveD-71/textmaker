# Unit 11: Final rehearsal and peer feedback

Rehearsal is where a presentation becomes usable. Reading through your slides or visual pack once is not enough because problems often appear only when you speak: the opening feels too long, a transition is unclear, a chart takes too much time, or your notes pull your eyes away from the audience.

This unit gives you a structured rehearsal process. You will practise the full presentation, receive focused peer feedback, and revise your message, visuals, language, delivery, and Q&A plan before the final presentation.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, you can:

- rehearse a full presentation with visuals, notes, timing, and Q&A preparation
- give and receive focused peer feedback using clear business English
- revise your presentation based on evidence from rehearsal

## Core concept: rehearsal is a repair process

A useful rehearsal has a purpose. Do not only ask, "Was it good?" Ask what you need to repair.

| Rehearsal focus | What to check | Repair action |
|---|---|---|
| Message | Is the main point clear after the opening? | Rewrite the opening or takeaway titles. |
| Structure | Can listeners follow the sequence? | Add or shorten transitions. |
| Evidence | Is the data explained before details become confusing? | State the takeaway first. |
| Visuals | Do visuals support the spoken message? | Reduce text, improve hierarchy, or move details to backup material. |
| Spoken English | Are key phrases natural and intelligible? | Practise thought groups, stress, and sentence frames. |
| Delivery | Do notes support speaking rather than reading? | Shorten notes and mark pauses. |
| Q&A | Are likely questions prepared? | Revise the response bank. |
| Timing | Is the presentation within the limit? | Cut detail, not the core message. |

Rehearsal should produce decisions. After each run-through, choose a small number of changes and make them before you rehearse again.

## Presentation English focus

Peer feedback needs tact and precision. Vague comments such as "It was nice" or "More energy" do not help the presenter revise.

### Giving feedback

- Your main message was clear when you said ...
- I understood the structure, but the transition to the data section was sudden.
- The chart title helped me understand the point before the details.
- I needed more context before the recommendation.
- The visual was readable, but the labels were small from the back of the room.
- Your answer to the second question was direct, but you could add one follow-up action.

### Asking for feedback

- Could you focus on my opening and first transition?
- Did the chart explanation sound clear?
- Where did you feel I gave too much detail?
- Which part should I cut if I need to save one minute?
- Did my Q&A answer sound direct enough?

### Responding to feedback

- That is useful. I will shorten the background section.
- I see the issue. I will move that detail to the appendix.
- I will keep the point, but I will change the wording.
- I am not sure I can cut that example, but I can make it shorter.
- I will rehearse that transition again.

### Timing language

- I am currently about 40 seconds over the limit.
- I can save time by shortening the explanation of the process.
- I should not cut the recommendation because it is the action point.
- I need a shorter answer for this likely Q&A question.

## Model: rehearsal notes after one run-through

Presentation purpose: request approval for a four-week pilot of a revised workflow.

Time limit: 6 minutes plus Q&A.

Rehearsal result:

| Area | Observation | Revision decision |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Clear context, but purpose came too late. | Move the action request into the first 30 seconds. |
| Structure | Problem and evidence were clear. Recommendation section was too short. | Add one sentence explaining what changes during the pilot. |
| Visuals | Workflow visual was useful but crowded. | Remove two labels and move details to backup material. |
| Spoken English | "We suggest to" sounded unnatural. | Use "We recommend starting with..." |
| Delivery | Presenter looked at notes during Q&A. | Practise first response sentences without reading. |
| Timing | 6 minutes 45 seconds. | Cut one background example. |

Notice that each comment leads to a specific action. This is the difference between useful feedback and general opinion.

## Practice task 2: peer feedback cycle

Work in groups of three or four.

1. Presenter gives the full presentation or an agreed rehearsal section.
2. Peers listen with the feedback checklist.
3. Presenter asks two focused feedback questions.
4. Peers give evidence-based feedback using target language.
5. Presenter writes three revision decisions.

## Practice task 4: final Q&A check

Exchange your response bank with a partner. Your partner should ask three questions:

- one clarification question
- one evidence or risk question
- one follow-up or next-action question

Answer without reading full sentences from your notes. You may look at keywords, but your answer should sound spoken, not read aloud.

## Optional model references

For a business-client rehearsal, you may compare your presentation with the finance/trading operations models for workflow improvement, service launch, or project results. Keep examples focused on operations, reporting, workflow, service quality, or control handoff.

For a government-agency rehearsal, you may compare your presentation with the administrative service or process models. Keep examples focused on administrative tasks, service delivery, reporting, coordination, or process improvement.

Use these models as references for structure and language, not as scripts to copy.

## Speaking task: timed rehearsal

Deliver the full presentation with visuals and notes. The Standard target is 5-7 minutes plus required Q&A, unless your teacher sets a different local requirement. After the presentation, answer at least two questions from classmates.

Your spoken English output for this unit is the timed rehearsal, the Q&A practice, and three revised spoken sentences marked for thought groups and stress.

## Unit wrap-up

The final presentation should not be a first performance. By this point, you should know where your message is strong, where listeners need more guidance, which visuals need repair, and which questions you are likely to receive. Use the rehearsal evidence to make targeted changes.

## Assessment alignment

This unit prepares evidence for all final rubric categories except the final live performance itself. Peer feedback remains formative, but the revision decisions affect message clarity, audience fit, structure, evidence, visual effectiveness, spoken English, delivery, Q&A, timing, professionalism, accessibility, and reflection.

## Teacher notes

Separate peer feedback from formal assessment. Learners should feel able to identify problems honestly during rehearsal.

Give each group a limited feedback target if time is short. For example, one group can focus on opening and structure, another on visuals and evidence, and another on delivery and Q&A. Require specific evidence in peer comments so feedback does not become personal preference.
