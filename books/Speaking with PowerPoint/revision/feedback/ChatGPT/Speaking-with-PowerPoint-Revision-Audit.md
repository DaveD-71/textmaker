# *Speaking with PowerPoint*: 2026 Revision Audit and Recommendations

## Overall assessment

The complete 39-page textbook was reviewed, including its page designs, model slides, exercises, and presentation language.

The book has a usable foundation, but it currently reads as a 2014–2016 speech-training manual with PowerPoint added to it. Bringing it into 2026 requires more than updating dates and screenshots. The central approach should shift from “delivering a formal speech” to “helping business audiences understand information and make decisions.”

Because the revision must be completed in two days, the issues are divided into three levels.

## Priority 1: Essential revisions

| Issue | Where it appears | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The central business model is badly outdated | Units 8–12 | Replace the fictional 2014 Ventura car launch completely. Use a contemporary business situation such as proposing a process improvement, recommending an investment, presenting project results, or requesting management approval. |
| 2. The course is framed as speech-making rather than business communication | Throughout; especially Units 2, 6 and 13 | Replace most uses of “speech” with “presentation.” Teach presentations as purposeful business interactions: informing, recommending, persuading, reporting, training, or obtaining a decision. |
| 3. Audience outcome is underdeveloped | Units 1, 2 and 5 | Make “What should the audience know, believe, decide, or do?” the central planning question. Distinguish the presenter’s topic from the audience’s required outcome. |
| 4. The three-part structure is too generic | Unit 2 | Retain introduction–body–conclusion as a basic framework, but add business-specific structures: problem–solution, situation–analysis–recommendation, past–present–future, options–criteria–recommendation, and claim–evidence–implication. |
| 5. The textbook assumes all presentations have three main points | Units 2, 6 and 8 | Present three points as one useful option, not a rule. Let the message and audience determine the number and organization of sections. |
| 6. The “Logic Tree” is memorable but conceptually weak | Unit 8 | Replace it with a presentation planning map: audience → purpose → key message → supporting points → evidence → action. Alternatively, retain the tree illustration but rename it a planning tool rather than a form of logic. |
| 7. The PowerPoint advice is based on old slide conventions | Unit 10 | Rewrite this unit around message-led slides, visual hierarchy, contrast, alignment, whitespace, consistency, and audience viewing conditions. |
| 8. The “Rule of 7” is treated as a design rule | pp. 26–27 | Remove the rigid maximum of seven lines and seven words. Replace it with: one main message per slide, remove unnecessary text, and test readability in the actual delivery environment. |
| 9. The font guidance is obsolete | pp. 25 and 27 | Remove the recommendation to use serif fonts for slide body text. Recommend a clear company-approved typeface, usually sans serif, with sufficient size, weight, spacing, and contrast. Do not prescribe Arial, Times New Roman, or Georgia. |
| 10. Slides are treated primarily as abbreviated text | Units 10 and 11 | Add guidance on selecting and designing charts, tables, diagrams, timelines, photographs, screenshots, and process visuals. The current textbook scarcely teaches “presentations with visuals.” |
| 11. There is no meaningful data-visualization instruction | Unit 11 | Teach learners to choose a chart for a communicative purpose, simplify it, label it clearly, highlight the relevant data, state the conclusion, and cite the source. |
| 12. The model slide deck demonstrates dated design | pp. 28–31 | Replace all model slides. Use 16:9 slides, takeaway titles, modern typography, clearer charts, restrained branding, appropriate imagery, and visible source notes. Include both effective and ineffective versions for comparison. |
| 13. Online and hybrid presentations are absent | Units 7 and 12 | Add camera position, microphone and lighting checks, screen sharing, Presenter View, chat and reactions, managing remote questions, speaking to the camera, and contingency planning. PowerPoint Live now allows audience-controlled navigation, captions, translation, and high-contrast viewing. |
| 14. Accessibility is absent | Unit 10 | Add contrast, color-blind-safe design, alt text, descriptive links, meaningful reading order, captions, accessible templates, and the Accessibility Checker. These are now ordinary professional requirements, not specialist additions. |
| 15. AI-assisted presentation creation is absent | Units 7 and 10 | Add a short, practical section on using tools such as Copilot for brainstorming, outlining, rewriting, and initial slide generation. Require users to verify facts, calculations, sources, images, and wording and to follow company confidentiality rules. |
| 16. The exercises do not constitute a teachable course | Throughout | Add short noticing, editing, planning, speaking, and peer-feedback tasks. At present, learners mainly read long explanations and are then told to write and deliver an entire presentation. |
| 17. There is no final performance standard | Throughout | Add a presentation rubric covering purpose, audience orientation, organization, evidence, slide design, language, delivery, interaction, timing, and Q&A. |
| 18. There are no stated learning outcomes | Beginning of book and units | Add measurable outcomes such as “By the end of this unit, you can identify the decision required from an audience” or “explain the main message of a chart.” |
| 19. The presentation language is excessively formal and scripted | Units 6 and 13 | Replace stiff expressions with contemporary alternatives and indicate register. For example, “Today, I’d like to…” is generally more natural than “Today, I am going to tell you about…” |
| 20. The book does not sufficiently teach Q&A | Unit 13 | Expand Q&A into listening, clarifying, answering directly, structuring longer answers, checking satisfaction, deferring responsibly, handling disagreement, and admitting when information must be checked. |

## Priority 2: Correct misleading or questionable guidance

| Current guidance | Problem | Recommended revision |
|---|---|---|
| “All speeches have the same three-part structure.” | Too absolute. | “Most presentations benefit from a clear opening, organized development, and purposeful close.” |
| “Don’t say ‘I’m in charge of…’ unless you are the boss.” | Incorrect. “In charge of” can mean responsible for a task, function, or project without being someone’s boss. | Teach the distinction among “I’m responsible for…,” “I manage…,” “I lead…,” and “I’m in charge of….” |
| “Prepare your speech in English. Don’t prepare it in Japanese and then translate it.” | Overgeneralized and dismissive. Strategic use of the first language can aid planning. | Recommend planning ideas in the most efficient language, then drafting and rehearsing natural English rather than translating sentence by sentence. |
| “Speak slowly. Say each word carefully.” | Can produce unnatural, disconnected speech. | Teach intelligible pace, thought groups, prominence, pausing, and reductions. |
| “Lengthen some vowels slightly.” | Linguistically unclear and potentially harmful without distinguishing long/short vowels and stressed syllables. | Remove or replace with targeted work on stressed vowels, consonant clarity, word stress, and chunking. |
| The marked thought groups include phrases such as “a / little” and “Claus / Meyer.” | Several divisions are unnatural. | Rewrite the pronunciation model using meaningful thought groups. |
| “Speak smoothly and without hesitation.” | Unrealistic for second-language speakers and inconsistent with natural speech. | Teach controlled pausing and recovery phrases. Distinguish purposeful pauses from disruptive hesitation. |
| “Eliminate the unnecessary pauses and Ummmms.” | Too negative and offers no technique. | Teach silent pausing, signposting, reformulating, and rehearsal strategies. |
| “Memorize parts of your speech.” | Can increase anxiety and cause failure when wording is forgotten. | Recommend knowing the opening, key messages, transitions, and close well, without requiring word-for-word memorization. |
| “Big gestures are not appropriate.” | Culturally prescriptive and context dependent. | Recommend natural, purposeful gestures appropriate to the room, audience, camera framing, and presenter. |
| “Your hands should be holding your notes, gesturing or pointing.” | Mechanistic and unnatural. | Encourage a neutral resting position and purposeful movement. |
| “Infrared pointers are dangerous to eyes.” | Technically confused: visible laser pointers are not simply “infrared pointers.” | Say never to point a laser at people and recommend PowerPoint’s digital pointer, cursor highlighting, or a safe presentation remote. |
| “Avoid animation and sweeps.” | Too absolute. | Use builds and transitions only when they reveal sequence, focus attention, or explain change. Avoid decorative movement. |
| “Index slide” | Dated terminology in many international contexts. | Prefer “agenda,” “overview,” or “roadmap,” and use it only when it helps orientation. |
| “One or two slides for each main idea.” | Arbitrary. | Use as many slides as the explanation requires, with one clear communicative purpose per slide. |
| “Do not use brackets `< >` in titles.” | Unexplained and not a meaningful general design principle. | Delete. |
| “Print or project the slides to test them.” | Projection remains relevant, but this ignores screens and online delivery. | Test on the actual platform and device: meeting-room display, laptop, shared screen, or mobile viewer. |
| “Carry a thumb drive; email it to yourself; take hardcopies.” | Dated and potentially insecure. | Prepare an approved cloud copy, an offline copy, a PDF fallback, embedded fonts/media, and a plan for presenting without slides. Follow company security policy. |
| Distribute handouts only when ready to use them | Sometimes useful, but not universally correct. | Decide based on the document’s function. Send detailed pre-reading before the meeting; provide worksheets during it; distribute leave-behind material afterwards. |
| Leave the conclusion slide on screen during Q&A | Often reasonable but too rigid. | Use a summary, recommendation, contact, or Q&A slide according to the purpose. Return to relevant evidence when answering. |
| “That’s a very good question.” | Overused and can sound evasive or patronizing. | Teach direct openings: “Yes—the main reason is…,” “There are two factors…,” or “I don’t have that figure with me, but I can confirm it after the meeting.” |
| “Never begin with Let’s…” | Far too absolute. | Explain that “Let’s…” can be effective for collaborative recommendations but may be too casual or insufficiently precise in some formal decisions. |
| “Then is not correct” for body transitions | This is not a reliable grammatical rule. | Explain that “then” is natural for sequences, while “next,” “turning to,” and message-specific transitions may better signal changes of topic. |
| “And so on” and “etc.” are meaningless | Overstated. | Say that vague endings should be avoided when the omitted items are important to the audience’s understanding or decision. |

## Priority 3: Rebuild the learning design

The textbook currently supplies information, but it does not provide enough guided learning.

Recommended sequence for each unit:

1. A realistic business situation.
2. A short model or before-and-after example.
3. A noticing task.
4. A concise explanation.
5. Controlled language or design practice.
6. Application to the learner’s own work.
7. Rehearsal or peer review.
8. Reflection using a checklist or rubric.

Useful task types:

- Rewrite a topic title as an audience-focused message.
- Reduce a text-heavy slide.
- Choose the best visual for a data set.
- Identify what is misleading in a graph.
- Organize evidence into a recommendation.
- Give a 60-second project update.
- Explain a chart without reading it.
- Respond to a skeptical question.
- Adapt the same presentation for executives, colleagues, and clients.
- Convert a detailed report into a five-minute presentation.
- Evaluate and correct an AI-generated slide.
- Present the same material in person and online.

## Recommended 2026 course structure

1. **Presentations as business decisions**  
   Purpose, audience, outcome, constraints, and presentation type.

2. **Build the message**  
   Key message, supporting points, evidence, and call to action.

3. **Choose an effective structure**  
   Informative, persuasive, proposal, project update, and data story structures.

4. **Design the story before the slides**  
   Outline, storyboard, section flow, and timing.

5. **Create message-led slides**  
   Titles, hierarchy, layout, text reduction, consistency, and branding.

6. **Use visuals and data responsibly**  
   Charts, tables, images, diagrams, sources, and data integrity.

7. **Use clear international business English**  
   Opening, signposting, explaining evidence, highlighting implications, and closing.

8. **Deliver naturally and intelligibly**  
   Thought groups, emphasis, pausing, pace, posture, eye contact, and notes.

9. **Present online and in hybrid meetings**  
   Camera, audio, screen sharing, captions, interaction, and platform controls.

10. **Handle questions and discussion**  
    Clarifying, answering, disagreeing, deferring, and follow-up.

11. **Use AI appropriately**  
    Drafting, slide generation, checking, confidentiality, attribution, and human responsibility.

12. **Prepare, rehearse, and evaluate**  
    Technical checks, Presenter View, timing, peer feedback, and final rubric.

Microsoft now supports Presenter View, live subtitles, smartphone controls, PowerPoint Live, translated slides, and individualized high-contrast viewing. The revised textbook should acknowledge these as normal presentation options rather than treating PowerPoint as a file projected onto a wall.

## Production and editorial problems

- The cover still says “May 2016.”
- The PDF metadata title is “Making Speeches,” not *Speaking with PowerPoint*.
- Page 2 is almost entirely blank.
- The model refers to the “2014 market.”
- “Venture” appears where “Ventura” is intended.
- “San-serif” should be “sans-serif.”
- “after awhile” should be “after a while.”
- “Deliver you message” should be “Deliver your message.”
- “at least16 points” needs a space.
- “high performance” should normally be hyphenated attributively.
- “PowerPoint that is simple and clear…” uses the product name as though it means “slides.”
- Unit cross-references are inconsistent: the text refers to the model as being in Units 8 and 10 even though it appears in Units 9 and 11.
- “Model Speech 2” is repeated in the contents and later sections without a corresponding modern sequence of models.
- Capitalization, bullets, indentation, spacing, punctuation, and heading formats are inconsistent.
- Several pages are text-dense, while others contain excessive unused space.
- The small decorative clip art and sample slides look visibly dated.
- The table of contents and internal structure do not show lesson length, outcomes, or assessment points.

## Material worth preserving

- The emphasis on a clear purpose and audience.
- The basic opening–development–close framework.
- The importance of signposting for ESL speakers.
- The connection between preparation and confident delivery.
- The advice not to read slides aloud.
- The focus on explaining visuals rather than merely displaying them.
- The use of models followed by learner application.
- The sections on clarifying and responding to questions.

These elements should be rewritten and integrated into realistic business tasks rather than discarded.

## Practical two-day revision strategy

Rather than polishing all 39 existing pages individually, the fastest defensible result would be a shorter, substantially rewritten book—perhaps 28–32 pages.

### Day 1

- Decide the target learners and course length.
- Establish the new 10–12-unit structure.
- Replace the Ventura model with one contemporary business case.
- Rewrite the planning, structure, slide-design, visual, online-delivery, and AI sections.
- Define the final task and assessment rubric.

### Day 2

- Rewrite the language and delivery sections.
- Build progressive activities around the new model.
- Replace the old model slides.
- Add accessibility and preparation checklists.
- Correct cross-references and language errors.
- Perform one final consistency and layout pass.
