# Speaking with PowerPoint — 2026 Revision Audit

Prepared for a 2-day revision sprint. Issues are grouped by priority: **A = structural/content, must fix**, **B = design/production, should fix**, **C = polish, fix if time allows**.

The core pedagogy — three-part speech structure, Flag Expressions, the 7 Elements of speaking clearly, the Logic Tree, delivery skills (voice/eye contact/posture/gestures) — is genuinely evergreen and needs little more than light editing. Don't spend your two days rebuilding what already works. Spend it on the sections below.

---

## A. Content & Currency Issues

### A1. The whole book assumes one delivery mode: standing, in-room, projector-and-screen
Unit 12 ("Delivery Skills for PowerPoint") only covers projected slides vs. printed handouts. In 2026, a huge share of business presentations are delivered over video call (Teams/Zoom/Google Meet), recorded async (Loom-style), or hybrid (some attendees in-room, some remote) — and Dave's own Avanti learners are very likely presenting to Japanese and international colleagues in exactly these formats.

**Recommendation:** Add a new sub-unit — "Speaking with PowerPoint on a Video Call" — covering: looking at the camera vs. the screen, sharing your screen without losing eye-contact cues, using the "Present" view so your camera stays visible, muting/unmuting discipline, handling the chat/Q&A box as well as verbal questions, and pacing for a flatter audio-only attention span. Keep the in-room unit — plenty of learners still present live — but retitle it "Speaking with PowerPoint In Person" and add the video-call unit alongside it.

### A2. The design-rules unit (Unit 10) is frozen at roughly Office 2007–2010
"Choose a template from PowerPoint Design (a drop-down menu)," "Century font," "avoid animation and sweeps," "infrared pointers" — these all read as dated to a modern learner and, more importantly, some of it is now simply wrong. Modern PowerPoint's Designer feature auto-generates layouts from bullet text, most professional templates are 16:9 not 4:3, and subtle motion (morph transitions, appear/build animations for one point at a time) is now considered a legitimate way to control pacing, not a gimmick to avoid.

**Recommendation:**
- Update to 16:9 widescreen as the default aspect ratio; mention 4:3 only as a legacy note.
- Replace the "choose a template" guidance with something reflecting how decks actually get built now: house/brand templates, PowerPoint Designer suggestions, or AI-assisted layout tools — with the same underlying principle (simple, uncluttered, on-brand) intact.
- Reframe animation: purposeful builds that reveal one point at a time are fine and often better than a static wall of bullets; gratuitous slide-transition effects (sweeps, fades-with-sound) are still the thing to avoid. The old rule conflated the two.
- Drop "infrared pointer" — nobody uses these; replace with laser pointer vs. cursor-highlight vs. just gesturing at a screen-shared cursor.

### A3. Font advice is dated and slightly wrong for on-screen reading
The book recommends Times New Roman/Georgia (serif) for body text and Arial for headings. That's print-era logic. On-screen — which is nearly all presentation viewing now — sans-serif is broadly recommended for *both* body and headings for legibility, and most modern templates default entirely to sans-serif (Calibri, Segoe UI, Aptos as of recent Office versions, or brand fonts).

**Recommendation:** Simplify to: use one or two clean sans-serif fonts throughout for projected/on-screen slides (serif is fine for printed handouts and formal written documents, which is a genuinely useful distinction to keep). Update the "Aptos is now Office's default font" if you want a concrete, checkable fact for students.

### A4. No accessibility guidance at all
Nothing on colour-blind-safe palettes, contrast ratios, alt text for screen readers, or captions — all now standard expectations in international business settings, and increasingly required by client/vendor accessibility policies.

**Recommendation:** Add a short accessibility checklist to Unit 10: sufficient text/background contrast, don't rely on colour alone to convey meaning (e.g., red/green charts), add alt text if the deck will be distributed as a file, use live captions on video calls when presenting internationally. Two paragraphs is enough — this doesn't need to be a full unit.

### A5. No mention of AI drafting/design tools
Zero acknowledgment that learners may draft slide text, generate images, or get design suggestions from AI tools (Copilot in PowerPoint, Gemini in Slides, Claude, ChatGPT, etc.). Given this is a 2026 revision and your own materials workflow uses AI tools, silence here will feel conspicuous to reviewers.

**Recommendation:** Add a short, practical note (not a whole unit) in the "Tips for Preparing Presentations" unit: AI tools can help draft text or suggest layouts, but the speaker is still responsible for accuracy, for the logic/structure, and for actually being able to speak to the content without reading it. This fits naturally next to the existing "don't write your speech in Japanese and translate it" advice — same underlying message: don't outsource the thinking.

### A6. Model Speech 2's content is dated in a way that undercuts the "environmentally responsible" persuasion example
The Ventura speech (Unit 9) uses hybrid engine + low emissions + 95% recyclable material as its environmental pitch, with sales data ending in 2013 and framed as "the 2014 market." In 2026, hybrid-as-innovation reads as behind the curve — a modern learner audience will find "hybrid engine" underwhelming as a persuasive environmental claim.

**Recommendation:** Two options:
1. **Light touch:** Update the framing — "efficient hybrid engine" → "full-electric powertrain," update years to a 2025/2026 model cycle, update sales figures. Keeps the same speech, same exercises, same structure notes — least rewrite work.
2. **Fuller touch:** Replace the product entirely with something more native to 2026 business contexts your ESP/finance learners might actually present about (a SaaS product launch, a fintech service, a logistics platform) — more work, but may land better with Business English/ESP audiences than a car if you're already leaning that direction in other materials.

Given your two-day timeline, Option 1 is almost certainly the right call — do a global find/replace on the dates and the environmental claim, and treat the deeper rewrite as a future edition task.

### A7. No treatment of data storytelling beyond a single bar chart
The one chart shown (Ventura's sales 2000–2013) is a plain bar chart with no other visualization guidance. Business presenters in 2026 routinely need to talk through dashboards, trend lines, comparison charts, and simple data narratives.

**Recommendation:** If time allows, add a short "Presenting Data" subsection to Unit 10 — plain-language principles for choosing chart types (trend → line, comparison → bar, share → avoid pie for more than 3–4 slices) and for narrating a chart out loud (the language for this is actually already implicitly modeled in the Ventura speech — "sales grew steadily... peaked... dropped sharply" — so you could cross-reference rather than rewrite).

## B. Design & Production Issues (the book itself)

### B1. The book's own visual design is dated
Grey rule under the title, generic clip-art silhouette of a man at a screen, clip-art keys/pen/tree/flag icons, and the Ventura slide mockups use an olive/tan PowerPoint template with a "Now for your future" tagline — all read as circa-2005–2010 corporate template design, which undercuts a book that's trying to teach *current* design standards.

**Recommendation:** This is the most visible "it's 2026 now" signal to anyone reviewing the book. Worth the investment even under time pressure: replace clip-art icons with clean line-icons or simple original graphics, and rebuild the Ventura mockup slides in a genuinely current 16:9 template so the "good design" examples actually look like good current design, not just described good design.

### B2. Presenter imagery is entirely one male silhouette, repeated three times
The cover, Unit 4, and Unit 12 all reuse the same male-silhouette presenter icon.

**Recommendation:** Vary or neutralize the imagery — simple abstract icons (a screen, a speech bubble, a slide-and-pointer glyph) sidestep the issue entirely and are easier to keep consistent across units than commissioning varied human figures.

### B3. Sample A / Sample B (Unit 7, page 17) is a scanned image of dense text, hard to read even as an example
The "easy to read vs. not easy to read" comparison is itself presented as a small, low-resolution scan.

**Recommendation:** Recreate both samples as clean text blocks in the new layout — ironically the current version demonstrates its own point about readability by being hard to read.

## C. Minor / Polish Issues

### C1. "Carry a backup copy on a thumb drive / flash memory stick" (Unit 7, page 16)
Not wrong, but incomplete for 2026 norms.

**Recommendation:** Add cloud backup (OneDrive/Google Drive/Dropbox) and note that presenting directly from a cloud-synced laptop is now the more common default; keep the USB-stick advice as a genuinely useful fallback for unreliable venue wifi, especially relevant for Japan-based learners presenting at client sites.

### C2. "Don't use sounds at all" (Unit 10, Design Points, item 9) is slightly too absolute
Sound is still generally to be avoided for design decoration (chimes on slide transitions), but audio/video clips embedded as content (a customer testimonial clip, a product demo) are now common and legitimate.

**Recommendation:** Narrow the rule: avoid decorative sound effects; embedded audio/video as actual content is fine if it serves the message, subject to the same "does it help or distract" test used elsewhere in the unit.

### C3. Katakana-English framing (Unit 3) is worth keeping as-is
This is specifically useful for your Japan-based learner base and isn't dated — flagging only so you don't accidentally cut it while modernizing other sections.

### C4. "Never say 'That's all'" / "Never say 'At first'" / "Never begin with 'Let's'" rules
These are still linguistically accurate and worth keeping — genuinely evergreen grammar/register guidance, not dated content.

### C5. Copyright/production page (back page)
Currently reads "Prepared by Kenneth Goldstone... September 2014, Revised & Expanded July & August 2015; May 2016." Will need a new revision line for the 2026 edition and a decision on whether to credit you as reviser/editor.

## Suggested Priority Order for Your Two Days

**Day 1 (content):** A1 (video-call delivery), A2 (design-rules update), A3 (fonts), A6 (Ventura dates/claims — light touch), A5 (AI note).
**Day 2 (production + polish):** B1 (visual redesign of icons/mockups), A4 (accessibility checklist), C1–C5 (quick edits), then a final pass to confirm the evergreen units (1–4, 6, 8, 13) haven't been disturbed.

A7 (data storytelling) and the fuller Model-Speech-2 rewrite (A6 option 2) are the two items I'd defer to a future edition if time runs short — both are additive rather than corrective, so leaving them out won't make the book feel dated, just slightly less rich.
