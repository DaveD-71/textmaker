Inflation is the rate at which prices rise over time. If a basket of goods costs 3% more than it did a year ago, inflation is 3%. A low, steady rate is normal. The problem comes when the rate jumps, because wages and savings usually do not keep up.
After 2021, prices rose sharply almost everywhere. In the United States, consumer prices in June 2022 were 9.1% higher than a year earlier, the largest 12-month increase in about 40 years. Petrol was up 59.9% over the year and food was up 10.4%. In the euro area, inflation reached 10.6% a few months later. The main causes were global: supply chains were still recovering from the pandemic, and energy and food prices rose sharply after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. In response, central banks in the United States, the euro area, the United Kingdom and many other countries raised interest rates quickly through 2022 and 2023.
The size of the shock still varied a lot by country. Japan had lived with near-zero inflation, and sometimes falling prices, for about 25 years. There, inflation rising above 2% was itself news. In 2024 the Bank of Japan raised its main interest rate above zero for the first time since 2007. Argentina was at the other extreme, with annual inflation of 211% in 2023 after years of economic crisis and a large fall in the value of its currency. Most countries were somewhere in between.
Local factors can add to the global picture. In Japan, a weaker yen made imported food and fuel more expensive. A poor rice harvest in extreme heat then pushed the price of a 5-kilogram bag of rice to about ¥4,260 in May 2025, roughly double a year earlier. A sudden jump in one staple food hits some households hard even when the overall inflation rate looks moderate.
Why aim for 2% rather than 0%? The European Central Bank, like the Bank of England and the US Federal Reserve, targets 2% over the medium term. A small positive rate gives a safety margin against deflation, which is falling prices. Deflation is dangerous because people delay spending when they expect things to get cheaper, and there is a limit to how far interest rates can be cut to fix it. Japan's long struggle to escape deflation is often given as the clearest example of that risk. When inflation instead runs well above target, a central bank usually raises interest rates. That makes mortgages, business loans and government debt more expensive, and slows the economy — the basic cost of bringing prices back under control.

In the United States in 2026, the average credit card charged about 21% a year in interest. The average new home loan charged about 6.7%. Both are loans. The difference in price comes mainly from security. A mortgage is backed by the house, which the lender can take and sell if payments stop, so the lender's risk is lower. A credit card is backed by nothing, so the rate is far higher. A car loan, backed by the car, sits in between.
Interest is the price of borrowing, charged as a percentage of the amount still owed. On a credit card, any unpaid interest is added to the balance and then charged interest itself the next month. This is compounding. It is why a balance that is never fully cleared can grow quickly even when no new spending is added to it.
Borrowing has been rising. US households owed US$18.8 trillion in total in mid-2026, including US$1.26 trillion on credit cards. Some countries carry much heavier household debt than others relative to the size of their economy. In South Korea it passed 2,000 trillion won in 2026, and the country now has one of the highest household-debt burdens in the world. Much of that is home loans. Whether a debt level is dangerous depends on incomes, on interest rates, and on how much of the debt is secured against property that could be sold.
A newer form of borrowing is "buy now, pay later", which splits a purchase into a few interest-free instalments at the checkout. It grew fast with little regulation. In the year to May 2024, about one in five UK adults had used it, and the UK market had grown from under £0.1 billion in 2017 to more than £13 billion. Regulators then moved in. From July 2026 these firms became regulated in the UK and must check that a customer can afford the repayments before offering the plan.
Japan has usually carried lighter household debt than the US or UK. Most Japanese cardholders also pay their balance in full the next month rather than borrowing on it. The defining reform came in 2006, after years of problems with people holding loans from many lenders at once. Before then, consumer-finance firms could charge between 20% and 29% a year, exploiting a gap between two laws. The reform closed the gap: the maximum interest rate on unsecured consumer loans was cut to 20%, and total borrowing from non-bank lenders was capped at one-third of a person's annual income. The number of people servicing loans from several lenders fell sharply in the years that followed.

People often use "saving" and "investing" to mean the same thing, but they are different. Saving is keeping money safe and available, usually in cash or a bank deposit, for short-term needs. Investing is putting money into assets such as company shares. Their value can fall as well as rise, but over long periods it is expected to grow more than cash would.
Over the very long run, shares have paid more. One widely used study of 35 markets found that global shares returned an average of about 5% a year after inflation from 1900 to 2024. Government bonds returned under 2%. That gap compounds, because returns earn further returns. Over several decades, a higher yearly rate produces a far larger final sum. The catch is that the average hides large swings. Any single 20-year period can be much better or much worse than 5%. The same study found the global real return over 2000 to 2024 was only about 3.5% a year. A long-run average is not a promise about the years ahead.
This is why the time horizon matters. Money needed next year should be saved, not invested, because a fall in value cannot be waited out in time. Money not needed for decades can be invested, because the ups and downs tend to even out over a long period. Diversification helps too: spreading money across many companies means no single failure is severe.
For most people, a low-cost "index fund" is the practical choice. An index fund simply tracks a whole market instead of trying to pick winners. Data from S&P Dow Jones Indices shows that over the 15 years to mid-2025, about 88% of professionally managed US funds investing in large companies did worse than the market index they were measured against. The fees those funds charge are a large part of the reason. A fund that beats the market in one period often falls behind in the next.
Governments encourage long-term investing through tax-advantaged accounts. Money held inside them grows tax-free or with tax delayed: the ISA in the United Kingdom, the 401(k) and IRA in the United States, compulsory superannuation in Australia. Japan reformed its version, the NISA, in January 2024. It sharply raised the tax-free limits and made the tax break permanent. Take-up was fast, and NISA accounts reached about 27 million by mid-2025. The push matters because Japanese households have long held about half their financial assets in cash and deposits, far more than households in the United States or the euro area. That money earned very little as inflation returned after decades of near-zero price rises.

Does a house always gain value? In much of the world the answer has been yes. But it is not true everywhere, and not in every decade.
House prices are set by demand meeting supply. Demand rises with population, with incomes, and with cheap credit. When interest rates are low, buyers can borrow more, so they bid prices up. Supply is slow to respond, because land is limited and new building is controlled by planning rules. When demand grows faster than supply, prices climb faster than incomes, and housing becomes less affordable.
That is what happened across much of the rich world after the 1990s. In New Zealand, house prices after inflation more than doubled between 2000 and 2007. Then interest rates rose sharply in 2022, and several of these markets fell. New Zealand prices dropped about 14% from their 2021 peak, and the largest cities fell by around 20%. Even after those falls, real house prices across advanced economies were still about 20% above their level at the end of the 2008–09 financial crisis.
Governments have tried different levers. Some cities have relaxed their planning rules to allow more building. After Auckland loosened its zoning in 2016, research found the number of new homes approved each year roughly doubled, and rents there rose more slowly than in the rest of New Zealand. Other governments have tried to cap rents directly. Berlin brought in a rent cap in 2020, but Germany's Federal Constitutional Court struck it down the next year, ruling that the city had no power to set it. A few, such as Singapore, have gone further and built most of the housing themselves: about 80% of resident households there live in flats built by a government agency.
Japan is the clearest exception to "houses always go up". There, the building and the land are valued separately, and the structure is treated as a wasting asset. The tax system assigns a wooden house a life of about 22 years. Older houses are often priced as if the building is worth almost nothing, with nearly all the value in the land. Two things reinforce this. One is heavy new construction: around 800,000 homes were started in the 2023 financial year, despite a shrinking population. The other is light planning rules that make it easy to knock a house down and rebuild. For a buyer, this means a home is closer to a car than to a store of wealth: it wears out, and most of the lasting value is in the land beneath it. The result is a rising number of empty homes. Japan's 2023 survey counted about 9 million, a record 13.8% of the total housing stock.

People in the United States reported losing US$12.5 billion to fraud in 2024, about a quarter more than the year before. The largest single category was fake investments. That figure counts only what was reported. Much fraud is never reported, out of embarrassment or because people do not know where to complain, so the real total is higher.
Fraud today is organised and industrial. Criminals use fake investment platforms, phishing messages that copy a real bank, impersonation calls, and "romance" approaches that build a relationship before asking for money. A growing type combines the two: a fake romance leads into a fake investment, sometimes in cryptocurrency, until the victim's savings are gone. A common method throughout is the authorised push payment scam. The victim is tricked into sending a bank transfer themselves, which makes it harder to reverse, because the payment was technically approved by the account holder. The United Nations has reported that much of this activity is run from large, guarded compounds in South-East Asia, often staffed by trafficked workers. It estimated that losses to victims across East and South-East Asia ran into tens of billions of US dollars in 2023 alone.
Countries protect victims very differently. From October 2024, the United Kingdom has required banks to reimburse most victims of authorised push payment fraud, up to £85,000 per claim, with the cost shared between the sending bank and the receiving bank. In most other countries, including the United States and Japan, whether a victim gets any money back is far less certain. Some countermeasures do seem to help. In Australia, reported scam losses fell by about 26% in 2024 after coordinated action by government, banks and telecoms firms.
Japan has fought "special fraud" for two decades. These scams often begin with a phone call from someone posing as a relative in trouble, or as a police officer. Losses hit a record ¥72 billion in 2024, up nearly 60% in a year, and about 45% of victims were aged 75 or over. A separate and newer category, investment and romance scams that start on social media, added a further ¥127 billion. The response has focused on adding friction. Many banks suspend cash-machine transfers for older customers who have not used the function recently. Some cap daily withdrawals. Convenience-store staff are trained to question elderly customers who try to buy large numbers of prepaid cards.
Self-defence comes down to a few habits. Treat any unexpected contact about money as suspicious. Never move money because someone has pressured you to act quickly. And check any request through a phone number or app you found yourself, not one the other person gave you.

In 2025 two American technology companies took opposite paths. Figma sold shares to the public and listed on the New York Stock Exchange, raising about US$1.2 billion. SpaceX did the reverse: it stayed private and raised money by selling shares to selected investors at a valuation of around US$400 billion, larger than most listed companies, without ever offering its stock on an exchange. Both routes lead to the same place, which is cash to run and grow the business, but they involve very different trade-offs.
A company can raise money in two basic ways. It can sell equity, meaning shares of ownership, which never has to be repaid but gives new owners a claim on future profits and a say in decisions. Or it can take on debt, meaning bank loans or bonds, which leaves ownership unchanged but must be repaid with interest whether business is good or bad. Equity itself comes in two forms. Private equity is raised from founders, venture capital funds and buyout firms, and the company stays closed. Public equity is raised through an initial public offering, or IPO, when shares are first sold to the public and then trade freely on a stock exchange.
For decades, going public was the natural goal for a successful company. That has changed in the United States and parts of Europe. The number of American listed companies peaked at more than 8,000 in 1996 and is now roughly half that. At the same time, a large pool of private capital, estimated at around US$22 trillion worldwide by 2024, now lets big companies raise billions and delay listing for years. Public markets still work: in 2024 there were about 1,145 IPOs worldwide, and India was among the busiest markets, with a record 268 new listings on its main exchange raising some US$19.5 billion.
In Japan and much of continental Europe and Asia, bank lending has long mattered more than share issues. Japanese firms traditionally relied on a "main bank" that lent to them, held their shares and watched over management. That arrangement is now slowly unwinding, as companies and banks sell off the cross-held shares that once locked ownership in place. The Tokyo Stock Exchange reorganised itself in 2022 into three segments, Prime, Standard and Growth. In 2023 it began pressing listed companies to set out plans to raise their share price, aiming especially at the many firms trading below the value of their own assets. The mix of equity and debt, and of private and public money, differs from one country to the next. But every company faces the same first question: whose money to take, and what to give up for it.

Of every ten new businesses in the United States, about eight survive their first year, about five reach year five, and about three reach year ten. Among companies backed by venture capital, the odds of a big payout are longer still: one widely cited study found that roughly three in four never return their investors' cash. Failure is not a rare accident in this part of finance. It is the normal outcome, and the whole model is built around that fact.
A startup usually raises money in stages, each tied to reaching a goal. The earliest money, often called seed funding, comes from founders, friends and specialist early investors. It buys the company time to build a product and find its first customers. If that works, a venture capital fund may lead a larger round, labelled Series A, then Series B, and so on. Each round buys a slice of ownership, usually at a higher price than the last, and the money is meant to last until the next milestone. Venture capital is money raised from pension funds, universities and wealthy individuals and placed in young, high-risk companies in exchange for equity. The investors know most of their bets will fail, so they need the rare winner to return many times its cost. The end goal, or exit, is a sale to a larger company or a stock market listing, which is how the fund turns its shares back into cash.
The size of the funding pool rises and falls sharply. Global venture investment reached roughly US$440 billion in 2025, with nearly half going to artificial intelligence companies, after a steep fall in 2022 and 2023. When funding tightens, companies that raised money at a high valuation must often accept a lower one in the next round, a "down round" that dilutes founders and early staff. The most striking recent failure was WeWork, an office-space company valued at US$47 billion in early 2019, which then failed to list, lost billions and filed for bankruptcy in 2023.
Japan's venture scene is smaller but growing. One estimate put Japan at seven "unicorns", meaning private companies worth over US$1 billion, in 2023, against more than 600 in the United States. In 2022 the government adopted a five-year plan to lift the number of startups and unicorns sharply, with public money and tax incentives behind it. Europe and India have both built up sizeable venture markets of their own over the same period. Whether in Tokyo, Bangalore or California, the arithmetic is the same: investors accept many total losses in exchange for a small chance of backing a company that becomes very large. That is why a single success, such as an early stake in a company that later lists, can carry a whole fund.

A delivery rider logs into an app on Friday evening, accepts trips for four hours, and is paid for each one. There is no set shift, no manager, and no promise of work next week. The rider values the freedom to choose hours, but carries risks an employee does not: earnings that swing from week to week, and usually no paid holiday, no sick pay, no employer pension contribution and no unemployment cover. This is platform work, meaning paid tasks arranged through an app or website, and it has grown fast. The International Labour Organization estimates that the number of digital labour platforms worldwide grew about five-fold in the decade to 2020.
The core dispute is a legal one: are these workers really self-employed, as the platforms say, or are they employees entitled to labour protections? The answer decides who bears the cost of holidays, illness and old age. Courts and parliaments have reached different conclusions. In February 2021 the United Kingdom Supreme Court ruled that Uber drivers are "workers", a middle category that brings the minimum wage and paid leave. It also held that their working time counts from the moment the app is switched on. The European Union adopted a directive in 2024 that presumes a platform worker is an employee unless the company can show otherwise, with member states to write it into national law by December 2026. Spain passed a law in 2021 making delivery couriers employees.
The United States has gone the other way, at least in its largest state. California passed a law in 2019 that would have made many gig drivers employees. Ride-hailing and delivery companies then funded a ballot measure, Proposition 22, that carved themselves out, spending more than US$200 million on the campaign. California's Supreme Court upheld the measure in 2024, so drivers there remain independent contractors with a limited set of benefits and no sick pay or unemployment cover. Most other United States states never adopted a test like California's in the first place.
Japan has taken two narrower routes rather than one big reclassification. A Freelance Act, in force from November 2024, does not make freelancers employees. Instead it puts duties on the companies that hire them: state the work and the fee in writing, pay within 60 days, guard against harassment, and give 30 days' notice before ending a continuing contract. Separately, in 2022 a Tokyo labour board found that Uber Eats couriers are "workers" under union law and can bargain collectively, a decision the company has challenged. Across all these systems the same tension runs. The flexibility that makes platform work attractive is bound up with the absence of the protections that make an income secure.

In 2024 the chief executives of the 350 largest United States companies were paid about 281 times as much as a typical worker in their industry, up from 21 times in 1965. The gap is now the subject of laws, disclosures and shareholder votes in most rich countries, and it looks very different from one country to the next.
Top executive pay usually has three parts. There is a base salary, fixed and relatively modest as a share of the total. There is an annual bonus tied to that year's results. And there is stock-based pay, meaning shares or options that vest over several years and now make up the large majority of a big American chief executive's package. Because so much of the pay is in stock, its final value depends on the share price, which is why supporters call it pay for performance and critics call it a windfall in a rising market.
Two kinds of rule have grown up around the question. The first is disclosure. Since 2018, United States listed companies must publish the ratio of chief executive pay to the pay of their median employee, meaning the employee exactly in the middle of the pay range. The United Kingdom has required a similar figure since 2020. The second kind of rule is the shareholder vote, known as "say on pay". In the United Kingdom and much of Europe the vote on pay policy is binding, so a company may not pay outside an approved policy. In the United States the vote is only advisory, meaning the board can proceed even if shareholders vote no. Switzerland went furthest, banning sign-on and severance bonuses for listed-company executives by referendum in 2013. Votes against pay do happen: Netflix shareholders rejected the company's executive pay package in an advisory vote in 2023.
Japan sits at the other end of the range. The median ratio of executive to employee pay across listed Japanese companies is roughly 10 times, far below the American figure. Since 2010, any director paid 100 million yen or more must be named individually in the company's annual report. Fewer than 300 executives crossed that line in 2009; a record 1,345 did so at 606 companies in the year to March 2025, and the highest-paid were mostly foreign executives at Japanese firms. The rise reflects the spread of stock-based pay rather than a jump in salaries. The underlying argument is the same everywhere: is very high pay the price of attracting and motivating rare talent, or is it simply what executives can command when boards decide their rewards?

On Friday 10 March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank in California was closed by regulators. The day before, its customers had tried to withdraw more than US$40 billion in a single day, faster than any bank run in modern records. It was, at the time, the second-largest bank failure in United States history. What went wrong was not fraud or reckless lending. It was the ordinary business of banking, done at the wrong moment.
A bank takes in deposits, which customers can withdraw at any time, and lends most of that money out for years, as mortgages and business loans. Turning short-term deposits into long-term loans is called maturity transformation, and it is useful: it channels idle savings into productive use. But it leaves every bank unable to repay all its depositors at once. If enough customers ask for their money together, even a sound bank can be forced to sell assets at a loss and collapse. That is a bank run, and the fear of one can cause one.
Governments try to break that logic in two ways. The first is deposit insurance: a government-backed promise that small savers will be repaid up to a limit even if their bank fails. In the United States the limit is US$250,000 per depositor per bank. In Japan the Deposit Insurance Corporation covers ordinary deposits up to 10 million yen, plus interest, and covers everyday settlement accounts in full. The point is to remove the reason for an ordinary saver to join a run. The second tool is the central bank acting as lender of last resort, lending cash to a sound bank against good collateral so it does not have to sell assets in a panic. When the British lender Northern Rock faced a run in 2007, queues formed outside its branches until the government guaranteed its deposits.
Sometimes a bank is judged too important to close. In March 2023 the Swiss authorities arranged for UBS to take over its troubled rival Credit Suisse over a single weekend. Central bank funding and a government guarantee stood behind the deal. Depositors lost nothing, but shareholders and holders of certain risky bonds lost heavily. Japan faced a broader failure in 1997 and 1998, when Hokkaido Takushoku Bank and the Yamaichi securities house collapsed. The government eventually took two large banks into public ownership and injected public money to steady the rest, a slow and costly clean-up that took years. The lesson from all these cases is that a bank's stability rests less on its own vault than on the belief that the safety net behind it will hold.

Why did the market fall today? Financial news answers this every evening, usually by naming one cause: an interest-rate decision, a disappointing profit report, a change in mood. All three are real drivers, and a fourth has grown more important — how much of an index depends on just a few very large companies. A stock index is a single number tracking a basket of shares, weighted by market capitalisation, which is the total value of each company's shares. What moves the index is whatever moves the shares inside it.
The most durable driver is company earnings, the actual profits firms make. A share is ultimately a claim on a company's future profits, so a higher expected profit stream is worth more. In 2025 the S&P 500, the main US index, returned about 18%, and more than three-quarters of that came from higher earnings rather than investors paying more per dollar of profit. When a large company's quarterly report misses expectations, its shares, and the index, can drop the same day. Earnings and interest rates together are what analysts call fundamentals.
Interest rates are the second driver. Higher rates make borrowing costlier for companies and make safe bonds a more attractive alternative to shares, so rate decisions move markets directly. On 18 December 2025 the European Central Bank held its main rate at 2%, its fourth pause in a row; Europe's Stoxx 600 rose about 1% that session and ended 2025 up roughly 17%. A US rate cut reaches further still, often sending money toward emerging markets in search of higher returns as the dollar softens.
The third driver is investor sentiment — collective confidence or fear. It is hard to measure, but the closest gauge is the VIX, an index of how much volatility options traders expect in the US market over the next month. Often called the market's "fear gauge," it stays low when markets are calm and jumps when investors rush to buy protection during a sell-off. A calm market can turn volatile within hours if confidence breaks, with no change in earnings or rates.
The fourth driver is index concentration. By the end of 2025 the ten largest companies in the S&P 500 made up a record 40.7% of its value, roughly double their share a decade earlier, most of them tied to artificial intelligence. When an index leans this heavily on a few firms, its daily direction can be set by one company's news. Japan shows that the usual rules can still surprise. On 16 June 2026 the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1%, the highest since 1995. Higher rates are supposed to weigh on shares, but the Nikkei 225 rose instead, passing 70,000 in intraday trading for the first time, because investors read the move as removing uncertainty rather than adding cost. On any given day, the same news can push a market either way, depending on what investors already expected.

In the first week of February 1637, buyers at a tulip-bulb auction in Haarlem simply stopped bidding. Sellers lowered their prices, then lowered them again, and still no one bought. Within days the market for Dutch tulip bulbs — a single bulb had earlier been traded for a house and farmland — had collapsed. Tulip mania is the oldest well-known asset bubble, and almost every element of it has recurred since.
A bubble forms when the price of an asset rises far above any sensible valuation. Three things carry it up: cheap or borrowed money, called leverage; herd behaviour, meaning people buy because others are buying; and a belief that "this time is different," so the usual warnings no longer apply. This becomes a speculative mania, in which buying is driven by expected further price rises rather than by value. Economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff traced that "this time is different" belief through financial crises across 66 countries and eight centuries and found it before nearly every one. The crash comes when buyers stop believing the price will keep rising, as at Haarlem.
The 1929 Wall Street crash is the classic modern case. The Dow Jones Industrial Average peaked near 381 in September 1929, then fell for almost three years to a low of 41.22 in July 1932, a peak-to-trough decline of about 89%. It did not regain its 1929 level until November 1954, a quarter of a century later. The US dot-com crash of 2000 and the 2008 global financial crisis followed the same shape: a boom built on new technology or easy credit, then a long repair. In each case the warning signs were visible beforehand and widely dismissed.
Japan's late-1980s bubble is one of the best-documented anywhere. The Nikkei 225 stock index closed at 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989, lifted by cheap credit and soaring land values used as loan collateral. It took 34 years to reclaim that level, in February 2024. Property fell even harder: an index of commercial land prices in six major cities dropped by roughly 87% from its peak and kept falling for 15 years, not turning up again until 2005. Banks were left holding loans backed by land worth a fraction of its former value, and the economy grew slowly for two decades.
The pattern reached new assets without changing. The cryptocurrency Luna had traded near US$120 in early April 2022; over three days in May, after confidence in its linked "stablecoin" broke, it fell from about US$80 to almost nothing. The details differ each time — bulbs, shares, land, tokens — but the sequence does not: easy money and rising prices draw in more buyers, the belief that the rise cannot stop becomes general, and then it stops. What varies most is the aftermath, and the risk of a slow one grows with the length of the boom and the amount of borrowed money behind it — a few weeks for Luna, more than three decades for the Nikkei.

In March 2022, a bakery in Tokyo that had paid a steady price for flour for years received a new quote: about a third higher, with no promise it would hold. Its wheat came from abroad, and two weeks earlier Russia had invaded Ukraine — together the two countries supplied roughly 30% of the world's wheat exports, so the price jumped on every market at once. The bakery could not negotiate with the war; it could only pay the new world price or use less.
This is the defining feature of commodities — raw materials such as oil, wheat and metals, traded in bulk and largely interchangeable between suppliers. Their prices are set globally, so a shock anywhere reaches buyers everywhere and feeds quickly into inflation. Oil showed it in the same weeks: Brent crude, the main benchmark price for oil, rose above US$120 a barrel, its highest since 2008. At the end of March 2022 the US released 180 million barrels from its strategic reserve, the largest drawdown in its history, to ease the shock.
The food side ran in parallel but was fixed differently. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization's Food Price Index hit an all-time high in March 2022, up almost 13% in a single month. Rather than wait for prices to draw out new supply, the UN and Türkiye brokered a protected shipping corridor in July 2022. It moved about 33 million tonnes of grain out of Ukraine's Black Sea ports over the following year, and the index fell back — a supply shock, unlike a slow price trend, can sometimes be reversed by a specific arrangement.
Metals show a different kind of price risk. In March 2022 the London Metal Exchange suspended nickel trading and later cancelled about US$12 billion of trades after the price doubled within hours, driven by supply fears and a large losing bet by one producer. Trades that dealers thought were final were undone — a reminder that even a commodity exchange can stop working under stress. For some metals the risk is a government's policy: China accounts for roughly 60% of the world's rare-earth mining and about 90% of its processing, and from 2023 it placed export licensing controls on a widening list of critical minerals.
Japan feels all of this more sharply than most because it produces almost none of it. It imports nearly all its oil, gas and coal, and its food self-sufficiency rate on a calorie basis was 38% in fiscal 2023, below the government's 45% target. Japan is close to a pure price-taker, too small a share of any market to move the price, so it simply pays the going rate — and in 2022 the weakening yen cut into that purchasing power still further. Its usual defences are the ones every importer uses: long-term contracts, part-ownership of overseas fields, and large stockpiles, including well over 200 days of oil reserves. Rice is the revealing exception: Japan grows almost all its own rice, yet a domestic shortage still pushed retail prices up sharply in 2024. Producing something yourself lowers your exposure to world prices; it does not remove price risk.

Many people assume a weak currency is always a problem. The story of the Japanese yen between 2022 and 2024 shows why that is too simple. An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another, and like any price it is set by supply and demand. When more people want to hold a currency, it strengthens; when they sell it, it weakens. What changes that demand most is the gap between interest rates in different countries. Money tends to move toward wherever it earns more, so when one central bank raises rates and another does not, the currency of the higher-rate country usually gains.
That is exactly what happened to the yen. Through 2022 the US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate sharply to fight inflation, while the Bank of Japan held its rate near zero. Investors moved money out of yen and into higher-yielding dollars. The yen fell from about 115 per US dollar at the start of 2022 to around 150 by late 2022, and touched 160 in 2024 — its weakest in over three decades. Japan's Ministry of Finance intervened in the currency market, selling dollars to buy yen, spending about JPY 9.8 trillion in 2022 to slow the fall. Intervention can smooth a move but rarely reverses a gap in interest rates.
The effects split the economy in two. A weak yen made Japanese exports cheaper abroad, and large exporters such as carmakers reported record profits, partly because foreign earnings converted into more yen. But the same weak yen made imported oil, gas and food more expensive, pushing consumer prices up and cutting the real spending power of households and small firms that buy from abroad. This is the core trade-off of a floating exchange rate — one that markets set freely: it cannot be strong for exporters and strong for importers at the same time.
Not every country lets its currency float. Some run a fixed exchange rate, promising to keep their currency at a set value against another, usually the dollar. Hong Kong has done this since 1983, and several Gulf states peg to the dollar too. A peg brings stability for trade, but it means importing the anchor country's monetary policy: when the Fed raised rates in 2022, Hong Kong's rates had to follow, whether or not its economy needed them. Other economies manage their rate loosely, intervening only at the edges.
The wider lesson is that an exchange rate is a relative price, not a scoreboard. When the yen was at 160, headlines called it a national weakness, yet Japan's export sector was thriving and tourism hit record numbers because visitors found the country cheap. By mid-2025, as the Bank of Japan raised rates and the Fed began cutting, the gap narrowed and the yen recovered toward 140. A currency's level is rarely simply good or bad. It shifts value between groups — exporters and importers, producers and consumers, savers and borrowers — and which way you read it depends on which side you are on.

In 2024 two large developing economies borrowed on very different terms. India sold ten-year government bonds to international investors at yields close to 7%, and demand was strong enough that the country was added to a major global bond index, drawing in billions of dollars of new foreign money. Egypt, in the same year, could only secure funding after agreeing an expanded IMF programme and receiving a large investment deal from the United Arab Emirates; its dollar bonds had been trading at distressed levels. Both are called emerging markets, but the label covers a wide range of risk.
An emerging market is an economy that is growing and opening to foreign investors but is not yet as stable, wealthy or well-regulated as an advanced economy. For investors, these markets offer higher potential returns and faster growth, in exchange for greater currency risk, political risk and the danger of sudden capital outflows — money leaving quickly when sentiment turns. Index providers such as MSCI and FTSE Russell decide which countries qualify, and inclusion matters: when a country joins an index, funds that track it must buy its assets, lowering the government's borrowing cost.
Developing countries fund growth from three main sources, each with a catch. The first is foreign direct investment — a foreign company building a factory or buying a lasting stake in a local business. It is the most stable form of foreign money because it is hard to pull out quickly, but it flows mainly to a few larger economies. The second is borrowing on international markets in dollars or euros, which is fast to arrange but creates a currency mismatch: if the local currency falls, the debt becomes far heavier. The third is lending from development finance institutions — bodies such as the World Bank, the regional development banks and national aid agencies — which offer lower rates and longer terms but attach conditions and move slowly.
Japan is one of the largest bilateral providers of this last kind of finance. Through the Japan International Cooperation Agency and its leading role at the Asian Development Bank, it funds transport, energy and water projects across South and Southeast Asia, often as long-term loans in yen at low interest. Supporters say this builds lasting capacity; critics note it can tie borrowers to Japanese contractors and add to debt loads.
The recurring danger is the sudden stop. Emerging markets depend on foreign capital that can reverse fast when global interest rates rise or risk appetite falls, as it did across 2022 when the US Federal Reserve tightened policy and investors pulled back from riskier assets worldwide. Countries that had borrowed heavily in dollars, such as Sri Lanka, defaulted; countries with deeper local savings, larger reserves and credible central banks, such as India and Indonesia, absorbed the shock. The clearest lesson of development finance is that how a country borrows — the currency, the maturity, the lender — often matters more than how much.

Where does government money actually come from? Almost every road, school, court and pension is paid for by tax, and the mix of taxes a country chooses decides who carries the cost.
The broadest measure is the tax-to-GDP ratio — total tax revenue as a share of the size of the economy. Across the OECD it averaged about 33.9% in 2023, ranging from about 17.7% in Mexico to about 43.8% in France. In a typical OECD country, social-security contributions and personal income tax each raise roughly a quarter of revenue. Goods-and-services taxes, including VAT, add about a fifth, corporate income tax about a tenth, and property taxes a few percent.
Countries at similar income levels choose very different structures. The United States has no federal VAT and leans heavily on personal income tax and payroll taxes; sales taxes are set by states at relatively low rates. Most European Union countries run high standard VAT rates that raise a large and stable share of revenue, usually the biggest single source after social contributions. Neither design is more "normal" than the other.
A country's tax structure follows its revenue base, and changes when that base looks less secure. Gulf states long funded themselves from oil and charged residents almost nothing. The United Arab Emirates introduced a 5% VAT in 2018 and a 9% federal corporate tax from June 2023 on profits above AED 375,000, while still charging no personal income tax.
Tax is also now an area of international coordination. Around 140 countries agreed in 2021 to a 15% minimum effective tax rate on the profits of large multinational groups. If a company shifts profit to a very-low-tax country, the shortfall is now "topped up" elsewhere. The rules took effect from 2024 in the EU, the UK, Japan and South Korea, among others, though the United States moved to withdraw its support in 2025. Underneath all of this sits the question of tax incidence: the person or company that hands the money to the government is often not the one who bears the cost. VAT is collected from businesses but paid by final consumers, and corporate tax is legally paid by companies but its burden is shared, in disputed proportions, among shareholders, employees and customers.
Japan's tax story is dominated by one instrument and one problem. The instrument is the consumption tax, introduced at 3% in 1989 and raised in steps to 5% (1997), 8% (2014) and 10% (2019, with food kept at 8%). Every increase cost the governing party politically, yet the consumption tax is now Japan's largest single national tax, because it is the most reliable way to raise revenue from a population with a growing share of retirees. The problem is that an ageing society needs ever more spending on pensions and health care while the number of working-age taxpayers falls, so Japan also leans hard on social-security contributions levied on wages rather than on higher income-tax rates. Even so, tax does not cover what the government spends; the gap is filled by selling bonds, which is the subject of the next topic.

On 23 September 2022 the UK government announced about GBP 45 billion of tax cuts with no stated plan to pay for them. UK government bonds, known as gilts, fell at their fastest rate in decades, the pound hit a record low near US$1.035, and forced selling by pension funds threatened a wider crash. On 28 September the Bank of England began emergency gilt purchases, authorised up to GBP 65 billion. The finance minister was sacked, the prime minister resigned after 49 days in office, and almost every measure was reversed.
The episode turns on three linked ideas. A deficit is the gap in one year between what a government spends and what it raises in tax. Debt is the accumulated total of past deficits. The debt-to-GDP ratio compares that stock to a year's national output, because a larger economy can carry more debt. A government can lower the ratio by running surpluses or by growing — or inflating — GDP faster than the debt.
Governments run deficits routinely and can carry high debt for decades, but the bond market sets a shifting, sometimes sudden limit, demanding a higher yield when it doubts a government's plans. That is the market discipline other checks lack. Even the United States has now been downgraded by all three major agencies — S&P in 2011, Fitch in 2023, Moody's in May 2025 — each citing large and growing deficits, with federal debt near US$40 trillion. Its bonds remain the world's benchmark safe asset, so it still borrows easily, but the cost of servicing that debt reached roughly US$880 billion in fiscal 2024, more than the defence budget.
The euro area tried to prevent crises with rules — a 3%-of-GDP deficit limit and 60%-of-GDP debt limit, set in 1997, suspended during COVID and replaced in 2024 with country-specific spending paths. Rules, though, meet politics. In 2024 Kenya's government proposed about US$2.6 billion of new taxes, partly to meet IMF targets, when interest payments alone already consumed about 37% of revenue. Mass protests forced the president to withdraw the bill. Deficit reduction is a political problem before it is an arithmetic one.
Japan is the puzzle that makes the topic interesting. Its government debt is well over 200% of GDP — comfortably the highest in the rich world — yet its bonds still pay very low interest and there has been no run on them. The debt is held almost entirely by Japanese institutions and households, the Bank of Japan owns around half of it, and a quarter-century of deflation kept the whole interest-rate structure near zero. The political consequence is that Japan has never faced the market pressure that forces other governments to act. Raising the consumption tax is so costly at the ballot box that it has happened only three times in 35 years, while spending on an ageing population keeps rising. Japan shows that a country can carry extraordinary debt for a long time when the conditions are right — and that "the conditions are right" is not a plan.

In a London auction room a single painting sells in under three minutes for more than a regional orchestra's entire annual budget. Both are "culture," but they belong to different economies. Sport, art and culture look like leisure; they are also industries, and they are funded in two very different ways.
At the commercial end, revenue comes from media rights, ticketing, sponsorship, player transfers and sales. In December 2023 the English Premier League sold its UK live-broadcast rights for a record GBP 6.7 billion, about US$8.45 billion, over four years from the 2025–26 season — the largest domestic sports-media deal in UK history. Media rights are the single biggest revenue source for top European football. The sums at the top are large enough that in the 2023–24 season Real Madrid became the first club ever to record more than EUR 1 billion of revenue in a year.
That scale draws in outside capital with mixed motives. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, a sovereign wealth fund, bought the Premier League club Newcastle United in 2021 for about GBP 305 million and four Saudi domestic-league clubs in 2023. It has also reportedly put around US$5 billion into the breakaway LIV Golf circuit. It states three aims at once: a financial return, "soft power" — influence gained through visibility rather than force — and building a domestic entertainment industry.
The other model cannot cover its costs from tickets. US orchestras, on average, take more revenue from donations than from ticket sales, and American concert tickets average close to US$80, which limits audiences. The economist William Baumol named the reason: a string quartet still needs four players for the same length of time as in 1800, so as wages rise economy-wide, the relative cost of live performance keeps climbing. This "cost disease" is why orchestras, opera and theatre need subsidy or philanthropy indefinitely, not only in a bad year. Countries differ sharply in how much public money they give — Germany funds a dense network of civic theatres and opera houses, while the US provides very little and relies on tax-deductible donations.
The art market sits at the commercial end but is concentrated and, lately, shrinking. Global sales were an estimated US$57.5 billion in 2024, down 12% for a second straight annual fall. The US took 43% of sales by value, the UK 18% and China 15%.
Japan's cultural economy is lopsided in an instructive way. Its fine-art market is small — around 1% of world sales — and its orchestras and theatres rely on the same public-and-corporate mix as elsewhere. But Japan has one cultural industry that is a genuine commercial powerhouse: animation. In 2023 the anime industry earned a record of roughly JPY 3.3 trillion, about US$21 billion, and for the first time in a full year, overseas revenue exceeded domestic revenue. Anime works as a business for reasons a live orchestra cannot copy: it can be reproduced at almost no extra cost, sold into every market at once, and earn from its characters for decades. The divide inside one country mirrors the topic's central split — culture that scales through reproduction can be a big business; culture that must be performed live, each time, generally cannot pay for itself.

It is tempting to think of philanthropy as charity on a bigger scale — the same act, just more money. The structure is different, and the difference is what the debate is about. Charity is direct help given now. Modern institutional philanthropy is large sums held in an endowed foundation that gives away only a slice of its investment returns each year, backed by tax advantages, and increasingly judged on measurable "impact."
That structure raises real questions. A private foundation can, in principle, exist forever: in the United States it must distribute only about 5% of its assets a year to keep its tax exemption. Donor-advised funds — accounts that give the donor an immediate tax deduction but let them recommend grants later — have no payout deadline at all. Critics, notably the political theorist Rob Reich, add that big philanthropy is subsidised by every taxpayer through deductions, is accountable to no electorate, and lets very wealthy individuals set public priorities in health, education and science.
The responses try to answer those objections. One is the spend-down foundation, which commits to giving everything away and closing. In May 2025 the Gates Foundation said it would spend roughly US$200 billion over the next 20 years, about double what it gave in its first 25, and shut permanently by the end of 2045 rather than last forever.
A second response is unrestricted giving. Since 2019 MacKenzie Scott has given more than US$19 billion to over 2,000 organisations, with no application process and minimal reporting. A three-year study published in 2025 found the gifts were "transformational," and that feared downsides — organisations overspending or losing other donors — had not materialised.
A third response is the "impact" turn: formal metrics and impact investing, deploying capital for a measurable social return alongside a financial one, which had grown to an estimated US$1.57 trillion in assets by 2024. A fourth is regulation: the United Kingdom requires charities to demonstrate "public benefit" and is home to one of the world's largest foundations, the Wellcome Trust, whose roughly GBP 37.6 billion endowment funds health and science research.
Japan's philanthropic sector is small relative to the size of its economy. Individual giving was around 0.23% of GDP in 2020, roughly a seventh of the US share of output, and large private foundations of the Gates or Wellcome type are rare. Three things explain this. Tax incentives were weak until recent reforms. There is an expectation that the state and companies, not individuals, handle social provision. And until a 2008 reform — the first rewrite of the relevant law in over a century — government ministries had wide discretion over which bodies counted as serving the public interest. Corporate giving is the stronger tradition, formalised in the Keidanren business federation's "1% Club" of firms that aim to give about 1% of profits. Recent large personal pledges by company founders suggest the picture may be shifting, but from a low base. That is the honest verdict on the responses generally: partial answers to the accountability problem, not a settled fix.

In Sweden, the share of people who used cash for their most recent purchase fell from about 40% in 2010 to about 8% in 2022. In Japan and Germany, cash is still used for a large share of everyday purchases. Same decade, opposite directions. "Money" has changed form many times before, and it is changing again.
Its form has moved in stages: commodity money, then minted coins, then paper notes — first backed by gold or silver, later "fiat" money that has value only because a government declares it legal tender. Then came book-entry bank deposits, payment cards, and mobile and QR-code payments. Each step kept the same unit of account, the krona or the yen, and changed only how value is stored and moved.
One point matters before looking ahead: most money is already digital and private. In modern economies the large majority of the money supply is commercial-bank deposits — money created when a bank lends, existing only as entries in a ledger — not central-bank cash. So "the future of money" is mostly a question about what backs and moves those deposits.
The newest forms sit on top of that. Privately issued tokens pegged to a national currency — over 90% of them to the US dollar — reached a combined value of about US$250 billion by mid-2025. Reported transfer volume hit roughly US$33 trillion in 2025, though most of that is automated trading, not payments. Their real use is growing for cross-border transfers and dollar access in high-inflation economies. Banks and central banks are separately piloting "tokenised deposits" on shared ledgers, so payment and settlement happen together, instantly. Central-bank digital currencies — a digital form of public central-bank money — are being tested in many countries, with China and India furthest along, but most remain at the pilot stage.
Sweden, having led the world toward cashlessness, is now legislating to protect cash. In November 2024 the authorities sent every household a brochure advising people to keep at least a week's worth of cash, around SEK 1,000 per adult, and the central bank backed a proposed legal duty for shops selling essential goods to accept it. Cash is now treated as resilience infrastructure — a backup if the electronic system fails — rather than a relic.
Japan is the useful counter-example to the "cash is dying" story. QR-code wallets have spread quickly, yet Japan's demand for physical currency is around 19–20% of GDP, the highest of any economy the Bank for International Settlements tracks, and cash is still used in more than four in ten retail purchases. Several things hold it in place: near-zero interest rates for decades removed any cost to holding cash rather than deposits; the population is old and used to tangible money; and crime is rare, so cash feels safe. Japan issued its first redesigned banknotes in twenty years in 2024, and only very recently has cash in circulation begun to fall. Whether a society goes cashless is not settled by the technology being available — it depends on interest rates, demographics, trust and habit, which is why the same decade produced near-cashless Sweden and cash-heavy Japan.
