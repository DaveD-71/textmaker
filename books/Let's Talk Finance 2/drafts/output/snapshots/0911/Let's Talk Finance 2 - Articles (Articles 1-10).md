## 1.1. The Cost of Living and Inflation

### 1.1.1. Goal

Explain what inflation is, why it rose around the world after 2021, and why central banks aim for a small positive rate rather than zero.

### 1.1.2. Reading

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise over time. If a basket of goods costs 3% more than it did a year ago, inflation is 3%. A low, steady rate is normal. The problem comes when the rate jumps, because wages and savings usually do not keep up.

After 2021, prices rose sharply almost everywhere. In the United States, consumer prices in June 2022 were 9.1% higher than a year earlier, the largest 12-month increase in about 40 years [1]. Petrol was up 59.9% over the year and food was up 10.4% [1]. In the euro area, inflation reached 10.6% a few months later. The main causes were global: supply chains were still recovering from the pandemic, and energy and food prices rose sharply after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. In response, central banks in the United States, the euro area, the United Kingdom and many other countries raised interest rates quickly through 2022 and 2023.

The size of the shock still varied a lot by country. Japan had lived with near-zero inflation, and sometimes falling prices, for about 25 years. There, inflation rising above 2% was itself news. In 2024 the Bank of Japan raised its main interest rate above zero for the first time since 2007 [2]. Argentina was at the other extreme, with annual inflation of 211% in 2023 after years of economic crisis and a large fall in the value of its currency [3]. Most countries were somewhere in between.

Local factors can add to the global picture. In Japan, a weaker yen made imported food and fuel more expensive. A poor rice harvest in extreme heat then pushed the price of a 5-kilogram bag of rice to about ¥4,260 in May 2025, roughly double a year earlier [4]. A sudden jump in one staple food hits some households hard even when the overall inflation rate looks moderate.

Why aim for 2% rather than 0%? The European Central Bank, like the Bank of England and the US Federal Reserve, targets 2% over the medium term [5]. A small positive rate gives a safety margin against deflation, which is falling prices. Deflation is dangerous because people delay spending when they expect things to get cheaper, and there is a limit to how far interest rates can be cut to fix it. Japan's long struggle to escape deflation is often given as the clearest example of that risk. When inflation instead runs well above target, a central bank usually raises interest rates. That makes mortgages, business loans and government debt more expensive, and slows the economy — the basic cost of bringing prices back under control.

## 1.2. Debt: Credit Cards, Mortgages and Loans

### 1.2.1. Goal

Explain how consumer credit works, why the interest rate varies so much between loan types, and what over-indebtedness looks like.

### 1.2.2. Reading

In the United States in 2026, the average credit card charged about 21% a year in interest. The average new home loan charged about 6.7% [1][2]. Both are loans. The difference in price comes mainly from security. A mortgage is backed by the house, which the lender can take and sell if payments stop, so the lender's risk is lower. A credit card is backed by nothing, so the rate is far higher. A car loan, backed by the car, sits in between.

Interest is the price of borrowing, charged as a percentage of the amount still owed. On a credit card, any unpaid interest is added to the balance and then charged interest itself the next month. This is compounding. It is why a balance that is never fully cleared can grow quickly even when no new spending is added to it.

Borrowing has been rising. US households owed US$18.8 trillion in total in mid-2026, including US$1.26 trillion on credit cards [3]. Some countries carry much heavier household debt than others relative to the size of their economy. In South Korea it passed 2,000 trillion won in 2026, and the country now has one of the highest household-debt burdens in the world [4]. Much of that is home loans. Whether a debt level is dangerous depends on incomes, on interest rates, and on how much of the debt is secured against property that could be sold.

A newer form of borrowing is "buy now, pay later", which splits a purchase into a few interest-free instalments at the checkout. It grew fast with little regulation. In the year to May 2024, about one in five UK adults had used it, and the UK market had grown from under £0.1 billion in 2017 to more than £13 billion [5]. Regulators then moved in. From July 2026 these firms became regulated in the UK and must check that a customer can afford the repayments before offering the plan [5].

Japan has usually carried lighter household debt than the US or UK. Most Japanese cardholders also pay their balance in full the next month rather than borrowing on it. The defining reform came in 2006, after years of problems with people holding loans from many lenders at once. Before then, consumer-finance firms could charge between 20% and 29% a year, exploiting a gap between two laws [6]. The reform closed the gap: the maximum interest rate on unsecured consumer loans was cut to 20%, and total borrowing from non-bank lenders was capped at one-third of a person's annual income [6]. The number of people servicing loans from several lenders fell sharply in the years that followed.

## 1.3. Saving and Investing for the Long Term

### 1.3.1. Goal

Explain the difference between saving and investing, why time and compounding matter, and why low-cost index funds are hard to beat.

### 1.3.2. Reading

People often use "saving" and "investing" to mean the same thing, but they are different. Saving is keeping money safe and available, usually in cash or a bank deposit, for short-term needs. Investing is putting money into assets such as company shares. Their value can fall as well as rise, but over long periods it is expected to grow more than cash would.

Over the very long run, shares have paid more. One widely used study of 35 markets found that global shares returned an average of about 5% a year after inflation from 1900 to 2024. Government bonds returned under 2% [1]. That gap compounds, because returns earn further returns. Over several decades, a higher yearly rate produces a far larger final sum. The catch is that the average hides large swings. Any single 20-year period can be much better or much worse than 5%. The same study found the global real return over 2000 to 2024 was only about 3.5% a year. A long-run average is not a promise about the years ahead.

This is why the time horizon matters. Money needed next year should be saved, not invested, because a fall in value cannot be waited out in time. Money not needed for decades can be invested, because the ups and downs tend to even out over a long period. Diversification helps too: spreading money across many companies means no single failure is severe.

For most people, a low-cost "index fund" is the practical choice. An index fund simply tracks a whole market instead of trying to pick winners. Data from S&P Dow Jones Indices shows that over the 15 years to mid-2025, about 88% of professionally managed US funds investing in large companies did worse than the market index they were measured against [2]. The fees those funds charge are a large part of the reason. A fund that beats the market in one period often falls behind in the next.

Governments encourage long-term investing through tax-advantaged accounts. Money held inside them grows tax-free or with tax delayed: the ISA in the United Kingdom, the 401(k) and IRA in the United States, compulsory superannuation in Australia. Japan reformed its version, the NISA, in January 2024. It sharply raised the tax-free limits and made the tax break permanent. Take-up was fast, and NISA accounts reached about 27 million by mid-2025 [3]. The push matters because Japanese households have long held about half their financial assets in cash and deposits, far more than households in the United States or the euro area [4]. That money earned very little as inflation returned after decades of near-zero price rises.

## 1.4. Housing Markets and Affordability

### 1.4.1. Goal

Explain what drives house prices, why housing became less affordable across much of the rich world, and why homes lose value in some places.

### 1.4.2. Reading

Does a house always gain value? In much of the world the answer has been yes. But it is not true everywhere, and not in every decade.

House prices are set by demand meeting supply. Demand rises with population, with incomes, and with cheap credit. When interest rates are low, buyers can borrow more, so they bid prices up. Supply is slow to respond, because land is limited and new building is controlled by planning rules. When demand grows faster than supply, prices climb faster than incomes, and housing becomes less affordable.

That is what happened across much of the rich world after the 1990s. In New Zealand, house prices after inflation more than doubled between 2000 and 2007 [1]. Then interest rates rose sharply in 2022, and several of these markets fell. New Zealand prices dropped about 14% from their 2021 peak, and the largest cities fell by around 20% [1]. Even after those falls, real house prices across advanced economies were still about 20% above their level at the end of the 2008–09 financial crisis [2].

Governments have tried different levers. Some cities have relaxed their planning rules to allow more building. After Auckland loosened its zoning in 2016, research found the number of new homes approved each year roughly doubled, and rents there rose more slowly than in the rest of New Zealand [3]. Other governments have tried to cap rents directly. Berlin brought in a rent cap in 2020, but Germany's Federal Constitutional Court struck it down the next year, ruling that the city had no power to set it [4]. A few, such as Singapore, have gone further and built most of the housing themselves: about 80% of resident households there live in flats built by a government agency [5].

Japan is the clearest exception to "houses always go up". There, the building and the land are valued separately, and the structure is treated as a wasting asset. The tax system assigns a wooden house a life of about 22 years. Older houses are often priced as if the building is worth almost nothing, with nearly all the value in the land. Two things reinforce this. One is heavy new construction: around 800,000 homes were started in the 2023 financial year, despite a shrinking population [6]. The other is light planning rules that make it easy to knock a house down and rebuild. For a buyer, this means a home is closer to a car than to a store of wealth: it wears out, and most of the lasting value is in the land beneath it. The result is a rising number of empty homes. Japan's 2023 survey counted about 9 million, a record 13.8% of the total housing stock [7].

## 1.5. Scams, Fraud and Financial Self-Defence

### 1.5.1. Goal

Explain how modern financial fraud works, why reported losses understate the problem, and how countries differ in protecting victims.

### 1.5.2. Reading

People in the United States reported losing US$12.5 billion to fraud in 2024, about a quarter more than the year before. The largest single category was fake investments [1]. That figure counts only what was reported. Much fraud is never reported, out of embarrassment or because people do not know where to complain, so the real total is higher.

Fraud today is organised and industrial. Criminals use fake investment platforms, phishing messages that copy a real bank, impersonation calls, and "romance" approaches that build a relationship before asking for money. A growing type combines the two: a fake romance leads into a fake investment, sometimes in cryptocurrency, until the victim's savings are gone. A common method throughout is the authorised push payment scam. The victim is tricked into sending a bank transfer themselves, which makes it harder to reverse, because the payment was technically approved by the account holder. The United Nations has reported that much of this activity is run from large, guarded compounds in South-East Asia, often staffed by trafficked workers. It estimated that losses to victims across East and South-East Asia ran into tens of billions of US dollars in 2023 alone [5].

Countries protect victims very differently. From October 2024, the United Kingdom has required banks to reimburse most victims of authorised push payment fraud, up to £85,000 per claim, with the cost shared between the sending bank and the receiving bank [2]. In most other countries, including the United States and Japan, whether a victim gets any money back is far less certain. Some countermeasures do seem to help. In Australia, reported scam losses fell by about 26% in 2024 after coordinated action by government, banks and telecoms firms [3].

Japan has fought "special fraud" for two decades. These scams often begin with a phone call from someone posing as a relative in trouble, or as a police officer. Losses hit a record ¥72 billion in 2024, up nearly 60% in a year, and about 45% of victims were aged 75 or over [4]. A separate and newer category, investment and romance scams that start on social media, added a further ¥127 billion [4]. The response has focused on adding friction. Many banks suspend cash-machine transfers for older customers who have not used the function recently. Some cap daily withdrawals. Convenience-store staff are trained to question elderly customers who try to buy large numbers of prepaid cards.

Self-defence comes down to a few habits. Treat any unexpected contact about money as suspicious. Never move money because someone has pressured you to act quickly. And check any request through a phone number or app you found yourself, not one the other person gave you.

## 2.1. How Companies Raise Money

### 2.1.1. Goal

Explain the two basic ways a company funds itself, the difference between staying private and going public, and why fewer companies list than before.

### 2.1.2. Reading

In 2025 two American technology companies took opposite paths. Figma sold shares to the public and listed on the New York Stock Exchange, raising about US$1.2 billion. SpaceX did the reverse: it stayed private and raised money by selling shares to selected investors at a valuation of around US$400 billion, larger than most listed companies, without ever offering its stock on an exchange [1]. Both routes lead to the same place, which is cash to run and grow the business, but they involve very different trade-offs.

A company can raise money in two basic ways. It can sell equity, meaning shares of ownership, which never has to be repaid but gives new owners a claim on future profits and a say in decisions. Or it can take on debt, meaning bank loans or bonds, which leaves ownership unchanged but must be repaid with interest whether business is good or bad. Equity itself comes in two forms. Private equity is raised from founders, venture capital funds and buyout firms, and the company stays closed. Public equity is raised through an initial public offering, or IPO, when shares are first sold to the public and then trade freely on a stock exchange.

For decades, going public was the natural goal for a successful company. That has changed in the United States and parts of Europe. The number of American listed companies peaked at more than 8,000 in 1996 and is now roughly half that [2]. At the same time, a large pool of private capital, estimated at around US$22 trillion worldwide by 2024, now lets big companies raise billions and delay listing for years [1]. Public markets still work: in 2024 there were about 1,145 IPOs worldwide, and India was among the busiest markets, with a record 268 new listings on its main exchange raising some US$19.5 billion [3].

In Japan and much of continental Europe and Asia, bank lending has long mattered more than share issues. Japanese firms traditionally relied on a "main bank" that lent to them, held their shares and watched over management. That arrangement is now slowly unwinding, as companies and banks sell off the cross-held shares that once locked ownership in place. The Tokyo Stock Exchange reorganised itself in 2022 into three segments, Prime, Standard and Growth. In 2023 it began pressing listed companies to set out plans to raise their share price, aiming especially at the many firms trading below the value of their own assets [2]. The mix of equity and debt, and of private and public money, differs from one country to the next. But every company faces the same first question: whose money to take, and what to give up for it.

## 2.2. Startups, Venture Capital and Failure

### 2.2.1. Goal

Explain how young companies raise money in stages, why most of them fail, and what venture investors expect from the few that succeed.

### 2.2.2. Reading

Of every ten new businesses in the United States, about eight survive their first year, about five reach year five, and about three reach year ten [1]. Among companies backed by venture capital, the odds of a big payout are longer still: one widely cited study found that roughly three in four never return their investors' cash. Failure is not a rare accident in this part of finance. It is the normal outcome, and the whole model is built around that fact.

A startup usually raises money in stages, each tied to reaching a goal. The earliest money, often called seed funding, comes from founders, friends and specialist early investors. It buys the company time to build a product and find its first customers. If that works, a venture capital fund may lead a larger round, labelled Series A, then Series B, and so on. Each round buys a slice of ownership, usually at a higher price than the last, and the money is meant to last until the next milestone. Venture capital is money raised from pension funds, universities and wealthy individuals and placed in young, high-risk companies in exchange for equity. The investors know most of their bets will fail, so they need the rare winner to return many times its cost. The end goal, or exit, is a sale to a larger company or a stock market listing, which is how the fund turns its shares back into cash.

The size of the funding pool rises and falls sharply. Global venture investment reached roughly US$440 billion in 2025, with nearly half going to artificial intelligence companies, after a steep fall in 2022 and 2023 [2]. When funding tightens, companies that raised money at a high valuation must often accept a lower one in the next round, a "down round" that dilutes founders and early staff. The most striking recent failure was WeWork, an office-space company valued at US$47 billion in early 2019, which then failed to list, lost billions and filed for bankruptcy in 2023 [2].

Japan's venture scene is smaller but growing. One estimate put Japan at seven "unicorns", meaning private companies worth over US$1 billion, in 2023, against more than 600 in the United States [3]. In 2022 the government adopted a five-year plan to lift the number of startups and unicorns sharply, with public money and tax incentives behind it. Europe and India have both built up sizeable venture markets of their own over the same period. Whether in Tokyo, Bangalore or California, the arithmetic is the same: investors accept many total losses in exchange for a small chance of backing a company that becomes very large. That is why a single success, such as an early stake in a company that later lists, can carry a whole fund.

### 2.2.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** startup, seed funding, funding round, down round, exit, unicorn

**Recycled terms:** venture capital, equity, listing, valuation

## 2.3. The Gig Economy and Income Security

### 2.3.1. Goal

Explain what platform work is, why the "employee or self-employed" question matters so much, and how different countries have answered it.

### 2.3.2. Reading

A delivery rider logs into an app on Friday evening, accepts trips for four hours, and is paid for each one. There is no set shift, no manager, and no promise of work next week. The rider values the freedom to choose hours, but carries risks an employee does not: earnings that swing from week to week, and usually no paid holiday, no sick pay, no employer pension contribution and no unemployment cover. This is platform work, meaning paid tasks arranged through an app or website, and it has grown fast. The International Labour Organization estimates that the number of digital labour platforms worldwide grew about five-fold in the decade to 2020 [1].

The core dispute is a legal one: are these workers really self-employed, as the platforms say, or are they employees entitled to labour protections? The answer decides who bears the cost of holidays, illness and old age. Courts and parliaments have reached different conclusions. In February 2021 the United Kingdom Supreme Court ruled that Uber drivers are "workers", a middle category that brings the minimum wage and paid leave. It also held that their working time counts from the moment the app is switched on [2]. The European Union adopted a directive in 2024 that presumes a platform worker is an employee unless the company can show otherwise, with member states to write it into national law by December 2026 [3]. Spain passed a law in 2021 making delivery couriers employees.

The United States has gone the other way, at least in its largest state. California passed a law in 2019 that would have made many gig drivers employees. Ride-hailing and delivery companies then funded a ballot measure, Proposition 22, that carved themselves out, spending more than US$200 million on the campaign. California's Supreme Court upheld the measure in 2024, so drivers there remain independent contractors with a limited set of benefits and no sick pay or unemployment cover. Most other United States states never adopted a test like California's in the first place.

Japan has taken two narrower routes rather than one big reclassification. A Freelance Act, in force from November 2024, does not make freelancers employees. Instead it puts duties on the companies that hire them: state the work and the fee in writing, pay within 60 days, guard against harassment, and give 30 days' notice before ending a continuing contract [4]. Separately, in 2022 a Tokyo labour board found that Uber Eats couriers are "workers" under union law and can bargain collectively, a decision the company has challenged. Across all these systems the same tension runs. The flexibility that makes platform work attractive is bound up with the absence of the protections that make an income secure.

### 2.3.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** platform work, the gig economy, self-employed, independent contractor, worker status, collective bargaining

**Recycled terms:** employee, minimum wage, pension contribution

## 2.4. Executive Pay and Inequality Inside Firms

### 2.4.1. Goal

Explain how top executive pay is built, how it compares with a typical worker's pay in different countries, and what rules and votes now surround it.

### 2.4.2. Reading

In 2024 the chief executives of the 350 largest United States companies were paid about 281 times as much as a typical worker in their industry, up from 21 times in 1965 [1]. The gap is now the subject of laws, disclosures and shareholder votes in most rich countries, and it looks very different from one country to the next.

Top executive pay usually has three parts. There is a base salary, fixed and relatively modest as a share of the total. There is an annual bonus tied to that year's results. And there is stock-based pay, meaning shares or options that vest over several years and now make up the large majority of a big American chief executive's package. Because so much of the pay is in stock, its final value depends on the share price, which is why supporters call it pay for performance and critics call it a windfall in a rising market.

Two kinds of rule have grown up around the question. The first is disclosure. Since 2018, United States listed companies must publish the ratio of chief executive pay to the pay of their median employee, meaning the employee exactly in the middle of the pay range. The United Kingdom has required a similar figure since 2020. The second kind of rule is the shareholder vote, known as "say on pay". In the United Kingdom and much of Europe the vote on pay policy is binding, so a company may not pay outside an approved policy. In the United States the vote is only advisory, meaning the board can proceed even if shareholders vote no. Switzerland went furthest, banning sign-on and severance bonuses for listed-company executives by referendum in 2013. Votes against pay do happen: Netflix shareholders rejected the company's executive pay package in an advisory vote in 2023 [2].

Japan sits at the other end of the range. The median ratio of executive to employee pay across listed Japanese companies is roughly 10 times, far below the American figure [3]. Since 2010, any director paid 100 million yen or more must be named individually in the company's annual report. Fewer than 300 executives crossed that line in 2009; a record 1,345 did so at 606 companies in the year to March 2025, and the highest-paid were mostly foreign executives at Japanese firms [3]. The rise reflects the spread of stock-based pay rather than a jump in salaries. The underlying argument is the same everywhere: is very high pay the price of attracting and motivating rare talent, or is it simply what executives can command when boards decide their rewards?

**New terms:** executive compensation, base salary, stock-based pay, pay ratio, say-on-pay vote, disclosure

**Recycled terms:** shareholder, board, median

## 2.5. Banks: What They Do and How They Fail

### 2.5.1. Goal

Explain the basic job of a bank, why that job makes every bank fragile, and how deposit insurance and a central bank try to prevent runs.

### 2.5.2. Reading

On Friday 10 March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank in California was closed by regulators. The day before, its customers had tried to withdraw more than US$40 billion in a single day, faster than any bank run in modern records [1]. It was, at the time, the second-largest bank failure in United States history. What went wrong was not fraud or reckless lending. It was the ordinary business of banking, done at the wrong moment.

A bank takes in deposits, which customers can withdraw at any time, and lends most of that money out for years, as mortgages and business loans. Turning short-term deposits into long-term loans is called maturity transformation, and it is useful: it channels idle savings into productive use. But it leaves every bank unable to repay all its depositors at once. If enough customers ask for their money together, even a sound bank can be forced to sell assets at a loss and collapse. That is a bank run, and the fear of one can cause one.

Governments try to break that logic in two ways. The first is deposit insurance: a government-backed promise that small savers will be repaid up to a limit even if their bank fails. In the United States the limit is US$250,000 per depositor per bank [2]. In Japan the Deposit Insurance Corporation covers ordinary deposits up to 10 million yen, plus interest, and covers everyday settlement accounts in full [3]. The point is to remove the reason for an ordinary saver to join a run. The second tool is the central bank acting as lender of last resort, lending cash to a sound bank against good collateral so it does not have to sell assets in a panic. When the British lender Northern Rock faced a run in 2007, queues formed outside its branches until the government guaranteed its deposits.

Sometimes a bank is judged too important to close. In March 2023 the Swiss authorities arranged for UBS to take over its troubled rival Credit Suisse over a single weekend. Central bank funding and a government guarantee stood behind the deal. Depositors lost nothing, but shareholders and holders of certain risky bonds lost heavily [4]. Japan faced a broader failure in 1997 and 1998, when Hokkaido Takushoku Bank and the Yamaichi securities house collapsed. The government eventually took two large banks into public ownership and injected public money to steady the rest, a slow and costly clean-up that took years. The lesson from all these cases is that a bank's stability rests less on its own vault than on the belief that the safety net behind it will hold.

**Recycled terms:** central bank, regulator, bond
