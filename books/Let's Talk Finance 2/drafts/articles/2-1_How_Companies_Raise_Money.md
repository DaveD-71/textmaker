## 2.1. How Companies Raise Money

### 2.1.1. Goal

Explain the two basic ways a company funds itself, the difference between staying private and going public, and why fewer companies list than before.

### 2.1.2. Reading

In 2025 two American technology companies took opposite paths. Figma sold shares to the public and listed on the New York Stock Exchange, raising about US$1.2 billion. SpaceX did the reverse: it stayed private and raised money by selling shares to selected investors at a valuation of around US$400 billion, larger than most listed companies, without ever offering its stock on an exchange [1]. Both routes lead to the same place, which is cash to run and grow the business, but they involve very different trade-offs.

A company can raise money in two basic ways. It can sell equity, meaning shares of ownership, which never has to be repaid but gives new owners a claim on future profits and a say in decisions. Or it can take on debt, meaning bank loans or bonds, which leaves ownership unchanged but must be repaid with interest whether business is good or bad. Equity itself comes in two forms. Private equity is raised from founders, venture capital funds and buyout firms, and the company stays closed. Public equity is raised through an initial public offering, or IPO, when shares are first sold to the public and then trade freely on a stock exchange.

For decades, going public was the natural goal for a successful company. That has changed in the United States and parts of Europe. The number of American listed companies peaked at more than 8,000 in 1996 and is now roughly half that [2]. At the same time, a large pool of private capital, estimated at around US$22 trillion worldwide by 2024, now lets big companies raise billions and delay listing for years [1]. Public markets still work: in 2024 there were about 1,145 IPOs worldwide, and India was among the busiest markets, with a record 268 new listings on its main exchange raising some US$19.5 billion [3].

In Japan and much of continental Europe and Asia, bank lending has long mattered more than share issues. Japanese firms traditionally relied on a "main bank" that lent to them, held their shares and watched over management. That arrangement is now slowly unwinding, as companies and banks sell off the cross-held shares that once locked ownership in place. The Tokyo Stock Exchange reorganised itself in 2022 into three segments, Prime, Standard and Growth. In 2023 it began pressing listed companies to set out plans to raise their share price, aiming especially at the many firms trading below the value of their own assets [2]. The mix of equity and debt, and of private and public money, differs from one country to the next. But every company faces the same first question: whose money to take, and what to give up for it.

### 2.1.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** equity, debt, initial public offering (IPO), listing, private equity, venture capital

**Recycled terms:** bond, stock exchange, valuation

### 2.1.4. Reading Questions

1. How did Figma and SpaceX raise money in 2025, and how were the two approaches different?
2. What is the difference between raising equity and taking on debt?
3. What has happened to the number of listed companies in the United States since 1996?
4. What is private capital, and how has it changed the choice about going public?
5. What was the traditional role of a "main bank" for a Japanese company?

### 2.1.5. Discussion Questions

1. Selling equity means giving up part ownership and some control. When is that a price worth paying, and when is it not?
2. Many large companies now stay private for years. What do ordinary savers lose if the most successful firms never list?
3. Bank lending dominates corporate finance in Japan; share markets dominate in the United States. What are the strengths of each system?
4. The Tokyo Stock Exchange is pressing companies to raise their share prices. Should an exchange do that, or is it none of its business? Explain.
5. You advise the founder of a fast-growing company deciding whether to go public. Set out the two main questions they should answer first.
