## 3.5. Emerging Markets and Development Finance

### 3.5.1. Goal

Explain what "emerging market" means for an investor and a government, and how developing countries raise money to grow.

### 3.5.2. Reading

In 2024 two large developing economies borrowed on very different terms. India sold ten-year government bonds at yields close to 7%, with demand strong enough that the country was added to a major global bond index, drawing in billions of new foreign money [1]. Egypt, the same year, could only secure funding after an expanded IMF programme and a large UAE investment deal, its dollar bonds having traded at distressed levels [2]. Both are called emerging markets, but the label covers a wide range of risk.

An emerging market is an economy that is growing and opening to foreign investors but is not yet as stable, wealthy or well-regulated as an advanced economy. For investors, these markets offer higher potential returns and faster growth, in exchange for greater currency risk, political risk and the danger of sudden capital outflows — money leaving quickly when sentiment turns. Index providers such as MSCI and FTSE Russell decide which countries qualify, and inclusion matters: when a country joins an index, funds that track it must buy its assets, lowering the government's borrowing cost.

Developing countries fund growth from three main sources, each with a catch. The first is foreign direct investment — a foreign company building a factory or buying a lasting stake in a local business. It is the most stable form of foreign money because it is hard to pull out quickly, but it flows mainly to a few larger economies. The second is borrowing on international markets in dollars or euros, which is fast to arrange but creates a currency mismatch: if the local currency falls, the debt becomes far heavier. The third is lending from development finance institutions — bodies such as the World Bank, the regional development banks and national aid agencies — which offer lower rates and longer terms but attach conditions and move slowly.

Japan is one of the largest bilateral providers of this last kind of finance. Through the Japan International Cooperation Agency and its leading role at the Asian Development Bank, it funds transport, energy and water projects across South and Southeast Asia, often as long-term loans in yen at low interest [3]. Supporters say this builds lasting capacity; critics note it can tie borrowers to Japanese contractors and add to debt loads.

The recurring danger is the sudden stop. Emerging markets depend on foreign capital that can reverse fast when global interest rates rise or risk appetite falls, as it did across 2022 when the US Federal Reserve tightened policy and investors pulled back from riskier assets worldwide [4]. Countries that had borrowed heavily in dollars, such as Sri Lanka, defaulted; countries with deeper local savings, larger reserves and credible central banks, such as India and Indonesia, absorbed the shock [5]. The clearest lesson of development finance is that how a country borrows — the currency, the maturity, the lender — often matters more than how much.

**New terms:** emerging market, capital outflow, foreign direct investment, currency mismatch, development finance institution, sudden stop

**Recycled terms:** bond, central bank, interest rate, default

### 3.5.4. Reading Questions

1. How did India's and Egypt's borrowing terms differ in 2024?
2. What does the reading say an emerging market offers investors, and what do they risk in return?
3. Why does joining a global bond index lower a government's borrowing cost?
4. What is the "catch" with borrowing on international markets in dollars or euros?
5. What role does Japan play in development finance, and what do critics say about it?

### 3.5.5. Discussion Questions

1. The reading says how a country borrows often matters more than how much. Do you agree? Use the examples in the text.
2. Foreign direct investment is the most stable foreign money but flows mainly to larger economies. What could a smaller country do to attract more of it?
3. Development finance institutions offer cheap, long loans but attach conditions and move slowly. Are those trade-offs worth it for a borrowing government?
4. Compare the positions of Sri Lanka and India during the 2022 tightening. What made the difference?
5. You advise the finance ministry of a fast-growing emerging economy. Recommend a borrowing strategy that reduces the risk of a sudden stop, and explain your choices.
