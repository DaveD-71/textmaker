## 1.1. The Cost of Living and Inflation

### 1.1.1. Goal

Explain what inflation is, why it rose around the world after 2021, and why central banks aim for a small positive rate rather than zero.

### 1.1.2. Reading

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise over time. If a basket of goods costs 3% more than it did a year ago, inflation is 3%. A low, steady rate is normal. The problem comes when the rate jumps, because wages and savings usually do not keep up.

After 2021, prices rose sharply almost everywhere. In the United States, consumer prices in June 2022 were 9.1% higher than a year earlier, the largest 12-month increase in about 40 years [1]. Petrol was up 59.9% over the year and food was up 10.4% [1]. In the euro area, inflation reached 10.6% a few months later. The main causes were global: supply chains were still recovering from the pandemic, and energy and food prices rose sharply after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. In response, central banks in the United States, the euro area, the United Kingdom and many other countries raised interest rates quickly through 2022 and 2023.

The size of the shock still varied a lot by country. Japan had lived with near-zero inflation, and sometimes falling prices, for about 25 years. There, inflation rising above 2% was itself news. In 2024 the Bank of Japan raised its main interest rate above zero for the first time since 2007 [2]. Argentina was at the other extreme, with annual inflation of 211% in 2023 after years of economic crisis and a large fall in the value of its currency [3]. Most countries were somewhere in between.

Local factors can add to the global picture. In Japan, a weaker yen made imported food and fuel more expensive. A poor rice harvest in extreme heat then pushed the price of a 5-kilogram bag of rice to about ¥4,260 in May 2025, roughly double a year earlier [4]. A sudden jump in one staple food hits some households hard even when the overall inflation rate looks moderate.

Why aim for 2% rather than 0%? The European Central Bank, like the Bank of England and the US Federal Reserve, targets 2% over the medium term [5]. A small positive rate gives a safety margin against deflation, which is falling prices. Deflation is dangerous because people delay spending when they expect things to get cheaper, and there is a limit to how far interest rates can be cut to fix it. Japan's long struggle to escape deflation is often given as the clearest example of that risk. When inflation instead runs well above target, a central bank usually raises interest rates. That makes mortgages, business loans and government debt more expensive, and slows the economy — the basic cost of bringing prices back under control.

1. In your own words, what is inflation, and what is deflation?
2. What were the main global causes of the price rises after 2021?

4. How did local factors add to inflation in Japan?

### 1.1.5. Discussion Questions

1. Which is worse for ordinary people: prices rising quickly, or prices slowly falling? Explain, using an example.
2. Inflation affects savers, borrowers, workers and pensioners differently. Rank these four groups from most harmed to least harmed by a sudden rise in inflation, and explain your order.
3. Should a central bank raise interest rates quickly to stop inflation, even if it causes job losses, or move slowly? Give your view.
4. A government wants to help with the cost of living by sending money to households. How might this help, and how might it make inflation worse?
5. Advise someone on how to protect the value of their savings during a period of high inflation. What are their options, and what is the drawback of each?
