## 1.3. Saving and Investing for the Long Term

### 1.3.1. Goal

Explain the difference between saving and investing, why time and compounding matter, and why low-cost index funds are hard to beat.

### 1.3.2. Reading

People often use "saving" and "investing" to mean the same thing, but they are different. Saving is keeping money safe and available, usually in cash or a bank deposit, for short-term needs. Investing is putting money into assets such as company shares. Their value can fall as well as rise, but over long periods it is expected to grow more than cash would.

Over the very long run, shares have paid more. One widely used study of 35 markets found that global shares returned an average of about 5% a year after inflation from 1900 to 2024. Government bonds returned under 2% [1]. That gap compounds, because returns earn further returns. Over several decades, a higher yearly rate produces a far larger final sum. The catch is that the average hides large swings. Any single 20-year period can be much better or much worse than 5%. The same study found the global real return over 2000 to 2024 was only about 3.5% a year. A long-run average is not a promise about the years ahead.

This is why the time horizon matters. Money needed next year should be saved, not invested, because a fall in value cannot be waited out in time. Money not needed for decades can be invested, because the ups and downs tend to even out over a long period. Diversification helps too: spreading money across many companies means no single failure is severe.

For most people, a low-cost "index fund" is the practical choice. An index fund simply tracks a whole market instead of trying to pick winners. Data from S&P Dow Jones Indices shows that over the 15 years to mid-2025, about 88% of professionally managed US funds investing in large companies did worse than the market index they were measured against [2]. The fees those funds charge are a large part of the reason. A fund that beats the market in one period often falls behind in the next.

Governments encourage long-term investing through tax-advantaged accounts. Money held inside them grows tax-free or with tax delayed: the ISA in the United Kingdom, the 401(k) and IRA in the United States, compulsory superannuation in Australia. Japan reformed its version, the NISA, in January 2024. It sharply raised the tax-free limits and made the tax break permanent. Take-up was fast, and NISA accounts reached about 27 million by mid-2025 [3]. The push matters because Japanese households have long held about half their financial assets in cash and deposits, far more than households in the United States or the euro area [4]. That money earned very little as inflation returned after decades of near-zero price rises.

1. What is the difference between saving and investing, as the reading describes it?
2. What did the long-run study find about the returns on shares compared with government bonds?
3. Why does the reading say money needed next year should be saved rather than invested?

### 1.3.5. Discussion Questions

1. Shares have paid more than cash over the long run, but with large swings along the way. Would you accept those swings for a higher expected return? Explain.
2. About 88% of professional US fund managers did worse than the index. Why might professionals, on average, fail to beat a simple market fund?
3. Compare keeping savings in a bank deposit with investing them in an index fund, for someone aged 30 and for someone aged 65.
4. Governments use tax breaks to encourage long-term investing. Is that a good use of public money? Give your view.
5. Advise someone in their twenties who has just started saving each month. How should they split the money between cash and investments, and why?
