## 1.3. Saving and Investing for the Long Term

### 1.3.1. Goal

Explain the difference between saving and investing, why time and compounding matter, and why low-cost index funds are hard to beat.

### 1.3.2. Reading

People often use "saving" and "investing" to mean the same thing, but they are different. Saving is keeping money safe and available, usually in cash or a bank deposit, for short-term needs. Investing is putting money into assets such as company shares that can fall as well as rise, but are expected to grow more over long periods.

Over the very long run, shares have paid more. One widely used study of 35 markets found that global shares returned an average of about 5% a year after inflation from 1900 to 2024, compared with under 2% for government bonds [1]. That gap compounds: returns earn further returns, so over decades a higher yearly rate produces a far larger final sum. The catch is that the average hides large swings, and any single 20-year period can be much better or worse.

This is why time horizon matters. Money needed next year should be saved, not invested, because a fall cannot be waited out. Money not needed for decades can be invested, because the swings tend to even out.

For most people, a low-cost "index fund" — one that simply tracks a whole market rather than trying to pick winners — is the practical choice. Data from S&P Dow Jones Indices shows that over the 15 years to mid-2025, about 88% of professionally managed US funds investing in large companies did worse than the market index they were measured against [2]. Fees are a large part of the reason.

Governments encourage long-term investing through tax-advantaged accounts: the ISA in the United Kingdom, the 401(k) and IRA in the United States, compulsory superannuation in Australia. Japan reformed its version, the NISA, in January 2024, sharply raising the tax-free limits and making the tax break permanent. Take-up was fast: NISA accounts reached about 27 million by mid-2025 [3]. The push matters because Japanese households have long held about half their financial assets in cash and deposits — far more than in the United States or the euro area [4] — earning very little while inflation returned.

### 1.3.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** saving, investing, return, real return, compounding, diversification, index fund, actively managed fund, time horizon, tax-advantaged account

**Recycled terms:** inflation, interest rate, volatility, purchasing power

### 1.3.4. Reading Questions

1. What is the difference between saving and investing, as the reading describes it?
2. What did the long-run study find about the returns on shares compared with government bonds?
3. Why does the reading say money needed next year should be saved rather than invested?
4. What did the S&P Dow Jones data show about professionally managed funds?
5. Why does the reading say the NISA reform "matters" for Japanese households?

### 1.3.5. Discussion Questions

1. Shares have paid more than cash over the long run, but with large swings along the way. Would you accept those swings for a higher expected return? Explain.
2. About 88% of professional US fund managers did worse than the index. Why might professionals, on average, fail to beat a simple market fund?
3. Compare keeping savings in a bank deposit with investing them in an index fund, for someone aged 30 and for someone aged 65.
4. Governments use tax breaks to encourage long-term investing. Is that a good use of public money? Give your view.
5. Advise someone in their twenties who has just started saving each month. How should they split the money between cash and investments, and why?
