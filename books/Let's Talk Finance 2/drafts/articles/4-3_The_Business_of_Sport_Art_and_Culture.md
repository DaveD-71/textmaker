## 4.3. The Business of Sport, Art and Culture

### 4.3.1. Goal

Contrast the parts of sport and culture that fund themselves commercially with the parts that depend on subsidy and philanthropy.

### 4.3.2. Reading

In a London auction room a single painting sells in under three minutes for more than a regional orchestra's entire annual budget. Both are "culture," but they belong to different economies. Sport, art and culture look like leisure; they are also industries, and they are funded in two very different ways.

At the commercial end, revenue comes from media rights, ticketing, sponsorship, player transfers and sales. In December 2023 the English Premier League sold its UK live-broadcast rights for a record GBP 6.7 billion, about US$8.45 billion, over four years from the 2025–26 season — the largest domestic sports-media deal in UK history [1]. Media rights are the single biggest revenue source for top European football, and the sums at the top are large enough that in the 2023–24 season Real Madrid became the first club ever to record more than EUR 1 billion of revenue in a year [2].

That scale draws in outside capital with mixed motives. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, a sovereign wealth fund, bought the Premier League club Newcastle United in October 2021 for about GBP 305 million, took ownership of four Saudi domestic-league clubs in June 2023, and has reportedly put around US$5 billion into the breakaway LIV Golf circuit [3]. The fund states three aims at once: a financial return, "soft power" — influence gained through visibility rather than force — and building a domestic entertainment industry.

The other model cannot cover its costs from tickets. US orchestras, on average, take more revenue from donations than from ticket sales, and American concert tickets average close to US$80, which limits audiences [4]. The economist William Baumol named the structural reason: a string quartet still needs four players for the same length of time as in 1800, so as wages rise across the economy the relative cost of live performance keeps climbing. This "cost disease" is why orchestras, opera and theatre need subsidy or philanthropy indefinitely, not only in a bad year. Countries differ sharply in how much public money they give: Germany funds a dense network of civic theatres and opera houses, while the United States provides very little and relies on tax-deductible private donations.

The art market sits at the commercial end but is concentrated and, lately, shrinking. Global sales were an estimated US$57.5 billion in 2024, down 12% and a second straight annual fall, with the steepest declines at the top end; the United States accounted for 43% of sales by value, the UK 18% and China 15% [5].

Japan's cultural economy is lopsided in an instructive way. Its fine-art market is small — around 1% of world sales — and its orchestras and theatres rely on the same mix of public and corporate money as elsewhere. But Japan has one cultural industry that is a genuine commercial powerhouse: animation. In 2023 the anime industry earned a record of roughly JPY 3.3 trillion, about US$21 billion, and for the first time across a full year, revenue from overseas exceeded revenue from Japan itself [6]. Anime works as a business for reasons a live orchestra cannot copy: it can be reproduced and distributed at almost no extra cost, sold into every market at once, and earn from its characters and merchandise for decades. The divide inside one country mirrors the topic's central split — culture that scales through reproduction and rights can be a big business; culture that must be performed live, each time, generally cannot pay for itself.

### 4.3.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** media / broadcast rights; sponsorship; sovereign wealth fund; "soft power"; philanthropy; "cost disease" (Baumol)

**Recycled terms:** market; return; capital

### 4.3.4. Reading Questions

1. What are the main revenue sources at the commercial end of sport and culture?
2. What three aims does Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund state for its sports investments?
3. What is "cost disease," and why does it affect live performance in particular?
4. How do Germany and the United States differ in how they fund the performing arts?
5. Why does the reading call anime a "commercial powerhouse" while Japan's fine-art market is small?

### 4.3.5. Discussion Questions

1. Sovereign wealth funds now own major sports clubs and events. What are the benefits and risks of that kind of ownership for a league?
2. If "cost disease" means live performance will always need subsidy, is that a good reason to fund it publicly, or a reason to let ticket prices rise?
3. The reading says culture that "scales through reproduction" can be a big business. Does that mean live performance is destined to shrink? Give reasons.
4. Compare the funding of elite football and a public orchestra. Which model is more secure, and which produces better outcomes for audiences?
5. You advise a mid-sized city orchestra that covers only 40% of its costs from tickets. Propose a funding plan and explain the trade-offs in your choices.
