## 1.4. Housing Markets and Affordability

### 1.4.1. Goal

Explain what drives house prices, why housing became less affordable across much of the rich world, and why homes lose value in some places.

### 1.4.2. Reading

Does a house always gain value? In much of the world the answer has been yes. But it is not true everywhere, and not in every decade.

House prices are set by demand meeting supply. Demand rises with population, with incomes, and with cheap credit. When interest rates are low, buyers can borrow more, so they bid prices up. Supply is slow to respond, because land is limited and new building is controlled by planning rules. When demand grows faster than supply, prices climb faster than incomes, and housing becomes less affordable.

That is what happened across much of the rich world after the 1990s. In New Zealand, house prices after inflation more than doubled between 2000 and 2007 [1]. Then interest rates rose sharply in 2022, and several of these markets fell. New Zealand prices dropped about 14% from their 2021 peak, and the largest cities fell by around 20% [1]. Even after those falls, real house prices across advanced economies were still about 20% above their level at the end of the 2008–09 financial crisis [2].

Governments have tried different levers. Some cities have relaxed their planning rules to allow more building. After Auckland loosened its zoning in 2016, research found the number of new homes approved each year roughly doubled, and rents there rose more slowly than in the rest of New Zealand [3]. Other governments have tried to cap rents directly. Berlin brought in a rent cap in 2020, but Germany's Federal Constitutional Court struck it down the next year, ruling that the city had no power to set it [4]. A few, such as Singapore, have gone further and built most of the housing themselves: about 80% of resident households there live in flats built by a government agency [5].

Japan is the clearest exception to "houses always go up". There, the building and the land are valued separately, and the structure is treated as a wasting asset. The tax system assigns a wooden house a life of about 22 years. Older houses are often priced as if the building is worth almost nothing, with nearly all the value in the land. Two things reinforce this. One is heavy new construction: around 800,000 homes were started in the 2023 financial year, despite a shrinking population [6]. The other is light planning rules that make it easy to knock a house down and rebuild. For a buyer, this means a home is closer to a car than to a store of wealth: it wears out, and most of the lasting value is in the land beneath it. The result is a rising number of empty homes. Japan's 2023 survey counted about 9 million, a record 13.8% of the total housing stock [7].

### 1.4.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** affordability, housing supply, zoning, upzoning, rent control, depreciation

**Recycled terms:** interest rate, central bank, inflation

### 1.4.4. Reading Questions

1. Why do low interest rates tend to push house prices up?
2. Why is the supply of housing "slow to respond" to rising demand?
3. What happened to house prices in New Zealand after interest rates rose in 2022?
4. What was the effect of Auckland loosening its zoning rules in 2016?
5. Why does the reading say Japan is "the clearest exception to 'houses always go up'"?

### 1.4.5. Discussion Questions

1. Is a home better understood as an investment or as shelter? Does the answer change depending on the country?
2. Auckland allowed more building; Berlin tried to cap rents. Which approach is more likely to make housing affordable, and why?
3. In Japan, houses lose value while the land holds it. What are the advantages and disadvantages of that pattern for ordinary buyers?
4. Rank these by how much each would improve housing affordability in a high-price city: lower interest rates, looser zoning, a rent cap, a tax on empty homes. Explain your order.
5. Advise a young couple deciding whether to buy a home now or keep renting and invest their savings instead. What should they weigh?
