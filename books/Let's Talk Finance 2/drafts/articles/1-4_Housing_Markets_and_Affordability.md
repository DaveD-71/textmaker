## 1.4. Housing Markets and Affordability

### 1.4.1. Goal

Explain what drives house prices, why housing became less affordable across much of the rich world, and why homes lose value in some places.

### 1.4.2. Reading

Does a house always gain value? In much of the world the answer has been yes, but not everywhere, and not always.

House prices are set by demand meeting supply. Demand rises with population, incomes, and — importantly — cheap credit: when interest rates are low, buyers can borrow more, and prices go up. Supply is slow to respond, because land is limited and building is controlled by planning rules. When demand grows faster than supply, prices climb faster than incomes, and housing becomes less affordable.

That is what happened across much of the rich world after the 1990s. In New Zealand, real house prices more than doubled between 2000 and 2007 [1]. Then interest rates rose sharply in 2022, and several of these markets fell: New Zealand prices dropped about 14% from their 2021 peak, with the largest cities down around 20% [1].

Governments have tried different levers. Some cities have relaxed planning rules to allow more building: after Auckland loosened its zoning in 2016, research found the number of new homes approved each year roughly doubled, and rents there rose more slowly than in the rest of New Zealand [2]. Others have tried to cap rents directly. Berlin introduced a rent cap in 2020, but Germany's Federal Constitutional Court struck it down in 2021, ruling that the city had no power to set it [3].

Japan is the clearest exception to "houses always go up". There, the building and the land are valued separately, and the structure is treated as a wasting asset: the tax system assigns a wooden house a life of about 22 years, and older houses are often priced as if the building is worth almost nothing, with the value in the land. This is reinforced by heavy new construction — around 800,000 homes were started in the 2023 financial year despite a shrinking population [4] — and by light planning rules that make rebuilding easy. The result is a growing number of empty houses: Japan's 2023 survey counted about 9 million, a record 13.8% of the total stock [5].

### 1.4.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** affordability, house price-to-income ratio, housing supply, zoning, upzoning, rent control, depreciation, housing starts

**Recycled terms:** interest rate, mortgage, real (vs nominal), central bank

### 1.4.4. Reading Questions

1. Why do low interest rates tend to push house prices up?
2. Why is the supply of housing "slow to respond" to rising demand?
3. What happened to house prices in New Zealand after interest rates rose in 2022?
4. What was the effect of Auckland loosening its zoning rules in 2016?
5. Why does the reading say Japan is "the clearest exception to 'houses always go up'"?

### 1.4.5. Discussion Questions

1. Is a home better understood as an investment or as shelter? Does the answer change depending on the country?
2. Auckland allowed more building; Berlin tried to cap rents. Which approach is more likely to make housing affordable, and why?
3. In Japan, houses lose value while the land holds it. What are the advantages and disadvantages of that pattern for ordinary buyers?
4. Rank these by how much each would improve housing affordability in a high-price city: lower interest rates, looser zoning, a rent cap, a tax on empty homes. Explain your order.
5. Advise a young couple deciding whether to buy a home now or keep renting and invest their savings instead. What should they weigh?
