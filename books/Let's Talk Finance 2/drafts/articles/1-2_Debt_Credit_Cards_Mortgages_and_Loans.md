## 1.2. Debt: Credit Cards, Mortgages and Loans

### 1.2.1. Goal

Explain how consumer credit works, why the interest rate varies so much between loan types, and what over-indebtedness looks like.

### 1.2.2. Reading

In the United States in 2026, the average credit card charged about 21% a year in interest, while the average new home loan charged about 6.7% [1][2]. Both are loans. The difference in price comes mainly from security: a mortgage is backed by the house, which the lender can take and sell if payments stop, so the risk to the lender is lower. A credit card is backed by nothing, so the rate is far higher.

Interest is the price of borrowing, charged as a percentage of the amount still owed. On a credit card, unpaid interest is added to the balance and then charged interest itself the next month. This is compounding, and it is why a balance that is never fully paid off can grow quickly even without new spending.

Borrowing has been rising. US households owed US$18.8 trillion in total in mid-2026, including US$1.26 trillion on credit cards [3]. Some countries carry much heavier household debt than others: in South Korea it passed 2,000 trillion won in 2026, among the highest levels in the world relative to the size of the economy [4].

A newer form of borrowing is "buy now, pay later", which splits a purchase into a few interest-free instalments at the checkout. It grew fast with little regulation. In the year to May 2024, about one in five UK adults had used it, and the market had grown from under £0.1 billion in 2017 to more than £13 billion [5]. Regulators then moved in: from July 2026 these firms became regulated in the UK and must check that a customer can afford the repayments [5].

Japan has usually carried lighter household debt than the US or UK, and most Japanese cardholders pay their balance in full the next month rather than borrowing on it. The defining reform came in 2006, after years of problems with borrowers holding loans from many lenders at once: the maximum interest rate on unsecured consumer loans was cut to 20%, and total borrowing from non-bank lenders was capped at one-third of a person's annual income.

2. In your own words, how does compounding make a credit-card balance grow?

### 1.2.5. Discussion Questions

1. People sometimes describe a mortgage or a student loan as "good debt" and credit-card debt as "bad debt". Is that a useful distinction? Explain.
2. "Buy now, pay later" is interest-free if you pay on time. Does that make it safe? Give your reasons.
3. Japan capped consumer loan interest at 20% and limited total borrowing to one-third of income. What are the benefits and the drawbacks of rules like these?
4. Rank these four by how much financial trouble they signal: a large mortgage, a car loan, a credit-card balance carried every month, several "buy now, pay later" plans at once. Explain your order.
5. Advise a young adult who has just been offered their first credit card. What would you tell them to do and not do?
