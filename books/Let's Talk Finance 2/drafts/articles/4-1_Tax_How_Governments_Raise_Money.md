## 4.1. Tax: How Governments Raise Money

### 4.1.1. Goal

Set out the main sources of government revenue, how the mix varies between countries, and who actually bears each tax.

### 4.1.2. Reading

Where does government money actually come from? Almost every road, school, court and pension is paid for by tax, and the mix of taxes a country chooses decides who carries the cost.

The broadest measure is the tax-to-GDP ratio — total tax revenue as a share of the size of the economy. Across the OECD it averaged about 33.9% in 2023, ranging from about 17.7% in Mexico to about 43.8% in France. In a typical OECD country, social-security contributions and personal income tax each raise roughly a quarter of revenue. Goods-and-services taxes, including VAT, add about a fifth, corporate income tax about a tenth, and property taxes a few percent [1].

Countries at similar income levels choose very different structures. The United States has no federal VAT and leans heavily on personal income tax and payroll taxes, with sales taxes set by states at relatively low rates [2]. Most EU countries run high standard VAT rates that raise a large, stable share of revenue, usually the biggest single source after social contributions — neither design is more "normal" than the other.

A country's tax structure follows its revenue base and shifts when that base looks less secure. Gulf states long funded themselves from oil and charged residents almost nothing; the UAE introduced a 5% VAT in 2018 and a 9% federal corporate tax from June 2023, while still charging no personal income tax [3].

Tax is also now an area of international coordination. Around 140 countries agreed in 2021 to a 15% minimum effective tax rate on large multinationals' profits, so a company shifting profit to a very-low-tax country now has the shortfall "topped up" elsewhere. The rules took effect from 2024 in the EU, UK, Japan and South Korea, though the US moved to withdraw its support in 2025 [4]. Underneath all this sits tax incidence: the person or company that hands money to the government is often not the one who bears the cost. VAT is collected from businesses but paid by final consumers; corporate tax is legally paid by companies but its burden is shared, in disputed proportions, among shareholders, employees and customers.

Japan's tax story is dominated by one instrument and one problem. The instrument is the consumption tax, introduced at 3% in 1989 and raised in steps to 5% (1997), 8% (2014) and 10% (2019, with food kept at 8%) [5]. Every increase cost the governing party politically, yet the consumption tax is now Japan's largest single national tax, because it is the most reliable way to raise revenue from a population with a growing share of retirees. The problem is that an ageing society needs ever more spending on pensions and health care while the number of working-age taxpayers falls, so Japan also leans hard on social-security contributions levied on wages rather than on higher income-tax rates. Even so, tax does not cover what the government spends; the gap is filled by selling bonds, which is the subject of the next topic.

### 4.1.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** tax-to-GDP ratio; value-added tax (VAT) / consumption tax; social-security contributions; progressive / regressive tax; tax incidence; global minimum tax ("Pillar Two")

**Recycled terms:** bond; shareholder; employee

### 4.1.4. Reading Questions

1. What does the tax-to-GDP ratio measure?
2. How does the United States' tax structure differ from that of most EU countries?
3. Why did the United Arab Emirates introduce a VAT and a corporate tax?
4. What problem is the global minimum tax meant to solve?
5. Why is Japan's consumption tax now its largest single national tax, despite each increase being politically costly?

### 4.1.5. Discussion Questions

1. VAT takes a larger share of a low earner's income than a high earner's. Does that make it a bad tax, or is it justified by other qualities? Explain.
2. Countries at similar income levels tax very differently. What might explain those choices — history, politics, or something else?
3. The global minimum tax needs many countries to act together, and the US has stepped back. Can it work without full participation?
4. Compare taxing consumption (VAT) and taxing income. Which would you rely on more, and why?
5. You advise a government that must raise revenue equal to 2% of GDP. Recommend which taxes to raise or introduce, and explain who would bear the cost under your plan.
