## 3.2. Bubbles, Crashes and Manias

### 3.2.1. Goal

Show the recurring pattern behind asset bubbles across four centuries, and why the aftermath often matters more than the crash.

### 3.2.2. Reading

In the first week of February 1637, buyers at a tulip-bulb auction in Haarlem simply stopped bidding. Sellers lowered their prices, then lowered them again, and still no one bought. Within days the market for Dutch tulip bulbs — a single bulb had earlier been traded for a house and farmland — had collapsed [1]. Tulip mania is the oldest well-known asset bubble, and almost every element of it has recurred since.

A bubble forms when the price of an asset rises far above any sensible valuation. Three things carry it up: cheap or borrowed money, called leverage; herd behaviour, people buying because others are buying; and a belief that "this time is different," so the usual warnings no longer apply. This becomes a speculative mania, buying driven by expected further price rises rather than value. Economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff traced that "this time is different" belief through financial crises across 66 countries and eight centuries, finding it before nearly every one [2]. The crash comes when buyers stop believing the price will keep rising.

The 1929 Wall Street crash is the classic modern case. The Dow Jones Industrial Average peaked near 381 in September 1929, then fell for almost three years to a low of 41.22 in July 1932, a peak-to-trough decline of about 89%. It did not regain its 1929 level until November 1954, a quarter of a century later [3]. The US dot-com crash of 2000 and the 2008 global financial crisis followed the same shape: a boom built on new technology or easy credit, then a long repair. In each case the warning signs were visible beforehand and widely dismissed.

Japan's late-1980s bubble is one of the best-documented anywhere. The Nikkei 225 closed at 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989, lifted by cheap credit and soaring land values used as loan collateral [4], and took 34 years to reclaim that level, in February 2024 [4]. Property fell even harder: commercial land prices in six major cities dropped by roughly 87% from their peak and kept falling for 15 years, not turning up again until 2005 [5]. Banks were left holding loans backed by land worth a fraction of its former value, and the economy grew slowly for two decades.

The pattern reached new assets without changing. The cryptocurrency Luna had traded near US$120 in early April 2022; over three days in May, after confidence in its linked "stablecoin" broke, it fell from about US$80 to almost nothing [6][7]. The details differ each time — bulbs, shares, land, tokens — but the sequence does not: easy money and rising prices draw in more buyers, the belief that the rise cannot stop becomes general, and then it stops. What varies most is the aftermath, and the risk of a slow one grows with the length of the boom and the amount of borrowed money behind it — a few weeks for Luna, more than three decades for the Nikkei.

**New terms:** asset bubble, speculative mania, herd behaviour, leverage, "this time is different" thinking, peak-to-trough decline

### 3.2.4. Reading Questions

1. What happened at the Haarlem tulip auction in February 1637?
2. What three things does the reading say carry an asset bubble upward?
3. How long did it take the Dow to regain its 1929 level, and how long did it take the Nikkei 225 to regain its 1989 level?
4. Why were Japanese banks left in a weak position after the late-1980s bubble burst?
5. The reading says "the sequence does not" change. What sequence does it mean?

### 3.2.5. Discussion Questions

1. The reading says the belief "this time is different" appears before nearly every bubble. Why is that belief so hard to resist during a boom?
2. Bubbles have formed around very different assets: bulbs, shares, land, cryptocurrencies. What makes some assets more prone to bubbles than others?
3. The aftermath of a bubble ranged from weeks (Luna) to decades (the Nikkei). What determines how long the recovery takes?
4. Compare the 1929 crash with the 2022 Luna collapse. What is similar, and what is different?
5. Design three warning signs an investor could watch for to judge whether a rising market has become a bubble.
