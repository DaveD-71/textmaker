## 3.1. What Moves Stock Markets

### 3.1.1. Goal

Explain the main forces that move stock market indexes — earnings, interest rates, sentiment, and concentration in a few large companies.

### 3.1.2. Reading

Why did the market fall today? Financial news answers this every evening, usually by naming one cause: an interest-rate decision, a disappointing profit report, a change in mood. All three are real drivers, and a fourth has grown more important — how much of an index depends on just a few very large companies. A stock index is a single number tracking a basket of shares, weighted by market capitalisation, which is the total value of each company's shares. What moves the index is whatever moves the shares inside it.

The most durable driver is company earnings, the actual profits firms make. A share is ultimately a claim on a company's future profits, so a higher expected profit stream is worth more. In 2025 the S&P 500, the main US index, returned about 18%, and more than three-quarters of that came from higher earnings rather than investors paying more per dollar of profit [7]. When a large company's quarterly report misses expectations, its shares, and the index, can drop the same day. Earnings and interest rates together are what analysts call fundamentals.

Interest rates are the second driver. Higher rates make borrowing costlier for companies and make safe bonds a more attractive alternative to shares, so rate decisions move markets directly. On 18 December 2025 the European Central Bank kept its main rate at 2%, its fourth pause in a row [3]. Europe's Stoxx 600 index rose about 1% that session and ended 2025 up roughly 17% [4]. A US rate change reaches further still: when the Federal Reserve is expected to cut, money often flows toward emerging markets in search of higher returns, and a softer dollar adds to the effect.

The third driver is investor sentiment — collective confidence or fear. It is hard to measure, but the closest gauge is the VIX, an index of how much volatility options traders expect in the US market over the next month [2]. Often called the market's "fear gauge," it stays low when markets are calm and jumps when investors rush to buy protection during a sell-off. A calm market can turn volatile within hours if confidence breaks, with no change in earnings or rates.

The fourth driver is index concentration. By the end of 2025 the ten largest companies in the S&P 500 made up a record 40.7% of its value, roughly double their share a decade earlier, most of them tied to artificial intelligence [1]. When an index leans this heavily on a few firms, its daily direction can be set by one company's news. Japan shows that the usual rules can still surprise. On 16 June 2026 the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1%, the highest since 1995 [6]. Higher rates are supposed to weigh on shares, but the Nikkei 225 rose instead, passing 70,000 in intraday trading for the first time, because investors read the move as removing uncertainty rather than adding cost [5]. On any given day, the same news can push a market either way, depending on what investors already expected.

### 3.1.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** stock index, market capitalisation, fundamentals, index concentration, investor sentiment, volatility

**Recycled terms:** interest rate, central bank, bond, return

### 3.1.4. Reading Questions

1. What four things does the reading say move stock prices?
2. In 2025, did the S&P 500's gain come mainly from higher earnings or from investors paying more per dollar of profit?
3. Why do higher interest rates tend to push share prices down?
4. What does the VIX measure, and how does it behave in a calm market compared with a sell-off?
5. Why did the Nikkei 225 rise after the Bank of Japan raised interest rates in June 2026, when higher rates usually weigh on shares?

### 3.1.5. Discussion Questions

1. Financial news usually explains a market move with a single cause. Given the four drivers in the reading, how useful are those daily explanations?
2. When ten companies make up 40% of an index, is that index still a good measure of "the market"? Explain.
3. Sentiment can move prices for days without any change in earnings or rates. Should investors pay attention to sentiment, or ignore it? Give reasons.
4. Compare earnings and interest rates as drivers of stock prices. Which would you watch more closely, and why?
5. A friend in Japan asks whether a Bank of Japan rate rise is good or bad for their share investments. Advise them, using the reading.
