## 1.5. Scams, Fraud and Financial Self-Defence

### 1.5.1. Goal

Explain how modern financial fraud works, why reported losses understate the problem, and how countries differ in protecting victims.

### 1.5.2. Reading

People in the United States reported losing US$12.5 billion to fraud in 2024, about a quarter more than the year before. The largest single category was fake investments [1]. That figure counts only what was reported. Much fraud is never reported, out of embarrassment or because people do not know where to complain, so the real total is higher.

Fraud today is organised and industrial. Criminals use fake investment platforms, phishing messages that copy a real bank, impersonation calls, and "romance" approaches that build a relationship before asking for money. A growing type combines the two: a fake romance leads into a fake investment, sometimes in cryptocurrency, until the victim's savings are gone. A common method throughout is the authorised push payment scam. The victim is tricked into sending a bank transfer themselves, which makes it harder to reverse, because the payment was technically approved by the account holder. The United Nations has reported that much of this activity is run from large, guarded compounds in South-East Asia, often staffed by trafficked workers. It estimated that losses to victims across East and South-East Asia ran into tens of billions of US dollars in 2023 alone [5].

Countries protect victims very differently. From October 2024, the United Kingdom has required banks to reimburse most victims of authorised push payment fraud, up to £85,000 per claim, with the cost shared between the sending bank and the receiving bank [2]. In most other countries, including the United States and Japan, whether a victim gets any money back is far less certain. Some countermeasures do seem to help. In Australia, reported scam losses fell by about 26% in 2024 after coordinated action by government, banks and telecoms firms [3].

Japan has fought "special fraud" for two decades. These scams often begin with a phone call from someone posing as a relative in trouble, or as a police officer. Losses hit a record ¥72 billion in 2024, up nearly 60% in a year, and about 45% of victims were aged 75 or over [4]. A separate and newer category, investment and romance scams that start on social media, added a further ¥127 billion [4]. The response has focused on adding friction. Many banks suspend cash-machine transfers for older customers who have not used the function recently. Some cap daily withdrawals. Convenience-store staff are trained to question elderly customers who try to buy large numbers of prepaid cards.

Self-defence comes down to a few habits. Treat any unexpected contact about money as suspicious. Never move money because someone has pressured you to act quickly. And check any request through a phone number or app you found yourself, not one the other person gave you.

### 1.5.4. Reading Questions

1. Why does the reading say the US$12.5 billion figure understates the real scale of fraud?
2. What is an authorised push payment scam, and why is it hard to reverse?
3. How does the United Kingdom's approach to fraud victims differ from that of most other countries?
4. What happened to reported scam losses in Australia in 2024, and why?
5. What kinds of "friction" have Japanese banks and shops added to slow fraud down?

### 1.5.5. Discussion Questions

1. The UK now makes banks refund most transfer-fraud victims. What are the benefits of that rule, and what problems could it create?
2. Fraud in several countries targets elderly people in particular. Whose responsibility is it to protect them — the banks, the family, the government, or the individual? Explain.
3. Adding "friction" (delays, limits, warnings) reduces fraud but also annoys honest customers. Where should the balance sit?
4. Compare protecting people by refunding victims afterwards with protecting them by stopping the payment beforehand. Which is more effective?
5. Design a short checklist you would give an older relative to help them recognise and resist a scam call.
