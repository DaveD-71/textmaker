## 3.3. Commodities: Oil, Metals and Food

### 3.3.1. Goal

Explain why the prices of raw materials swing so sharply, and contrast a sudden supply shock with a slower, structural price story.

### 3.3.2. Reading

In March 2022, a bakery in Tokyo that had paid a steady price for flour for years received a new quote: about a third higher, with no promise it would hold. Its wheat came from abroad, and two weeks earlier Russia had invaded Ukraine. Together the two countries supplied roughly 30% of the world's wheat exports, and the price of that wheat had jumped on every market at once [2]. The bakery could not negotiate with the war; it could only pay the new world price or use less.

This is the defining feature of commodities — raw materials such as crude oil, wheat and industrial metals, traded in bulk and largely interchangeable between suppliers. Their prices are set globally, so a shock anywhere reaches buyers everywhere, and higher commodity prices feed quickly into inflation. Oil showed it in the same weeks: Brent crude, the main benchmark price for oil, rose above US$120 a barrel, its highest since 2008. On 31 March 2022 the United States released 180 million barrels from its Strategic Petroleum Reserve, the largest release since the reserve was created in 1975, to ease the shock [1]. A strategic reserve exists precisely so a government can add supply when a shock removes it.

The food side ran in parallel but was fixed differently. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization's Food Price Index, a basket of world cereal, oil, dairy, meat and sugar prices, hit an all-time high in March 2022, up almost 13% in a single month [2]. Rather than wait for high prices to draw out new supply, the UN and Türkiye brokered a protected shipping corridor in July 2022 that moved about 33 million tonnes of grain out of Ukraine's Black Sea ports over the following year [3]. The index then fell back as harvests and trade adjusted: a supply shock, unlike a slow price trend, can sometimes be reversed by a specific arrangement.

Metals show a different kind of price risk. In March 2022 the London Metal Exchange suspended nickel trading and later cancelled about US$12 billion of trades after the price doubled within hours, driven by supply fears and a large losing bet by one producer [4]. Trades that dealers thought were final were undone — a reminder that even a commodity exchange can stop working under stress. For some metals the risk is one government's policy: China accounts for roughly 60% of the world's rare-earth mining and about 90% of its processing, and from 2023 it placed export licensing controls on a widening list of critical minerals [5].

Japan feels all of this more sharply than most because it produces almost none of it. It imports nearly all its oil, gas and coal, and its food self-sufficiency rate on a calorie basis was 38% in fiscal 2023, below the government's 45% target [6]. Japan is close to a pure price-taker: too small a share of any of these markets to move the price, so it simply pays the going rate, and in 2022 the yen was also weakening, so the cost of each imported cargo rose twice over and cut into households' purchasing power. The usual defences are the ones every importer uses: long-term contracts, part-ownership of overseas fields, and large stockpiles — Japan holds well over 200 days of oil reserves. Rice is the revealing exception. Japan grows almost all its own rice, yet a domestic shortage still pushed retail prices up sharply in 2024. Producing something yourself lowers your exposure to world prices; it does not remove price risk.

### 3.3.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** commodity, benchmark price, supply shock, strategic reserve, price-taker, self-sufficiency rate

**Recycled terms:** risk, inflation, purchasing power

### 3.3.4. Reading Questions

1. Why did the Tokyo bakery's flour price jump in March 2022?
2. What does the reading say is the "defining feature" of commodities?
3. How was the 2022 food-price shock partly reversed?
4. Why does Japan feel commodity-price swings more sharply than most countries?
5. Why does the reading call rice "the revealing exception"?

### 3.3.5. Discussion Questions

1. Governments hold strategic reserves of oil and grain. Should every import-dependent country do this, given the cost of storage? Explain.
2. For some metals, the main price risk is one country's export policy rather than weather or war. What can other countries do about that kind of risk?
3. The reading says producing something yourself lowers exposure to world prices but does not remove price risk. Do you agree? Use the rice example.
4. Compare an oil supply shock with the slow rise in copper demand. Which is harder for a government to manage, and why?
5. Advise a food importer on three steps it could take now to reduce the impact of a future commodity-price shock.
