## 4.5. The Future of Money

### 4.5.1. Goal

Trace how money's form has changed over time and give a sober account of what is actually arriving next.

### 4.5.2. Reading

In Sweden, the share of people who used cash for their most recent purchase fell from about 40% in 2010 to about 8% in 2022 [1]. In Japan and Germany, cash is still used for a large share of everyday purchases. Same decade, opposite directions. "Money" has changed form many times before, and it is changing again.

Its form has moved in stages: commodity money, then minted coins, then paper notes — first backed by gold or silver, later "fiat" money that has value only because a government declares it legal tender and people trust it — then book-entry bank deposits, payment cards, and mobile and QR-code payments. Each step kept the unit of account, the krona or the yen, and changed only how value is stored and moved.

One point matters before looking ahead: most money is already digital and private. In modern economies the large majority of the money supply is commercial-bank deposits — money created when a bank lends, existing only as entries in a ledger — not central-bank cash [3]. So "the future of money" is mostly a question about what backs and moves those deposits.

The newest forms sit on top of that. Privately issued tokens pegged to a national currency, over 90% of them to the US dollar, reached a combined value of about US$250 billion by mid-2025; reported transfer volume hit roughly US$33 trillion in 2025, though much of that is automated trading rather than payments [4]. Their real use is growing for cross-border transfers and for dollar access in high-inflation economies. Banks and central banks are separately piloting "tokenised deposits" on shared programmable ledgers, so that payment and settlement happen together, instantly, around the clock [5]. Central-bank digital currencies — a digital form of public central-bank money — are being tested in many countries, with China and India furthest along in mobile payments, but most remain at the pilot stage.

Sweden, having led the world toward cashlessness, is now legislating to protect cash. In November 2024 the authorities sent every household a brochure advising people to keep at least a week's worth of cash, around SEK 1,000 per adult, and the central bank backed a proposed legal duty for shops selling essential goods to accept it [2]. Cash is now treated as resilience infrastructure — a backup if the electronic system fails — rather than a relic.

Japan is the useful counter-example to the "cash is dying" story. QR-code wallets and contactless cards have spread quickly, yet Japan's demand for physical currency is around 19–20% of GDP, the highest of any economy the Bank for International Settlements tracks, and cash is still used in more than four in ten retail purchases [6]. Several things hold it in place: with interest rates near zero for decades there has been no cost to keeping money as cash rather than in a bank; the population is old and used to tangible money; and crime and counterfeiting are rare, so cash feels safe. Japan issued its first redesigned banknotes in twenty years in July 2024, and only very recently has the total amount of cash in circulation begun to fall. Whether a society goes cashless, Japan shows, is not settled by the technology being available; it depends on interest rates, demographics, trust and habit — which is why the same decade produced near-cashless Sweden and cash-heavy Japan.

### 4.5.4. Reading Questions

1. How did cash use in Sweden change between 2010 and 2022?
2. What does the reading mean by saying "most money is already digital and private"?
3. Why does the reading say the reported US$33 trillion stablecoin transfer figure overstates payment use?
4. What did Sweden do in November 2024, and how is cash now described there?
5. What four things does the reading say hold cash in place in Japan?

### 4.5.5. Discussion Questions

1. Sweden went furthest toward cashlessness and is now protecting cash. What does that reversal suggest about how fast countries should move?
2. If most money is already private bank deposits, how much would a central-bank digital currency actually change for an ordinary user?
3. Stablecoins are widely used for trading but less so for everyday payments. What would have to change for that to shift?
4. Compare Sweden and Japan. Which country's approach to the future of money looks wiser, and why?
5. You advise a central bank deciding whether to issue a digital currency. List the strongest argument for and against, and recommend a decision.
