# 2 Introducing a Colleague

Procedure & language

> <u>Situation: At an international conference</u>
>
> Tomoya Ishii is with a colleague at ISRA, Ayako Mori. During a break between meetings they happen to meet Ted Marks from Safety Advisory Canada. Mr. Ishii greets Mr. Marks then introduces Ms Mori to him.

- Standard Procedure for Introducing a Colleague

> Ishii Hello, Mr. Marks. It’s nice to see you again (Holds out hand to shake).
>
> Marks (Shaking hands) Oh, hello Mr. Ishii. It’s nice to see you again, too.
>
> Ishii Mr. Marks, I’d like to introduce my colleague, Ayako Mori. She works with me at IRSA in the consumer products department. Ms Mori, this is Ted Marks. He’s head of the editorial department at Safety Advisory Canada.
>
> Marks I’m pleased to meet you, Ms Mori (Holds out her hand to shake).
>
> Mori (Shaking hands) I’m pleased to meet you, too, Mr. Marks. Here is my card.
>
> Marks Thank you. And here’s mine.
>
> Mori Thank you.
>
> Marks I was just going for a cup of coffee. Would you like to join me?
>
> Ishii Yes, that would be very nice. Thank you.

<u>Business Protocol</u>

1.  Introducing others

> The situation above illustrates the standard western protocol for introductions. This is the pattern to follow and the language to use.
>
> In the situation above, Mr. Ishii knows both Ms Mori and Mr. Marks so he <u>must</u> do the introductions. It would be extremely rude if they had to introduce themselves.

2.  Showing respect in introductions

> During introductions, look at and speak to the person to whom you want to show respect. For example: To introduce your colleague to a counterparty, show respect to the counterparty by looking at and speaking to him or her.

- The junior/senior rule for introductions

> In business, the rule for making introductions is to introduce the junior person to the senior person:

- Speak to the senior person and introduce the junior person.

- Counterparties and guests to the office are senior to your colleagues; directors are senior to staff.

3.  Courtesy notes

<!-- -->

1.  Notice that when Ms Mori greets Mr. Marks she does not repeat her own name. Repeating her name would be rude to Mr. Ishii because he would be ignoring Mr. Ishii’s introduction. It would also be embarassing for Mr. Marks.

2.  Notice also that Ms Mori says Mr. Marks’ name. This is a courtesy to Mr. Marks and it helps Ms Mori to remember his name.

> <u>Expressions for Introductions</u>

1.  **Common expressions**

- I’d like to introduce my colleague, Ayako Mori.

- Let me introduce my colleague. This is Ayako Mori.

- Let me introduce Ayako Mori. She works with me at ISRA.

2.  **Introducing a colleague to someone senior (counterparty or director)**

- Ms Wilson, <u>allow me to introduce</u> my colleague, Ayako Mori.

- Ms Wilson, <u>may I introduce</u> my colleague, Ayako Mori?

3.  **Introducing a counterparty to your director or someone senior**

- … allow me introduce you to …

- … let me introduce you to …

> For example: Mr. Marks, <u>allow me introduce you to</u> my director, Ms Yamamoto. Ms Yamamoto is the director of the consumer products department.
>
> <u>Business Card Etiquette</u>
>
> When juniors meet seniors, juniors offer business cards but seniors might not always offer a business card:

- If the introduction was just a courtesy, the senior person would probably not offer his/her card. However, if the introduction was going to lead to meetings in the future then the senior person would probably offer his/her card.

> <u>Expressions to Avoid</u>

1.  ***His/her name is*…**

*This is my colleague. Her name is Ayako Mori* is correct English, however, it is not suitable for introductions.

It is more polite to say, *I’d like to introduce my colleague, Ayako Mori.*

2.  ***I’d like you to meet…***

*I’d like you to meet…* is not suitable for business because it confuses the positions of seniority. It can be used in non-business social occasions amongst friends.

*Bob, I’d like you to meet my friend Hiroshi.*
