# Unit 1: Why Presentations Matter

## Essentials

### What a speech is for

Every speech has one job: change what the audience thinks, feels, or does. Before you write a word, answer this: what do you want different by the end?

Three things decide your message. Not you alone.

1. **You** — your expertise, your goal.
2. **Your audience** — what they need, what they already know.
3. **Your occasion** — why this talk, why now.

Ignore any one of the three and the speech falls flat.

### Are all speeches the same? No.

A toast is not a sales pitch. A team update is not a conference keynote. Same skills, different shape. Check the occasion before you plan the content.

### The 6 Keys

Six things make a presentation work. Learn the list. Use it every time you prepare.

1. **Purpose** — what change you want.
2. **Content** — what's true and useful, for this audience.
3. **Structure** — the order it comes in.
4. **Language** — words that work when heard, not just read.
5. **Delivery** — how you say it — voice, body, pace.
6. **Visual Aids** — slides or props that support you, not replace you.

Weak spot in any one key sinks the whole talk. Check all six, every time.

### Online changes two of the keys

Purpose and audience analysis shift online. A muted, half-watching audience on a screen is not the same room as people sitting in front of you.

- In person: you read faces, adjust live.
- Online: you can't. Assume shorter attention, more distraction. Plan for it before you start.

Ask "who is this for, in person or on a screen" every time. Never assume the same plan works both ways.

### Activity: Presentation history checklist

Five minutes. Grab a pen.

1. List every presentation you've given — work, school, anywhere. Three is enough if that's all you've got.
2. Next to each, write one thing that worked.
3. Next to each, write one thing that didn't.
4. Circle the pattern — the thing that shows up more than once.
5. Write one goal for this course, one sentence: "By the end, I want to ___."

Keep this page. You'll check it again later.

# Unit 2: Structure of a Speech (+ Theme vs. Message)

## Essentials

### The Three-Part Structure

Every speech has three parts. Use them every time.

1. **Introduction** — Say what you're going to say.
2. **Body** — Say it.
3. **Conclusion** — Say what you said.

That's the whole shape. Don't skip a part. Don't reorder it.

**Introduction:** State your message in the first 15 seconds. Preview your main points. Three points, max.

**Body:** Cover each point in order. One point, then the next. Signal the switch: "First... Next... Finally..."

**Conclusion:** Restate your message. Summarize your points in one line each. Stop talking.

### Theme vs. Message

Two different things. Don't confuse them.

- **Theme** = your topic. "Remote work." "Our Q3 results."
- **Message** = your opinion on the topic. It has to take a position.

A theme alone isn't enough. "I'm going to talk about remote work" tells the audience nothing. Add a message: "Remote work is cutting our turnover — we should expand it." Now they know what you actually think.

Test your message with one question: does it contain an opinion or a proposal? If not, rewrite it. "Sales figures for Q3" is a theme. "Q3 sales prove we need to cut the East region budget" is a message.

### Speaking on Video

Online audiences drop off fast. Adjust your structure:

- Put your message in the first sentence. Don't build up to it.
- Cut your body to two points, not three.
- Move faster between points. No long pauses.

### Activity: Critique, Then Draft

**Step 1 — Critique.** Theme: "Employee training budgets." Pick the strongest message from these three:

- A) "Training budgets are a topic worth discussing."
- B) "We spent money on training this year."
- C) "We should double the training budget — it cut our error rate by 20%."

Answer: C. It takes a position and backs it with a reason. A and B are just restated themes — no opinion, no proposal.

**Step 2 — Draft.** Using the self-introduction model in the Self-Introduction Models appendix, write your own three-line outline:

1. Introduction line — your message in one sentence.
2. Body line — your one supporting point.
3. Conclusion line — restate your message.

Keep it to three lines. Don't write full paragraphs.

# Unit 3: Flag Expressions

## Essentials

### What a Flag Expression Is

A Flag Expression is a phrase that marks a step in your speech. Think of a tour guide holding up a flag. The group follows the flag. No flag, no group — people get lost.

Flag Expressions do the same job for a listener. Use them at every step. Memorize the list below.

### The Flag Expression Table

**Part 1: Introduction**

| Step | Say This |
|---|---|
| Thank the chair | "Thank you for the introduction, M. Chair." |
| Greet the room | "Good evening, everybody." |
| Give your theme | "Today, I am going to tell you about..." |
| Preview your points | "I have three main points. First... Second... Third..." |

**Part 2: Body**

| Step | Say This |
|---|---|
| Start point one | "Let me begin with my first point." |
| Start point two | "Now, I'll move on to my second point." |
| Start point three | "Finally, I will tell you my third point." |

**Part 3: Conclusion**

| Step | Say This |
|---|---|
| Signal the review | "In conclusion, let me review my main points." |
| Review each point | "First... Then... Finally, I talked about..." |
| State your message | "I think that..." |
| Close | "Thank you. And now, I will be happy to answer your questions." |

### Three Rules — Don't Break These

1. **"First / Second / Third" is for the Introduction and Conclusion only.** In the Body, use transition words instead: "Now," "Next," "Finally." Never count points in the Body.
2. **"At first" does not mean "firstly."** "At first" signals a change — something was one way, then it changed. "At first it was hard, but then it got easier." Don't use it to introduce point one.
3. **"Let's" is informal only.** Fine for "Let's take a break." Not for a formal proposal. Say "I think we need to..." instead.

Drop "Mr. Chairman." Say "M. Chair" or "M. Chairperson." Works for anyone.

### Activity: Flag Expression Drill

Fill in each blank with the correct Flag Expression from the table.

1. "___, I am going to tell you about our new onboarding process." *(give your theme)*
2. "I have two main points. ___, I will cover the timeline. ___, I will cover the cost." *(Introduction — counting)*
3. "Let me begin with my first point. ___, I'll move on to my second point." *(Body — transition, not counting)*
4. "In conclusion, let me review my main points. ___, I talked about the timeline. ___, I talked about the cost." *(Conclusion — counting)*
5. "___ improve our onboarding process." Fix this. It's a formal proposal — don't use "Let's."

**Answers:** 1. Today 2. First / Second 3. Now 4. First / Finally 5. "I think we need to improve our onboarding process."

Check your answers against the table. Get every one right before you move on.

# Unit 4: Delivery Skills (incl. webcam/online basics)

## Essentials

### Eye Contact

Pick three or four people around the room. Look at each one for a full sentence, not a glance. Rotate slowly. Never scan the whole crowd like a lighthouse.

On webcam: look at the camera lens, not the screen. The lens is your eye contact. Put a small sticky arrow next to it if you keep forgetting.

### Voice

Slow down. Most people rush when nervous.

Drop your volume at the end of a sentence and you sound unsure. Keep your pitch steady through the last word.

Pause after your key point. Silence is not dead air — it's emphasis.

### Gestures

Keep your hands between your waist and your chest. That's your gesture zone.

Use a gesture to land a number or a contrast. Don't wave your hands the whole time.

On webcam, keep gestures smaller and closer to your body — the frame is tight, and big gestures clip out of shot.

### Posture

Stand with feet hip-width apart. Weight even on both feet.

Don't lock your knees. Don't rock side to side.

On webcam, sit tall and lean in slightly. Slouching reads worse on camera than in a room.

### Notes

Use cue cards, not full script pages. One card per point, key words only.

Glance down, then look back up before you speak. Never talk to your notes.

### Clothing

Wear what fits the room, not a fixed uniform. A suit is not required — match your audience and the occasion, then go slightly smarter.

Check for camera issues before you go on webcam: no busy patterns, no logos that distract, solid colors read best.

### Platform Controls (Webcam/Online)

Before you start:

1. Mute on entry, unmute when you speak.
2. Test your camera and audio device — don't assume they're right.
3. Know where your screen-share button is before you need it.
4. Close every tab you don't need. One less thing to accidentally share.

### Screen-Share Setup

Open your slides before the call starts. Don't fumble for the file live.

Share the window, not your whole desktop, unless you need to.

Turn off notifications. A message popping up mid-share kills your credibility.

### Recording and Backup

Record if you can — you can't fix what you don't review.

Have a backup: a phone hotspot, a PDF of your slides, a second device. If the platform fails, you keep going.

### Activity: Peer Observation + Webcam Self-Review

Do this now, not later.

1. Grab your Unit 2 draft.
2. Partner up.
3. Deliver 60 seconds of your draft. Your partner checks off three things only: eye contact, voice, posture.
4. Swap. Your partner delivers, you check.
5. Now record yourself on webcam for 60 seconds of the same material.
6. Watch it back. Check the same three things against yourself.

No skipping the playback. Watching yourself is the part that actually changes what you do next time.

# Unit 5: The Logic Tree

## Essentials

### The Logic Tree

One tool. Five parts. Plan any speech with it.

The Logic Tree is a picture of your speech before you write it. It works for any topic, any length, any medium — in person or on a screen doesn't change it.

The five parts, bottom to top:

1. **Roots** — your prep. Research, audience read, purpose.
2. **Trunk** — your theme. One line. The core idea holding everything up.
3. **Branches** — your main points. Three is the right number. Fewer feels thin. More gets lost.
4. **Leaves** — your support. Numbers, examples, quotes. Each branch needs its own leaves.
5. **Fruit** — your message. What the audience walks away holding.

Roots feed the Trunk. The Trunk grows Branches. Branches grow Leaves. Leaves produce Fruit. Skip a level and the tree falls over.

### Blank template

Copy this before you plan your next talk.

```
FRUIT (the one takeaway):

BRANCH 1:              BRANCH 2:              BRANCH 3:
  Leaf —                 Leaf —                  Leaf —
  Leaf —                 Leaf —                  Leaf —

TRUNK (one-line theme):

ROOTS (prep notes — audience, purpose, research):
```

### Worked example: a product launch

Topic: launching a new budgeting app to a room of small-business owners.

**Roots.** Audience runs small businesses, hates spreadsheets, wants speed. Purpose: get them to sign up for the free trial.

**Trunk.** "This app gives you your evenings back."

**Branch 1 — It's fast.**
- Leaf: Setup takes four minutes.
- Leaf: Syncs bank data automatically, no manual entry.

**Branch 2 — It's accurate.**
- Leaf: Built by accountants, not just developers.
- Leaf: Catches errors spreadsheets miss — 30% fewer mistakes in beta testing.

**Branch 3 — It's affordable.**
- Leaf: $12 a month, no contract.
- Leaf: Cheaper than one hour of a bookkeeper's time.

**Fruit.** "Try it free for 30 days. Sign up before you leave the room."

Notice the shape: one theme, three branches, each branch loaded with its own proof. That's the whole tree.

### Activity: Build your own tree

Fifteen minutes. Pick a topic you'd actually present.

1. Look at the Logic Tree Worked Models appendix. Two more filled-in trees there — copy the shape, not the content.
2. Use the template below. It's pre-labeled — just fill it in.

```
FRUIT (the one takeaway):

BRANCH 1: [Main point 1]        BRANCH 2: [Main point 2]        BRANCH 3: [Main point 3]
  Leaf —                          Leaf —                           Leaf —
  Leaf —                          Leaf —                           Leaf —

TRUNK (one-line theme):

ROOTS (audience, purpose, research):
```

3. Fill Roots first, Fruit last. Work bottom to top.
4. Minimum three branches. Not two. Not one big one.
5. Every branch needs at least two leaves. No leaves, no proof.

Keep this tree. You'll build your outline straight from it.

# Unit 6: Slide Design (Visual Aids)

## Essentials

### Slides support you. They don't replace you.

A slide is a prop, not a script. If the audience is reading, they're not listening to you. Fix that with two rules.

### Rule of 1

One idea per slide. Nothing else.

If you have two ideas, make two slides. Never cram them together to save space.

### Rule of 7

Max 7 lines per slide. Max 7 words per line.

Over that limit, the slide becomes a reading test. Cut it down or split it into more slides.

### The 4-part structure

Every deck follows the same shape:

1. **Title** — what the talk is.
2. **Index** — what you'll cover, in order.
3. **Content** — one slide per idea, following Rule of 1 and Rule of 7.
4. **Conclusion** — the one thing to remember.

Build your deck in this order. Don't skip the index — it tells the audience where they are.

### Keep it readable for everyone

Some people in your audience are colorblind. Red-and-green contrasts often disappear for them completely.

Use these safe combos instead: dark text on light background, or light text on dark background. Never rely on color alone to make a point — add a label or a shape too.

### Check it on a small screen

Your slide might be shared on a laptop screen, not projected on a wall. Before you present, shrink the slide to thumbnail size. Can you still read it? If not, cut words or make the font bigger.

### Activity: Fix the bad slide

You'll get one slide packed with dense text, poor contrast, and no clear structure.

Redesign it using this checklist:

1. Does it break one idea into one slide? If not, split it.
2. Does it pass the Rule of 7? Count the lines. Count the words per line.
3. Does the text pass the contrast check? Dark on light, or light on dark — not red on green.
4. Shrink it to thumbnail size. Still readable? If not, fix it.

Redesign the slide. Then compare your version against the checklist one more time before you move on.

# Unit 7: Chairing a Session

### Chairing a Session

Chairing is a job with three parts: introduce, manage Q&A, close. Learn the phrases for each. Use them in order.

**1. Introducing the speaker**

Say these four things, in this order:
- Name.
- Their role or credential — one line only.
- Talk title or topic.
- Handover line: "Please welcome [name]" or "Over to you, [name]."

Don't summarize the talk. Don't add your own opinion. Hand over and sit down.

**2. Managing Q&A**

Use this fixed script:
- Open it: "We'll now move to questions" or "What questions do you have for [name]?"
- Point, don't guess: "Yes, the person in the blue jacket" or use their name if you know it.
- Repeat the question if the room is large: "The question was..." This confirms you heard it right and lets everyone hear it.
- Cut off a rambling question: "So your question is...?" Turn it into one sentence, then hand to the speaker.
- Time check, one question before the end: "We have time for one more question."
- No questions come: don't panic. Have one ready yourself: "Can I ask you about...?"

**3. Online: hands and chat**

Same job, different tools. Use these lines:
- "Use the raise-hand button and I'll bring you in." Say this once, early.
- Unmute them yourself if the platform allows it: "I've unmuted you, go ahead."
- Chat questions: read them out loud before the speaker answers. "There's a question in the chat from [name]: ..."
- Stacking: if three hands go up at once, say so. "I've got three hands up, we'll take them in order."
- Always mute background noise if a questioner forgets. Say it plainly: "You might be on mute" or just mute/unmute for them if you have host controls.

**4. Closing**

Three lines, every time:
- Thank the speaker by name: "Thank you, [name], for that."
- Thank the room: "Thanks everyone for your questions."
- Close the session: "That brings us to the end of this session" or point to what's next: "Next up is..."

Don't add a long summary. Say your three lines and move on.

### Activity: Round-Robin Chair

Groups of three. Roles: chairperson, speaker, questioner. Rotate every round so each person chairs once.

- Use the Unit 3 phrase banks — flag expressions for the speaker's mini-talk, chairing phrases for the chair.
- Round 1: speaker gives a 60-second mini-talk. Chairperson introduces the speaker using the four-part intro above.
- Round 2: questioner asks one question. Chairperson manages it: point, repeat if needed, hand to speaker.
- Rotate roles. Repeat until everyone has chaired once.

Chair's checklist for each round: intro said, one question managed, done. That's the whole job at this level.

# Unit 8: Capstone Practice Activity (Timed Impromptu Circuit)

This is the drill. No new theory. You use what you've already got: the Logic Tree (Unit 5) to structure fast, and Chairing (Unit 7) to run the room.

**Format: Short Circuit — one round, ~10 minutes per learner.**

Run it in this order.

1. **Draw a topic.** Blind pick from the topic list or jar. No swaps.
2. **Prepare — 2 minutes, alone.** Use the Logic Tree. Trunk: your one main claim. Branches: two or three supporting points. Leaves: one example per branch. Write nothing but the tree. No full sentences.
3. **Present — 2 minutes.** Stand up. Speak from the tree, not from notes. Trunk first, then branches in order.
4. **Q&A — 2 minutes.** Chair opens the floor. Take two questions minimum. Answer each with a point, then stop. Don't ramble to fill time.
5. **Feedback — 3 minutes.** Chair runs it: one thing that worked, one thing to change. Speaker listens. No defending, no explaining. Just take it.
6. **Rotate the chair.** Next learner chairs the next round.

**Chair's job (Unit 7 skill in action):** keep time, call on questioners, cut off anyone running long, run the feedback round in order — strength, then fix.

**Rules for the room:**
- One speaker at a time. No side comments during the talk.
- Chair enforces time out loud: "30 seconds left."
- Feedback is specific. "Good energy" is not feedback. "You looked at three people, not the room" is.

Done. Next learner draws.

# Appendix: Self-Introduction Models

## Model 1: Business Context

*Setting: A new operations lead introduces herself at the first all-hands meeting after joining a mid-sized logistics company.*

**Introduction**

Good morning, everyone. I'm Elena Vasquez, and I'm joining the company as your new Head of Operations. Before I tell you about my background, I want to tell you what I actually believe, because I think it's more useful than a list of past job titles: I believe our biggest opportunity right now isn't cutting costs — it's cutting delay. Over the next few minutes, I'll walk you through where that belief comes from, what it looked like in practice at my last company, and what it means for how we'll work together over the coming months.

**Body**

My first point is about where I've come from. I spent the last six years in operations roles at two different companies — a regional freight carrier and, most recently, a fulfillment company where I ran the west-coast distribution network. In both places, I noticed the same pattern: the teams weren't short on effort or talent, but small delays kept compounding into large ones. A shipment held an extra half-day at one node would ripple into missed windows three or four steps down the chain.

My second point is what we did about it there, because I don't want this to sound like an abstract theory. We ran a project focused entirely on identifying the three or four handoff points where delay was most likely to start, and we redesigned those specific points rather than trying to overhaul the whole system at once. Within eight months, our on-time delivery rate improved by eighteen percent, without adding headcount or budget — the gains came from fixing where the friction actually was, not from working harder everywhere.

My third point is what this means here. I've spent my first two weeks mostly listening — sitting in on your team meetings, walking the floor, asking questions rather than proposing answers. What I've heard so far tells me we likely have a similar pattern: strong people, real effort, and a handful of specific points where delay is quietly building up. I don't have the answers yet, and I want to be upfront about that. What I do have is a process for finding them, and I'd like your help running it.

**Conclusion**

So to bring this back to where I started: I believe the fastest way to improve how we operate isn't a sweeping overhaul, it's finding the handful of places where delay starts and fixing those first. I've done this before, it worked, and I think the same approach applies here. Over the next month, I'll be reaching out to many of you directly to learn more about where you see friction in your own part of the process. Thank you for the warm welcome, and I'm looking forward to working with all of you.

# Appendix: Self-Introduction Models

## Model 2: Government / Public-Sector Context

*Setting: A newly appointed director of a municipal public-health division introduces herself at a community town hall meeting.*

**Introduction**

Good evening, and thank you all for coming out tonight. My name is Amara Osei, and I've recently been appointed Director of the Public Health Division for our city. I want to start with what I think matters most, rather than working up to it: I believe our health services are currently too hard to find, and that fixing that is more urgent, right now, than adding new programs. In the next few minutes, I'll explain why I've come to that conclusion, share what I've seen in communities that solved this well, and outline what I'd like to do here over the coming year.

**Body**

My first point concerns what I found when I reviewed how residents actually access our services. Over the past month, I asked our team to trace, step by step, what a resident has to do to find a vaccination clinic, apply for assistance, or reach a caseworker. In several cases, the information existed, but it was scattered across four or five different offices and two outdated web pages, with no single, clear starting point. We are not short on services. We are short on a clear front door to them.

My second point is drawn from what other municipalities have done about a similar problem. Two cities of comparable size to ours consolidated their public health information into a single access point — one phone line, one website, one intake process — and both reported meaningful increases in service usage within the first year, particularly among residents who had previously found the system too confusing to navigate. This wasn't a matter of spending significantly more money; it was a matter of organizing what already existed so people could actually find it.

My third point is what I'd like to propose here. Over my first ninety days, I intend to lead an effort to build a single, clearly signposted access point for our public health services — one number to call, one place to start, regardless of which specific service a resident ultimately needs. I want to be candid that this will take real coordination across departments that don't always work closely together, and I don't expect it to be simple. But I believe it is the single highest-value change available to us right now, more valuable than any new program we could introduce on top of a system that's still hard to navigate.

**Conclusion**

To return to where I began: I believe our most urgent task isn't adding new services, it's making the services we already provide genuinely easy to find and use. Cities that have taken this on have seen real results, and I believe we can do the same here. I'll be holding office hours and community sessions over the next several weeks to hear directly from you about where our current system is hardest to navigate, and I'd welcome your input. Thank you again for being here tonight.

# Appendix: Logic Tree Worked Models

Two more filled-in trees. Study the shape before you build your own.

## Model 1: Business Context

Topic: pitching a remote-work stipend to leadership as a new employee benefit.

**Roots.** Audience is the executive team. They care about retention and cost, not perks for their own sake. Purpose: get approval to pilot the stipend for one quarter.

**Trunk.** "A $75 monthly stipend will cut our turnover costs by more than it costs us."

**Branch 1 — It solves a real problem.**
- Leaf: Exit interviews cite home-office costs in 40% of resignations this year.
- Leaf: Three of our five open roles have been vacant over 60 days.

**Branch 2 — It's cheap next to the alternative.**
- Leaf: Stipend costs $900 per employee per year.
- Leaf: Replacing one mid-level hire costs about $18,000 in recruiting and lost productivity.

**Branch 3 — It's easy to run.**
- Leaf: Paid through existing payroll software, no new vendor.
- Leaf: Other teams in our industry already do this — it's not experimental.

**Fruit.** "Approve a one-quarter pilot with our engineering team. Review the retention numbers in 90 days."

## Model 2: Government / Public-Sector Context

Topic: proposing a pedestrian-safety upgrade for a dangerous intersection, to city council.

**Roots.** Audience is council members and nearby residents. They care about safety and cost to taxpayers. Purpose: get the intersection added to next year's capital budget.

**Trunk.** "One intersection is causing most of our pedestrian injuries — fix that one first."

**Branch 1 — The danger is concentrated, not spread out.**
- Leaf: 6 pedestrian collisions at Fifth and Main in the last two years — more than any other intersection in the district.
- Leaf: A school and a senior center both sit within two blocks of it.

**Branch 2 — The fix is proven elsewhere.**
- Leaf: A raised crosswalk and shorter crossing distance cut injuries 50% at a similar intersection three years ago.
- Leaf: Design already used in two other cities in this state.

**Branch 3 — The cost fits inside the existing budget.**
- Leaf: Estimated cost is $140,000, fully covered by unused state safety-grant funds.
- Leaf: No new tax increase required.

**Fruit.** "Add this intersection to next year's capital plan. Construction can begin next summer."

Same shape both times: one trunk, three branches, two-plus leaves each, one fruit. Build yours the same way.
