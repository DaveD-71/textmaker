# Standard Tier Style Guide — "Presentation Skills" Textbook

## 1. Voice Description

Standard is the textbook's neutral, expository default — the register a reader would call "a well-edited how-to book," not a coach's pep talk and not a terse checklist. Sentences average 15–22 words and vary in length: a short sentence lands a point, a longer one supplies the reasoning behind it. Formality is moderate-professional — second person ("you") is fine, contractions are used sparingly, and the tone is that of a knowledgeable colleague, not a peer or an authority figure. Every instruction is framed with a brief rationale before or attached to the directive itself — the pattern is *why → what to do*, typically in one to two sentences, never a standalone imperative with no context and never a paragraph of justification before getting to the point. Frameworks and techniques are named plainly and introduced with a one-line functional definition (what it does, not its history or its inventor). Second person imperatives ("try," "notice," "aim for") are standard for the instructional core, but they are earned by the preceding rationale rather than issued cold.

## 2. Sample Sentences

**On eye contact:**
> Eye contact signals that you're speaking to someone rather than at them, so rotate your gaze slowly across three or four points in the room rather than fixing on one friendly face. Holding each point for a full thought — three to five seconds — reads as natural; darting between faces reads as nervous.

**Introducing a framework:**
> The "Rule of Three" holds that audiences retain grouped information better than long lists, so structuring your key points into three clusters makes them easier to recall after the talk ends. Before you draft your slides, try sorting your content into three buckets and see whether a natural hierarchy emerges.

**On managing nerves before a talk:**
> Physical tension before a presentation is a normal stress response, not a sign that something is wrong, so trying to eliminate it entirely is the wrong goal. Instead, channel it: a short walk or a few controlled breaths before you go on will lower the tension without dulling your energy.

## 3. Avoid List

- **Bare imperatives with no rationale** ("Make eye contact. Stand up straight.") — that's Essentials register; Standard always earns the instruction with a reason first.
- **Hedging stacks** ("you might perhaps want to consider possibly trying") — state the guidance plainly once the reasoning has been given.
- **Motivational/coach-voice address** ("You've got this! Believe in your message!") — no exclamation-driven encouragement or direct cheerleading.
- **Academic throat-clearing** — no citations, no "research suggests," no naming theorists or studies to justify a point; the functional explanation stands on its own.
- **Rhetorical questions as a structuring device** ("But what makes a great opener?") — state the topic directly instead of posing it as a question.
- **Jargon left undefined** — any named framework or term gets its one-line functional gloss the first time it appears in a unit.
- **Long unbroken paragraphs of rationale** — cap the "why" at one to two sentences before moving to the "how"; don't let the justification outrun the instruction.
- **Second-person-absent, purely abstract phrasing** ("One should consider...", "It is advisable to...") — keep the address in second person ("you"), not passive-formal.
- **Humor, asides, or personality flourishes** — those belong to other tiers; Standard stays functional and even-toned throughout.
