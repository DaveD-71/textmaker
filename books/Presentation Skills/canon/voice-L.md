Long Tier Style Guide — "Presentation Skills"

**1. Voice description**

The Long tier writes the way a thoughtful mentor talks through a problem with someone who has time to actually think it over — not the way a manual gives orders. Sentences vary deliberately in length: a short sentence lands a point, then a longer one unpacks the reasoning, complication, or exception behind it, often across two clauses joined by "but," "which means," "though," or a colon that opens into an example. Paragraphs run three to six sentences and typically move from observation to nuance to application, rather than stating a rule and stopping. Formality sits at educated-conversational: contractions are fine ("you'll notice," "it's not that simple"), first- and second-person voice is normal ("you might find," "I'd suggest treating this as a starting point rather than a rule"), but slang and casual asides are avoided. Instructions are almost never bare imperatives; they're framed as reasoned suggestions with the "why" attached, and they routinely acknowledge that context changes the right answer — different audiences, different rooms, different speakers will need different things. The tier makes regular use of direct reflection prompts ("Think about a presentation you sat through recently — what made the speaker easy, or hard, to trust?") and openly flags tradeoffs or alternative schools of thought rather than presenting one method as the only correct one.

**2. Sample sentences**

- On eye contact: "There's a common piece of advice that says to hold eye contact with one audience member for a full sentence before moving to the next — and it works, in the sense that it looks deliberate rather than scattered, but it isn't the only way to do this well. Some experienced speakers instead work the room in loose zones, letting their gaze rest on clusters of people rather than individuals, which can feel more natural if darting between specific faces makes you self-conscious. The point underneath either technique is the same: your eyes should be doing something intentional, not searching for an exit."

- Introducing a framework: "One way to think about structuring a persuasive talk is the classic problem-solution-benefit shape, and it's popular for good reason — it mirrors how people naturally process an argument, moving from 'why should I care' to 'here's what to do' to 'here's what I get.' That said, treat it as a lens rather than a template you fill in mechanically; a room that already agrees there's a problem doesn't need three minutes of convincing that one exists, and forcing the structure there will cost you credibility, not build it. Try sketching your content against this shape first, then ask where it's genuinely useful and where it's just adding steps."

- On rehearsal: "It's tempting to treat rehearsal as something you do until you stop making mistakes, but that's a fairly narrow goal — a more useful question might be what you're actually trying to learn each time you run through the material. Early rehearsals are for finding out whether your argument holds together out loud, which is a different thing from your fifth or sixth pass, where you're really working on timing and delivery. Worth asking yourself: which of those two problems are you actually solving right now?"

**3. Avoid list**

- Numbered step-by-step instructions presented as the only path ("Step 1... Step 2...") without acknowledging alternatives or context-dependence
- Bare imperatives with no reasoning attached ("Make eye contact. Stand still. Speak slowly.")
- Absolute claims ("always," "never," "the only way") presented without qualification
- Uniformly short, clipped sentences — a whole paragraph of 6-8 word sentences reads as Essentials tier, not Long
- Jargon or framework names dropped without unpacking why they matter or where they came from
- Treating a single method, framework, or rule as universally correct rather than as one useful option among several
- Skipping the "why" — stating what to do without at least gesturing at the reasoning or tradeoff behind it
- Omitting reflection: going more than a section or two without inviting the learner to pause and think, not just execute
- Overly casual slang or jokey asides that undercut the mentor-like, conversational-but-substantive register
- Padding for length's sake — nuance and reflection should earn their word count, not just extend sentences artificially
