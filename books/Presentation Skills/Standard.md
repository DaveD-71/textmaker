# Unit 1: Why Presentations Matter

## Standard

### What a presentation is actually for

It's worth starting from a basic question, because skipping it is how presentations go wrong before a single slide gets made: what is this speech actually supposed to do? A presentation isn't primarily an act of information transfer — audiences can read a document for that — it's an attempt to change something in the listener: a belief, a decision, an emotional state, or a next action. Framing your task this way, before you touch structure or slides, changes what "good" looks like for the rest of your preparation.

That change is shaped by three factors working together, not by the speaker alone. The speaker brings expertise and a goal; the audience brings existing knowledge, expectations, and needs; and the occasion brings constraints — the reason this talk is happening now, in this format, for this length of time. A message that ignores any one of these three tends to miss, even if the content itself is accurate and well-organized, because "good content" is only good relative to who's receiving it and why.

### Are all speeches the same?

No, and treating them as if they were is a common early mistake. A toast, a project update, and a conference keynote all draw on the same underlying skill set, but they differ enormously in purpose, tone, and structure, so a plan built for one will often feel wrong in another. Before you prepare content, it's worth explicitly naming the type of speech you're giving and what "success" looks like for that specific occasion, rather than defaulting to a general-purpose template.

### Three Phases of Preparation

A useful way to organize how you get ready for a talk is to think of preparation as three sequential phases, each with its own components, rather than a flat list of things to check. Sequencing it this way solves a real problem: it's hard to know what to say before you know why you're saying it, and it's hard to know how you'll say it before you've actually written it down.

**Phase 1: Planning.** This is where you decide what the talk is actually going to be, before you draft a single sentence of it.

- **Purpose** is the specific change you want in your audience by the end — not a topic, but an outcome.
- **Message** is the one thing you want your audience to walk away remembering. It's the single sentence your entire talk is organized around, and it's worth getting precise about early, since everything else in Planning is built to support it. (Unit 2 covers how to test whether you actually have a real message, rather than just a restated topic.)
- **Content** is the material itself — the facts, examples, and evidence that support your message — filtered for what's true, relevant, and useful to this particular audience rather than everything you know.
- **Structure** is the order and shape that content takes, so the audience can follow the logic rather than just receive facts.
- **Visual Aids** are the slides, props, or other visual support you'll use, planned now because they shape what you'll actually be able to do later, during Delivery — you can't practice standing beside a screen you haven't designed yet.

**Phase 2: Writing.** This is where the plan becomes actual words on a page.

- **Drafting** is writing the script out in full, working from the plan rather than starting from a blank page.
- **Honing** is editing that draft specifically for the ear rather than the eye — cutting sentences that read fine on paper but sound stiff or overlong out loud, since spoken and written language behave differently.

**Phase 3: Practice.** This is where the written script becomes an actual spoken talk.

- **Delivery** covers voice, pacing, and body language — the physical and vocal execution of the talk, built on top of everything decided in the first two phases.

A talk can have a strong plan and still fail in Delivery, or have a clear purpose and still lose the room through weak Structure — the three-phase framework works as a checklist precisely because weakness at any stage can undercut everything that came before it.

### Purpose and audience analysis look different online

Two of these elements — Purpose and the audience analysis that feeds into Content and Structure, both part of Planning — need to be reconsidered specifically for online delivery, because an online audience is not simply an in-person audience viewed through a screen. In person, you can read faces and body language in real time and adjust your pacing or emphasis accordingly; online, that feedback loop is largely gone, and attention is competing with notifications, other browser tabs, and the general fatigue of screen-based meetings.

Practically, this means your purpose statement and your read of the audience need to build in shorter attention spans and more fragmented focus from the outset, rather than assuming the same plan will simply transfer. Ask explicitly, for every talk you prepare: is this being delivered in person or on a screen, and what does that change about what the audience can actually absorb?

### Activity: Presentation history inventory and goal-setting

Take about fifteen minutes for this exercise, either before your next session or at its start.

1. List the presentations you've given so far — professional, academic, or informal. Three or four is a reasonable starting point if your experience is limited.
2. For each one, note briefly what worked and what didn't. Be specific rather than general ("lost the room around the data slide" is more useful than "it was okay").
3. Look across your list for a pattern — a strength or a weakness that shows up more than once. That recurring item is often the most useful thing to work on this course.
4. Write a single personal goal for the course: one sentence describing what you want to be able to do differently by the end.
5. In pairs or small groups, share your pattern and your goal with a partner. Listening to someone else's inventory is often as useful as reflecting on your own, since it surfaces habits you might not have named in yourself yet.

Keep this inventory — later units will ask you to revisit it as your skills develop.

# Unit 2: Structure of a Speech (+ Theme vs. Message)

## Standard

### The Three-Part Structure

Audiences can't flip back a page the way a reader can, so a spoken structure has to do more work to keep them oriented. The classic three-part structure — Introduction, Body, Conclusion — solves this by repeating your core content in three different forms: you preview it, deliver it, then review it. The shorthand for this is "say what you're going to say, say it, then say what you said," and while it sounds repetitive on paper, that repetition is exactly what makes a spoken argument easy to follow without notes in hand.

**The Introduction** does two jobs: it states your message and previews your structure. Because listeners decide quickly whether a talk is worth their attention, the message should appear early — ideally in your first few sentences, not built up to through throat-clearing or scene-setting. After the message, a brief preview of your main points (two or three is typical) gives the audience a mental map to follow as you move through the body.

**The Body** delivers the content, organized around the points you previewed. Because listeners can't see paragraph breaks, verbal signposting — "my first point is...", "turning now to...", "finally..." — carries the structural weight that headings and white space would carry on a page. Each point should be developed enough to support your message, but not so exhaustively that the throughline gets lost.

**The Conclusion** restates the message and briefly reviews the points that supported it. This isn't filler: because audiences retain a talk's opening and closing more reliably than its middle, using the conclusion to reinforce the message is one of the most efficient ways to make sure it's what people actually remember.

### Theme vs. Message

A theme and a message sound similar but do different work, and mixing them up is one of the most common reasons a speech feels flat. The **theme** is simply your topic — the subject area you're addressing, such as "customer retention" or "the new expense policy." The **message** is your specific opinion or proposal about that theme, and it has to contain evaluative content: a stance, a recommendation, a judgment. "I'd like to talk about customer retention" states a theme with no message attached, which leaves the audience waiting to find out what you actually think.

A useful test is to ask whether your statement could be disagreed with. "Our customer retention rate is 62%" is a fact, not a message — nobody can meaningfully argue with it. "Our 62% retention rate means we're underinvesting in onboarding" is a message, because someone could push back on it, which means it's actually saying something. Before you build a speech, confirm you have a message and not just a well-dressed theme; the three-part structure works best when there's a clear position for the introduction to state and the conclusion to reinforce.

### Online Delivery: Pacing and Front-Loading

Delivering a speech on video changes how much structural patience your audience has. Viewers on a call or a recording drop off faster than an audience in a physical room, partly because leaving is so much lower-friction — closing a tab takes less nerve than walking out of a conference room. That makes front-loading the message even more important online than in person: state it in your opening sentence rather than easing into it, since a viewer who hasn't heard your point within the first 10–15 seconds is a viewer at real risk of disengaging.

Pacing matters too. Trim your body to fewer, tighter points online — two well-developed points usually land better on video than three, since the lack of physical presence makes every extra transition a place where attention can drift. Moving briskly between points, with clear verbal signposting, helps compensate for the reduced sense of momentum that a screen naturally creates compared to a live room.

### Activity: Critique, Then Draft

**Step 1 — Critique.** Theme: "Employee training budgets." Evaluate the three candidate messages below and identify which is strongest, and why the other two fall short.

- A) "Training budgets are a topic worth discussing."
- B) "We spent a significant amount on training this year."
- C) "We should double the training budget, because it cut our error rate by 20% this year."

Option C is the strongest message: it takes a clear position (double the budget) and supports it with evaluative reasoning (the error-rate result). Options A and B restate the theme in different words — A gestures at the topic without a stance, and B offers a fact with no judgment attached. Neither gives the audience something to agree or disagree with, which is the test a real message has to pass.

**Step 2 — Build your own theme/message pair.** Choose a theme relevant to your own work (for example, "remote work policy" or "our onboarding process") and write one weak, theme-only statement and one strong message that takes a real position on it. Checking your own pair against the test above — could someone reasonably disagree with it? — is a fast way to confirm you've written a message and not just a restated topic.

**Step 3 — Draft your outline.** Using the self-introduction model in the Self-Introduction Models appendix as your reference, draft a full Introduction/Body/Conclusion outline for your own self-introduction:

- **Introduction:** your message (who you are and the one thing you want people to take away), plus a preview of your two or three points.
- **Body:** each point, briefly developed, in the order you previewed.
- **Conclusion:** a restatement of your message and a one-line review of your points.

Write this as a working outline rather than a full script — phrases and short sentences are enough to test whether the structure holds together before you build it out into a full speech.

# Unit 3: Flag Expressions

## Standard

### What Flag Expressions Do

A **Flag Expression** is a short phrase that marks a structural transition in a speech — it tells your audience which part of the talk they're in and where you're headed next. The name comes from a useful image: a tour guide holding up a flag so the group can follow without getting lost in a crowd. A speech works the same way for a listener, who can't see headings or paragraph breaks the way a reader can. Without a flag at each turn, your audience has to guess whether you've moved to a new point or are still developing the last one, and that guesswork pulls their attention away from your content.

Because a listener can't scan ahead or flip back, Flag Expressions are worth memorizing rather than improvising in the moment — you want them to come out automatically so your attention stays on your content, not on hunting for a transition.

### The Flag Expression Table

Flag Expressions are organized by which part of the speech they serve, since the Introduction, Body, and Conclusion each have a different job and need different signposting.

**Part 1: Introduction**

| Purpose | Flag Expression |
|---|---|
| Appreciate the introduction | "Thank you for the introduction, M. Chair." |
| Greet the audience | "Good evening, everybody." |
| Give your theme | "Today, I am going to tell you about..." |
| Preview your main points | "I have three main points. First... Second... Third..." |

**Part 2: Body**

| Purpose | Flag Expression |
|---|---|
| Start the body | "Let me begin with my first point." |
| Move to the second point | "Now, I'll move on to my second point." |
| Move to the third point | "Finally, I will tell you my third point." |

**Part 3: Conclusion**

| Purpose | Flag Expression |
|---|---|
| Signal the review | "In conclusion, let me review my main points." |
| Review the points | "First... Then... Finally, I talked about..." |
| State your message | "I think that..." |
| Close | "Thank you. And now, I will be happy to answer your questions." |

### Three Usage Errors Worth Fixing Early

These three mistakes are common enough, and easy enough to avoid once you know them, that they're worth calling out directly rather than leaving you to notice them on your own.

**Counting words belong to the Introduction and Conclusion, not the Body.** "First, second, third" is a counting device — it's how you preview and review a list of points. But once you're inside the Body actually delivering those points, counting words stop working; a listener who hears "second" in the middle of a talk has no idea whether you mean your second point or your second example within the first point. The Body needs transition expressions instead — "Now, I'll move on to..." or "Finally, I will tell you..." — which signal movement without implying a numbered list.

**"At first" is not a formal synonym for "firstly."** It's tempting to reach for "at first" as a slightly more formal-sounding version of "first," but the two aren't interchangeable — "at first" is grammatically a different structure that introduces a condition that later changes, as in "At first it was difficult, but after a while it became easier." Using it to introduce your first main point creates a sentence that sounds like it's setting up a contrast that never arrives.

**"Let's" is informal, and formal proposals need a different construction.** "Let's take a short break" works fine in a relaxed moment, but "Let's improve our products and advertising" reads as too casual for a formal recommendation or conclusion. Swap it for a construction like "I think we need to improve our products and advertising," which carries the same proposal without the informal register.

One more note on register: this unit uses "M." as a gender-neutral form of address (M. Chair, M. Chairperson) rather than "Mr. Chairman," since the expression itself shouldn't assume the listener's gender.

### Activity: Drill, Then Apply to Your Outline

**Step 1 — Drill.** Fill in each blank with the correct Flag Expression from the table above, paying attention to which part of the speech each blank belongs to.

1. "___, I am going to tell you about our new onboarding process." *(Introduction — theme)*
2. "I have two main points. ___, I will cover the timeline. ___, I will cover the cost." *(Introduction — preview)*
3. "Let me begin with my first point. ___, I'll move on to my second point." *(Body — transition, not a counting word)*
4. "In conclusion, let me review my main points. ___, I talked about the timeline. ___, I talked about the cost." *(Conclusion — review)*
5. Rewrite this so it's appropriate for a formal recommendation: "___ improve our onboarding process."

**Answers:** 1. Today 2. First / Second 3. Now 4. First / Finally 5. "I think we need to improve our onboarding process."

**Step 2 — Apply Flag Expressions to your own outline.** Return to the Introduction/Body/Conclusion outline you drafted in Unit 2's activity. Go through it line by line and insert a Flag Expression at each structural turn: the opening greeting and theme statement, the preview of your points, the transition into each Body point, and the review and message in your Conclusion. Read the outline aloud once you've added them — the goal is for each transition to sound automatic, not bolted on, so if a Flag Expression feels awkward in place, try an alternative from the table rather than forcing it.

# Unit 4: Delivery Skills (incl. webcam/online basics)

## Standard

### Introduction

Content and structure only carry a presentation so far — delivery is what the audience actually experiences in the room, and it's often what they remember when the details of your argument have faded. This unit covers the core nonverbal and vocal skills — eye contact, voice, gestures, posture, notes-handling, and clothing — and extends them into the online and hybrid settings where an increasing share of presentations now happen, covering platform controls, screen-share setup, recording and backup, and webcam framing as part of the same skill set rather than a separate topic.

### Eye Contact

Eye contact signals that you're speaking to someone rather than at them, so rotate your gaze slowly across three or four points in the room rather than fixing on one friendly face. Holding each point for a full thought — roughly three to five seconds — reads as natural; darting between faces reads as nervous, and sweeping the room in a continuous scan reads as avoidance rather than connection.

On webcam, the equivalent behavior is looking into the lens rather than at the screen, since that's what creates the sensation of eye contact for the person watching. Because it feels unnatural to stare at a small piece of glass instead of a face, it helps to place your camera at eye level and treat the lens itself as the audience member you're rotating your attention toward.

### Voice

Vocal delivery carries meaning independently of your words — pace, pitch, and pause all signal confidence or its absence. Speaking too quickly is the most common nerves-driven habit, so consciously slowing down, especially at the start of a talk, buys you both clarity and a calmer opening.

Letting your pitch drop at the end of a sentence can make even a confident statement sound like a question or an apology, so aim to keep your pitch level or falling deliberately on your final word, not trailing off. Pausing after a key point gives the audience a moment to absorb it rather than moving straight into your next idea — treat the pause as part of the delivery, not a gap to fill.

### Gestures

Gestures should support your meaning rather than distract from it, which generally means keeping them within a zone roughly between your waist and chest, where the audience's eye naturally rests. Reserve larger or sharper gestures for the moments that need emphasis — a contrast, a number, a turning point in your argument — so that they retain their impact instead of blending into constant movement.

On webcam the frame is tighter, so gestures that would read as natural in a room can clip out of shot or appear exaggerated in close-up. Keeping your gestures smaller and closer to your body on camera, and testing your own framing beforehand, helps you avoid distracting movement just outside the visible frame.

### Posture

Standing with your weight evenly balanced and your feet roughly hip-width apart projects steadiness, whereas rocking, swaying, or shifting your weight repeatedly reads as nervous energy even when your content is strong. In an online setting, posture is still visible from the waist up, so sitting upright and leaning slightly toward the camera — rather than slouching back — sends the same signal of engagement that standing tall does in person.

### Notes-Handling

Relying on cue cards with key words, rather than a full script, tend to serve most speakers better, because a full script invites you to read rather than speak, which flattens vocal delivery and breaks eye contact. Glance down to retrieve your next point, then look back up before you speak it — the goal is for notes to support your memory, not replace your preparation.

### Clothing

Dress codes for presenting have shifted well beyond a single suit-and-tie default, so the more useful guide is to match your audience and occasion and then dress slightly more formally than that baseline, rather than defaulting to a fixed uniform. On webcam, solid colors and simple patterns read more clearly than busy prints or fine stripes, which can distort under video compression, so it's worth checking your on-screen appearance on a test call before a real one.

### Platform Controls, Screen-Share, and Recording

Online delivery adds a layer of mechanical setup that in-person delivery doesn't require, and treating it as part of your rehearsal — not an afterthought — avoids most of the common failures. Before you start, confirm your camera and microphone are the correct active devices, keep yourself muted until you're speaking, and know exactly where your screen-share control is so you're not hunting for it live.

When sharing your screen, share a specific window rather than your full desktop where possible, and close unrelated tabs and notifications beforehand — a pop-up message mid-share undermines the professionalism you've otherwise built. Recording your session, where appropriate, gives you material for later review, and having a backup plan — a second device, a hotspot, a PDF version of your slides — means a platform failure doesn't end your presentation.

### Activity: Peer Observation and Webcam Self-Review

This activity uses your Unit 2 draft to practice reading your own delivery against a fixed checklist, first through a partner's eyes and then through your own.

1. Working with a partner, deliver 60 seconds of your Unit 2 draft while they complete the full delivery-skills observation checklist (eye contact, voice, gestures, posture, notes-handling, clothing).
2. Swap roles so your partner delivers their 60 seconds while you observe.
3. Individually, record yourself delivering the same 60 seconds on webcam.
4. Watch the recording back and complete the same checklist against your own performance.
5. Compare your self-review notes with your partner's observation notes — the gaps between what you noticed and what they noticed are usually the most useful data point.

Both the peer-observation and the self-review passes are required; each surfaces different blind spots, and together they give you a more complete read on your delivery than either alone.

# Unit 5: The Logic Tree

## Standard

### The Logic Tree

Planning a speech directly on the page — writing sentences before you know your shape — is how talks end up wandering. The Logic Tree solves this by giving you a visual planning structure to fill out before you draft a word of the actual speech, so your organization is settled before your language is. It works the same way regardless of whether the talk will be delivered in person or online, since it's a planning tool rather than a delivery technique.

The tree has five parts, and they build on each other from the ground up:

- **Roots** — your preparation: audience research, purpose, and any background reading. Everything else in the tree depends on what you dig up here.
- **Trunk** — your theme, condensed into a single line. This is the one idea the whole talk is actually about, and every branch should visibly connect back to it.
- **Branches** — your main points. Three branches is generally the right target: fewer than three tends to leave a talk feeling thin or underdeveloped, while more than three starts to strain what an audience can hold onto and remember afterward.
- **Leaves** — the supporting material for each branch: examples, numbers, quotes, short stories. A branch with no leaves is just an assertion; the leaves are what make it credible.
- **Fruit** — the takeaway. This is what the audience is actually meant to walk away holding, and it's worth writing last, once you can see the whole tree and know what it adds up to.

Because each part depends on the one below it, working the tree in order — Roots, then Trunk, then Branches, then Leaves, then Fruit — tends to produce a more coherent talk than jumping straight to the message and working backward.

### Blank template

Use this template as a starting point for any talk you're planning:

```
FRUIT (the one takeaway):

BRANCH 1:                BRANCH 2:                BRANCH 3:
  Leaf —                   Leaf —                   Leaf —
  Leaf —                   Leaf —                   Leaf —

TRUNK (one-line theme):

ROOTS (audience, purpose, research notes):
```

### Worked example: a product launch

To see the template in use, here's a populated tree for a talk introducing a new budgeting app to a room of small-business owners.

**Roots:** The audience runs small businesses and is generally frustrated by spreadsheet-based bookkeeping; the purpose of the talk is to get attendees to sign up for a free trial by the end of the session.

**Trunk:** "This app gives you your evenings back."

**Branch 1 — Speed.** The app is fast to adopt and fast to use day to day.
- Leaf: Setup takes about four minutes from download to first use.
- Leaf: Bank data syncs automatically, removing manual entry entirely.

**Branch 2 — Accuracy.** The app is more reliable than the spreadsheets it replaces.
- Leaf: It was built with input from working accountants, not developers alone.
- Leaf: Beta testing showed 30% fewer errors than manual spreadsheet tracking.

**Branch 3 — Affordability.** The cost is easy to justify against the alternative.
- Leaf: $12 a month, with no long-term contract.
- Leaf: Less than the cost of one hour of a bookkeeper's time.

**Fruit:** "Try it free for 30 days — sign up before you leave the room today."

Notice that each branch is backed by two leaves rather than asserted on its own, and that the fruit ties directly back to the trunk's promise. That relationship between levels is what makes the tree function as a planning tool rather than just an outline.

### Activity: Build your own tree

Take the Logic Tree Worked Models appendix as your reference — it contains two fully worked examples, one business and one government, showing this template applied to real content. Use them as a model for shape and depth, not as content to copy.

1. Choose a topic for a talk you could realistically give — for practice, or for something you're actually preparing.
2. Copy the blank template below and complete it yourself, from scratch.

```
FRUIT (the one takeaway):

BRANCH 1:                BRANCH 2:                BRANCH 3:
  Leaf —                   Leaf —                   Leaf —
  Leaf —                   Leaf —                   Leaf —

TRUNK (one-line theme):

ROOTS (audience, purpose, research notes):
```

3. Work from the bottom up: fill in your Roots first, then Trunk, then Branches and Leaves, and write your Fruit last, once you can see what the whole tree is actually arguing.
4. Aim for three branches — resist the urge to cram in a fourth "just in case," and resist collapsing to two if a third genuinely belongs.
5. Give each branch at least two leaves. A branch without support is just a claim, and claims without support tend to be the first thing an audience stops trusting.

Hold onto this completed tree — the next unit will use it directly as the basis for building your speech outline, so it's worth taking the time to get the branches and leaves right now rather than patching them later.

# Unit 6: Slide Design (Visual Aids)

## Standard

### Slides support your talk; they shouldn't compete with it

Slide design matters because a slide that's doing too much work pulls attention away from you and onto itself — the audience starts reading instead of listening, and once that happens, you've effectively lost the room to your own visual aid. The fix isn't to remove slides but to design them around two disciplined constraints: the Rule of 1 and the Rule of 7.

### The Rule of 1

The Rule of 1 holds that each slide should carry exactly one idea, because a slide juggling two or three ideas forces the audience to decide which one matters, and they'll often guess wrong or simply disengage. In practice, this means that if you notice a slide covering two separate points, the right move is to split it into two slides rather than compress both onto one — a longer deck with clean, single-idea slides reads faster than a shorter one crammed with multi-point slides.

### The Rule of 7

The Rule of 7 sets a practical ceiling: no more than seven lines per slide, and no more than seven words per line. The number itself isn't a hard scientific threshold, but it functions as a useful guardrail against the natural tendency to over-explain on the slide instead of in your delivery. When you find yourself exceeding it, treat that as a signal to either cut supporting detail you can say aloud instead, or split the content across an additional slide.

### The four-part structure

A well-organized deck follows a consistent shape, and using it consistently across your presentations means audiences always know where they are in your talk:

1. **Title** — states what the talk is and, often, who's delivering it.
2. **Index** — previews the sections to come, functioning as a map the audience can mentally return to.
3. **Content** — the body of the talk, built slide by slide according to the Rule of 1 and Rule of 7.
4. **Conclusion** — distills the talk down to the single takeaway you want retained.

Building your deck in this order — rather than diving straight into content slides — makes the overall structure visible to you as the designer, which usually surfaces gaps or redundancies before you've invested time in polishing individual slides.

### Typography, color, and template choices

Font and color choices should serve legibility first and branding second. A sans-serif font at a large size (24pt or larger for body text is a reasonable floor) reads more reliably at a distance or on a small screen than a decorative or serif typeface. Limit yourself to two fonts per deck — one for headings, one for body — since more than that starts to look inconsistent rather than intentional.

Color needs the same scrutiny, because a meaningful share of any audience has some degree of color vision deficiency, and red-green contrasts — the most common failure case — can render two supposedly distinct colors as visually identical to them. Favor high-contrast combinations (dark text on a light background or the reverse) and avoid using color as the only signal for meaning; pairing a color cue with a label, icon, or shape means the information still comes through even if the color itself doesn't register. Choose a template that reinforces this simplicity rather than fighting it — ornate templates with heavy borders, gradients, or busy backgrounds compete with your content instead of framing it.

### Designing for the screen-share thumbnail, not just the projector

It's worth treating on-screen legibility as a first-order design constraint rather than an afterthought, because a growing share of the audience for any given deck will see it as a small thumbnail in a video call rather than projected on a wall. A slide that reads comfortably from the back of a room can become illegible once it's reduced to a quarter of a laptop screen alongside a speaker's video feed. Before you finalize a deck, it's worth previewing it at a reduced size — many presentation tools offer a thumbnail or grid view for exactly this — and treating any slide that becomes hard to read at that size as needing simplification, not just a font-size increase.

### Activity: Redesign a mini-deck

You'll be given a deliberately poor three-slide deck: dense blocks of text, low-contrast color choices, and no visible hierarchy between slides.

Redesign all three slides by applying what this unit covers:

1. Check each slide against the Rule of 1 — does it carry a single idea? Split any slide that doesn't.
2. Check each slide against the Rule of 7 — count lines and words per line, and trim or split where it exceeds the limit.
3. Review your color and contrast choices — replace any combination that would fail for a colorblind viewer, and make sure no meaning depends on color alone.
4. Confirm the three slides now map onto the four-part structure — you may need to add a simple title or index slide to frame them properly.

Compare your redesigned deck against the original and be ready to explain what changed and why.

# Unit 7: Chairing a Session

### Chairing a Session

Chairing a session is a distinct skill from presenting, because the chair's job is to manage other people's airtime rather than fill their own, and that requires a different set of phrases and instincts. A good chair is largely invisible — the audience remembers the speaker and the content, not the person running the clock — but a weak chair is very visible, because dead air, awkward handovers, and mistimed cutoffs all trace back to the chair. This unit covers the three functions the role requires: introducing a speaker, managing question-and-answer, and closing the session, with the online variant of each folded in rather than treated separately, since most chairing now happens at least partly on video calls.

**Introducing the speaker**

An introduction has one job: to hand the room's attention to the speaker as efficiently as possible, so it should cover name, credential, and topic and then get out of the way. A useful template is: state the speaker's name and one line of relevant background, state the topic or title of the talk, then use a clear handover phrase such as "please welcome [name]" or "I'll hand over to you now." Avoid previewing the content yourself — if you summarize the talk before the speaker gives it, you've taken some of their material and some of the room's attention with it.

**Managing questions and answers**

Q&A is the part of a session most likely to go off the rails, because it's the one segment that isn't scripted, so the chair's phrases here are doing real management work rather than just ceremony. Open the segment plainly — "we have time for questions now" — and then manage the mechanics: point to questioners rather than letting the loudest voice win, repeat back a question in a large room so everyone has heard what's being answered, and gently compress a rambling question into its core ask before passing it to the speaker ("so the question is whether…?"). As time runs short, a single clear signal — "we have time for one more question" — tells the room the segment is ending without cutting it off abruptly. If no hands go up, having a backup question ready avoids an awkward silence and keeps the session moving.

**The online layer: hands, mutes, and chat**

Online sessions add a mechanical layer on top of the same social task, and the chair needs phrases for both. Set expectations early — "please use the raise-hand function and I'll bring you in" — so participants know the mechanism before they need it. When a participant is unmuted, a short acknowledgment ("you're unmuted, go ahead") confirms the floor is theirs. Chat-based questions need to be surfaced out loud before they're answered, both for participants who aren't reading the chat and for anyone joining by audio only: "there's a question in the chat from [name] asking…" When several hands or chat questions arrive at once, naming the queue out loud ("I've got three questions queued, we'll take them in order") keeps the room calm and signals that no one has been skipped.

**Closing the session**

A close should be short and should always contain three elements: thanks to the speaker, thanks to the audience, and a clear signal that the session has ended or what comes next. Skipping any of these tends to leave the room unsure whether it's really over, which produces the awkward half-standing shuffle you've probably sat through yourself. "Thank you, [name], for a great session — and thanks to all of you for your questions. That brings us to a close" covers all three in one breath and hands control of the room back cleanly.

### Activity: Full Session Cycle

Work in small groups. Each learner rotates through all three roles — chairperson, speaker, and questioner — across the exercise, so that everyone chairs a complete session at least once.

- Draw on the Unit 3 phrase banks: use the flag expressions there for structuring the speaker's short talk, and the chairing phrases from this unit for the introduction, Q&A, and close.
- As chairperson, run the full cycle for one round: introduce the speaker, open and manage a short Q&A (aim to use the "one more question" signal at least once), and close the session with all three closing elements.
- As speaker, give a two-to-three-minute mini-talk using a flag-expression structure from Unit 3, then take one or two questions.
- As questioner, ask a genuine question and, in at least one round, ask a deliberately rambling one so the chairperson gets practice compressing it before handing it to the speaker.
- After each rotation, give the chairperson brief feedback: was the introduction efficient, was the queue managed clearly, did the close cover all three elements?

# Unit 8: Capstone Practice Activity (Timed Impromptu Circuit)

This unit doesn't introduce a new skill — it puts the skills from the rest of the course under time pressure, which is a different test than practicing them one at a time. The format is a timed impromptu circuit: learners draw an unseen topic, structure a response using the Logic Tree (Unit 5), deliver it, field questions, receive feedback, and rotate through the chairing role (Unit 7) that keeps the whole thing on schedule. Because the activity *is* the content, this unit reads as a facilitation procedure rather than a lesson followed by an exercise.

**Format: Full Circuit — approximately 15 minutes per learner.**

**Step 1 — Topic draw.** Each learner draws a topic blind, from a prepared list or jar, and doesn't see it in advance. Working from an unseen prompt is the point: it tests whether you can structure thinking on the spot rather than recalling a rehearsed answer.

**Step 2 — Prepare with the Logic Tree (2–3 minutes).** The Logic Tree gives you a fast way to organize an argument under time pressure: one trunk (your central claim), two or three branches (supporting points), and a leaf per branch (a concrete example). Working silently within this structure, rather than drafting full sentences, keeps preparation time from running long and forces the same discipline the tree was designed for.

**Step 3 — Present (3–4 minutes).** Deliver the talk from the tree, not from a script. Because the structure is already decided, delivery time can go toward eye contact, pacing, and framing rather than toward remembering what comes next.

**Step 4 — Q&A (2–3 minutes).** The chair opens the floor for at least two questions. Answering under real (not rehearsed) questioning is a different skill from delivering prepared content, and it's the reason this unit exists: a presenter who can only perform a memorized structure hasn't yet learned to think in front of an audience.

**Step 5 — Feedback (3–4 minutes).** The chair runs a structured round: one specific strength, one specific area to change. Keeping feedback concrete ("you returned to your trunk claim before each branch" rather than "good job") makes it usable, and the speaker's job here is to listen rather than respond — defending a choice in the moment burns time and misses the point of the round.

**Step 6 — Rotate the chair.** The next learner takes the chairing role for the next round, which means everyone practices Unit 7's skills — timekeeping, calling on questioners, and structuring the feedback sequence — at least once across the circuit.

**Running the room:** the chair enforces turn-taking and calls time out loud (a 30-second warning works well), and observers hold side comments until the feedback round so the speaker isn't interrupted mid-talk. If time is tight across a group, the prepare and present steps are the ones to compress first — the Q&A and feedback steps are where most of the learning happens, so protect them.

# Unit 9: Speaking Clearly (Pronunciation and Prosody)

## Standard Tier

### Why Prosody Matters More Than Individual Sounds

Audiences forgive an unfamiliar accent far more readily than they forgive unclear pacing, because accent is about individual sounds while clarity is mostly about prosody — the rhythm, stress, and pausing that shape how speech is grouped and understood. A listener can usually reconstruct a slightly mispronounced word from context, but if the words arrive in an unbroken stream with no pauses to mark where one idea ends and the next begins, the listener has nowhere to rest and starts losing the thread. This unit gives you a concrete notation system for marking up your own speech so that pacing, grouping, and stress become deliberate choices rather than whatever happens to come out under pressure.

The guidance here applies regardless of your first language. Rather than targeting specific sound substitutions tied to any one language background, it focuses on four adjustments that make spoken English easier to follow for any listener: slowing down, lengthening key vowels slightly, grouping words into meaningful chunks with pauses, and stressing the words that carry the most information.

### The Four Adjustments

**Speak slowly.** A pace that feels slow to you as the speaker usually sounds normal, even brisk, to a listener — this is because you already know what you're about to say, so the words feel instant to you and require more processing time for the person hearing them for the first time. Aim for a pace noticeably slower than your natural conversational speed, especially at the start of a presentation.

**Lengthen vowels slightly.** Stretching the vowel sound in a stressed syllable — turning "great" into a fraction longer "greaat" — gives the listener's ear more time to register the word and signals which word in a phrase carries the meaning. This is a small adjustment, not exaggerated drawling; the goal is clarity, not a caricature of slow speech.

**Group words with pauses.** Speech is easier to follow when it's chunked into short phrases separated by brief pauses, roughly matching the natural grammatical units of the sentence (after a subject, before a new clause, around a listed item). Pausing between sentences and between paragraphs — holding a beat longer than feels natural — gives the audience a moment to absorb one idea before the next one starts.

**Stress key words.** Every sentence has a small number of words carrying the actual information — usually nouns, verbs, and numbers rather than articles, prepositions, or auxiliary verbs — and giving those words slightly more volume, pitch, and length tells the listener where to focus. Stressing the wrong word, or stressing everything equally, forces the listener to work out the meaning themselves.

### A Notation System for Marking Up Your Speech

Writing these adjustments directly onto your script turns them into something you rehearse deliberately rather than something you hope happens naturally. Use three marks:

- **A single slash `/`** for a short pause — roughly the length of a comma, marking a word group boundary.
- **A double slash `//`** for a longer pause — between sentences or at a major structural break, such as moving from your introduction to your first point.
- **An underline** under a vowel to mark slight lengthening on a stressed syllable.
- **Bold text** for the word or words in each phrase that carry primary stress.

Here's a worked example applying all four marks to a single sentence:

> Th<u>i</u>s **quarter**, / our **sales** / **grew** by **twelve** perc<u>e</u>nt. // That's the **strongest** result / we've **seen** / in **three years**. //

Read that aloud, pausing at each slash and leaning slightly on the bolded words, and notice how different it sounds from reading the same sentence at a flat, even pace with no marks at all. The pauses give the listener time to process "this quarter" as a unit before "our sales" arrives; the stress on "twelve" and "strongest" tells them exactly which numbers and claims matter most.

### Speaking Online: Mic Technique and Pacing

When you're presenting through a microphone rather than to a room, two additional factors affect clarity. First, mic technique: keep a consistent distance from the microphone (roughly a hand's width for most built-in laptop or headset mics) so that your volume doesn't spike or fade as you move, and be aware that leaning in to emphasize a point can cause distortion rather than the intended effect. Second, pacing needs to account for audio compression and network latency, both of which can clip the very start of a sentence or introduce a slight delay — building in a fractionally longer pause after any pause mark in your script gives the audio pipeline, and any listeners on a delayed connection, room to catch up without losing your opening words.

### Activity: Annotated Self-Practice

Start with the worked example above, or a short model paragraph of your own choosing, and mark it up independently using the four-symbol notation — pauses, lengthening, and stress — before checking your version against a model markup. This step trains your eye to spot where pauses and stress naturally belong.

Next, take the self-introduction you wrote in Unit 2 and apply the same notation to it, marking slash pauses at your phrase boundaries, double slashes between sentences, underlines on vowels you intend to lengthen, and bold on your key words. Read your marked-up self-introduction aloud and record it. Play the recording back and check it against your markup: did you actually pause where you marked a slash, or did the pause disappear under pressure? Did the words you bolded on paper come out stressed when spoken? Revise the markup where your instincts disagreed with your plan, and record a second pass to hear the difference.

# Unit 10: Logic (Description + Reasoning)

## Standard Tier

A presentation earns trust when its structure is easy to follow, not just when its content is accurate. This unit covers three tools for building that structure: Wh-questions for organizing what you cover, connector language for showing how your ideas relate to each other, and a four-part reasoning chain for making individual claims sound and well-supported. None of this is about persuasion — there are no appeals to emotion, authority, or shared values here. That comes later. This unit is about making sure the argument itself holds together before you worry about making it compelling.

### Wh-Questions as Organizing Tools

The six Wh-questions — Who, What, When, Where, Why, How — are a simple audit tool for checking whether your content is complete before you check whether it's persuasive. Each question exposes a gap a listener will otherwise have to guess at, so working through them systematically during preparation catches holes you might miss by just re-reading your draft.

Applied to a presentation about a new internal process, for example: Who does this affect? What exactly is changing? When does it take effect? Where does it apply? Why is it changing? How will people actually do it differently on Monday morning? A presentation that answers "what" and "why" but skips "who" and "how" will sound conceptually sound but practically useless — the audience will nod along and then have no idea what to do differently.

Try running your draft against all six questions before you build slides. You don't need to answer every question out loud in your delivery — some answers might be obvious from context, and forcing all six into every section would make the talk repetitive — but you should be able to answer each one if asked. Gaps you find at this stage are cheap to fix; gaps an audience member finds during Q&A are not.

### Connector Language: Showing the Relationships Between Ideas

Content organized into the right categories still needs signposting to show how those categories relate to each other. Connector language does that work. Four relationship types cover most presentation content:

**Comparison** connectors flag similarity: *similarly, likewise, in the same way, both... and...*. Use these when two things you're describing behave the same way, so the audience doesn't have to work out the parallel themselves.

**Contrast** connectors flag difference: *however, on the other hand, whereas, in contrast, unlike*. These matter more than comparison connectors in most technical or business content, because audiences default to assuming continuity — a contrast connector is what tells them to update that assumption.

**Sequence** connectors flag order: *first, next, subsequently, finally, once... has happened*. These are especially load-bearing in process or timeline content, where getting the order wrong changes the meaning of what you're describing, not just the style.

**Causal** connectors flag cause and effect: *because, as a result, consequently, this means that, which leads to*. Causal connectors are the ones most often missing from first drafts, because the cause-effect link is obvious to the presenter — who has been thinking about the material for weeks — and invisible to an audience hearing it for the first time.

A useful habit is to read back through a draft section and underline every connector you find. If a section has none, check whether it actually needs one; dense technical or numerical content especially tends to drop connectors because the presenter is focused on the facts rather than the relationships between them.

### The Claim-Evidence-Analysis-Conclusion Chain

Individual assertions in a presentation are more persuasive by being sound before they're persuasive by being delivered well, and a reliable way to make a claim sound is to build it as a four-part chain:

- **Statement**: the claim itself, stated plainly.
- **Evidence**: the concrete fact, data point, or example that supports it.
- **Analysis**: the explanation of how the evidence supports the statement — this step is the one most often skipped, and skipping it is what makes a claim feel asserted rather than proven.
- **Conclusion**: what the statement plus evidence plus analysis together establish, often tied back to the presentation's larger point.

Here's a short example. Statement: "Response times for the support team have improved this quarter." Evidence: "Average first-response time dropped from 6 hours to 90 minutes." Analysis: "That's a reduction of roughly 75%, and it starts right at the point when the new ticket-routing system went live in April." Conclusion: "The routing system is the more likely explanation for the improvement, rather than headcount changes, since headcount stayed flat over the same period."

Notice that the analysis step is where the real work happens — it's the difference between showing a number and showing what the number means. A presentation that lists evidence without analysis is really just reciting statistics.

### Activity: Evidence-Chain Construction

Using the organizational or product presentation model you developed for the Organizational/Product Presentation Models appendix, build one complete Statement → Evidence → Analysis → Conclusion chain for a claim relevant to that model.

Work through the template below:

1. **Statement**: Write the claim in one sentence. Keep it specific enough that someone could disagree with it — "our approach works" is too vague; "our approach reduced onboarding time by a third" is a claim.
2. **Evidence**: Identify the concrete support for the statement — a figure, a documented result, a specific example. If you don't have real evidence available, note what evidence would be needed and where it would come from.
3. **Analysis**: Write one to two sentences explaining how the evidence connects to the statement. Don't just restate the evidence — say what it demonstrates and why.
4. **Conclusion**: State what the chain establishes, and connect it back to the larger point of your presentation.

Once you've built the chain, read it back and check for the most common gap: a jump straight from evidence to conclusion with no analysis in between. If your chain reads as sound without the analysis line, add detail to it until removing it would leave a visible hole.

# Unit 12: Analyze (Problem-Solving / Reporting)

### Why this unit exists

When something has gone wrong — a process is underperforming, a metric has moved in the wrong direction, a customer complaint has become a pattern — the instinct is to jump straight to a fix. But a fix proposed before the problem is properly explained tends to solve the wrong thing, or the right thing for the wrong reason, so this unit deals only with explaining and reporting. It stops deliberately short of recommending action: that's a separate skill, covered in a later unit for learners continuing to the Long-tier material. Here, your job is to make a problem understandable and your findings credible, not to say what should happen next.

### The Background–Situation–Results model

Background–Situation–Results (B-S-R) is a three-part structure for explaining a problem clearly enough that someone unfamiliar with it can follow your reasoning and trust your findings.

- **Background** establishes the context a listener needs before the problem makes sense — what the normal, expected, or baseline state of affairs is.
- **Situation** describes what is actually happening now, and specifically how it differs from that baseline. This is where you name the problem in concrete, observable terms rather than in vague impressions.
- **Results** presents what your investigation actually found — the data, patterns, or evidence that explain the situation.

Because B-S-R ends at Results, it produces a report, not a pitch. Resist the pull to add a fourth beat — "and here's what we should do about it" — even when the recommendation feels obvious.

### Building each section

Background should be brief — two or three sentences that orient a listener who knows the general subject but not this specific issue. Situation is where precision matters most: quantify wherever you can and describe it in terms of what changed. Results is where you show your work — lay out what you looked at and what patterns emerged, and if your findings point in more than one direction, say so.

A useful check before you present: read your Results section and ask whether it contains a single recommendation, suggestion, or "so we should." If it does, move that sentence out — it belongs in a different conversation.

### Activity: Problem Analysis Report

Choose one problem from the list below, or substitute a real one from your own work.

1. A team's on-time project delivery rate has declined over the last two quarters.
2. Customer support response times have gotten noticeably longer.
3. Attendance or engagement at a recurring internal meeting has dropped off.
4. A product or service is generating more complaints about a specific feature than before.

Draft a short spoken report (three to five minutes) using Background–Situation–Results. Deliver it to a partner and ask: "Based on what I just told you, do you understand the problem well enough that you could describe it to someone else?"

# Unit 14: Delivering with Slides & Handouts

## Standard Tier

### Where to Stand, and Why It Matters

Where you stand relative to the screen shapes whether the audience is watching you or watching your back, so the basic rule is simple: stand beside the screen, not in front of it, and angle your body toward the audience rather than the display. This keeps your face visible and your voice projected outward instead of into the slide. When you need to reference something on the screen, turn your head toward it briefly rather than turning your whole body — a quick glance keeps the audience connected to you as the speaker instead of losing you to the display for several seconds at a time.

In a screen-share, the same principle applies in a different form: your camera is the "room," so keep your face visible in a webcam tile rather than switching to a slides-only view, if your platform allows it. Since audience attention now splits between your slide and your video feed, treat the video feed as your stand-in for physical stage presence, and check periodically that it's still active and well-lit.

### Sequencing: Point, Then Speak — or Speak, Then Change

A common timing error is changing the slide and talking about it at the same time, which forces the audience to choose between reading and listening. The fix is a simple sequence: change the slide, pause briefly while the audience takes in the new visual, and only then begin speaking about it. This pause needs to be no longer than a second or two — just enough for the eye to register the new layout before the ear is asked to process new information on top of it.

The same logic applies to pointing. If you're directing attention to a specific part of a slide, point (with a hand, a cursor, or a laser pointer) and give the audience a beat to find the spot before you start explaining what it means. Speaking first and pointing second means half your sentence is already gone by the time anyone finds what you're talking about.

### Changing Slides Without Breaking Your Flow

Advancing slides is a small mechanical action, but it's also one of the most common places where a presenter's rhythm breaks, because it's easy to either forget to advance (leaving an outdated slide up while talking about the next point) or to advance too early (revealing information before you're ready to discuss it). Try building your slide advances into your rehearsed script as explicit cues — a specific word or phrase in your talk that is always the signal to click forward — so the action becomes automatic rather than a decision you make mid-sentence.

In a screen-share, slide advances carry an added risk: a control lag or a shared-screen delay can mean your slide changes a beat after you expect it to, so what looks synchronized on your end may look mistimed to the audience. Build in a slightly longer pause after each click when presenting online, and confirm early in the session — before diving into content — that your slide changes are appearing for the audience roughly when you expect them to.

### Presenting Handouts to a Small Audience

Handouts today are more often a shared document, slide deck, or link sent in advance or dropped into a chat than a stack of paper passed around the room, but the core skill they require hasn't changed: pacing the audience through the material so everyone is looking at the same section at the same time. Unshared pacing is the main failure mode — one person is three pages ahead while another is still on the first paragraph, and both lose track of what you're saying. Announce clearly which section, slide, or heading you're on before you start talking about it, and pause after each transition to let people locate the right spot before you continue.

For a small audience, this pacing can be conversational rather than rigid: check in periodically ("everyone with me on this section?") and adjust your speed based on what you see or hear back, rather than plowing through the material at a fixed pace. Where handouts are shared digitally in advance, you can shorten the walkthrough considerably, since the audience has already had time with the material — in that case, your job shifts from guiding first exposure to highlighting the parts that matter most.

### Activity: Rehearsal with Feedback

Retrieve the redesigned slides you produced in Unit 6. Deliver them aloud to a partner — in person or over a screen-share — applying the sequencing guidance above: change the slide, pause, then speak; point, pause, then explain. Do a single full rehearsal pass, start to finish, without stopping to restart or self-correct mid-way.

Afterward, ask your partner for feedback focused specifically on synchronization: were there moments where you spoke before the slide had changed, or changed the slide before finishing your point? Was there ever a visible gap between a physical or cursor point and the explanation that followed it? Use this feedback to identify one or two specific timing habits to adjust before your next rehearsal, rather than trying to fix everything at once.

Good morning, everyone. I'm Elena Vasquez, and I'm joining the company as your new Head of Operations. Before I tell you about my background, I want to tell you what I actually believe, because I think it's more useful than a list of past job titles: I believe our biggest opportunity right now isn't cutting costs — it's cutting delay. A shipment held an extra half-day at one node would ripple into missed windows three or four steps down the chain.

We ran a project focused entirely on identifying the three or four handoff points where delay was most likely to start, and we redesigned those specific points rather than trying to overhaul the whole system at once. Within eight months, our on-time delivery rate improved by eighteen percent, without adding headcount or budget — the gains came from fixing where the friction actually was, not from working harder everywhere.

My third point is what this means here. I've spent my first two weeks mostly listening — sitting in on your team meetings, walking the floor, asking questions rather than proposing answers. What I've heard so far tells me we likely have a similar pattern: strong people, real effort, and a handful of specific points where delay is quietly building up. I don't have the answers yet, and I want to be upfront about that. What I do have is a process for finding them, and I'd like your help running it.

**Conclusion**

So to bring this back to where I started: I believe the fastest way to improve how we operate isn't a sweeping overhaul, it's finding the handful of places where delay starts and fixing those first. I've done this before, it worked, and I think the same approach applies here. Over the next month, I'll be reaching out to many of you directly to learn more about where you see friction in your own part of the process. Thank you for the warm welcome, and I'm looking forward to working with all of you.

# Appendix: Logic Tree Worked Models

Unit 5 walked through one populated Logic Tree — the budgeting-app launch — to show the template in use. Two more worked examples are collected here, one business and one government, so you have a wider base of models to draw on before you build your own tree in that unit's activity. Both follow the identical five-part shape: Roots, Trunk, Branches, Leaves, Fruit.

## Model 1: Business Context

Here is a populated tree for a talk pitching a remote-work stipend as a new employee benefit to a company's leadership team.

**Roots:** The audience is the executive team, who evaluate proposals primarily through the lens of retention and cost rather than employee goodwill on its own. The purpose of the talk is to secure approval for a one-quarter pilot of the stipend.

**Trunk:** "A $75 monthly stipend will cut our turnover costs by more than it costs us."

**Branch 1 — The problem is real and current.** Turnover is already a documented, active cost, not a hypothetical risk.
- Leaf: Exit interviews cite home-office costs as a factor in 40% of this year's resignations.
- Leaf: Three of the company's five currently open roles have sat vacant for more than 60 days.

**Branch 2 — The stipend is inexpensive relative to the alternative it prevents.** The comparison that matters isn't the stipend's cost in isolation, but its cost against the turnover it's meant to reduce.
- Leaf: The stipend costs approximately $900 per employee per year.
- Leaf: Replacing a single mid-level hire costs roughly $18,000 once recruiting time and lost productivity are counted.

**Branch 3 — The stipend is simple to administer.** A proposal that requires new systems or vendors is a harder sell than one that doesn't.
- Leaf: It can be paid through the company's existing payroll software, with no new vendor contract.
- Leaf: Several comparable companies in the same industry already offer an equivalent benefit, so this isn't an untested idea.

**Fruit:** "Approve a one-quarter pilot with the engineering team, and review the retention numbers after 90 days."

## Model 2: Government / Public-Sector Context

Here is a populated tree for a talk proposing a pedestrian-safety upgrade at a specific intersection, delivered to a city council.

**Roots:** The audience is council members, alongside nearby residents attending the session, both of whom weigh safety benefits against cost to taxpayers. The purpose is to get this specific intersection added to next year's capital improvement budget.

**Trunk:** "One intersection accounts for most of our pedestrian injuries in this district — fixing it first is where the safety budget will do the most good."

**Branch 1 — The risk is concentrated at one location, not distributed evenly across the district.** This matters because it turns a vague safety concern into a specific, fundable project.
- Leaf: The intersection of Fifth and Main has recorded six pedestrian collisions in the past two years, more than any other intersection in the district.
- Leaf: A school and a senior center both sit within two blocks of that intersection, meaning the affected population includes two particularly vulnerable groups.

**Branch 2 — The proposed fix has already been proven to work.** A council is more willing to fund a redesign with a track record than an untested one.
- Leaf: A raised crosswalk paired with a shortened crossing distance reduced injuries by 50% at a comparable intersection in this district three years ago.
- Leaf: The same design is already in use in two other cities within the state.

**Branch 3 — The cost fits inside funding the district already has access to.** Removing the need for a new tax or budget line makes approval considerably easier.
- Leaf: The estimated cost is $140,000, fully coverable by an existing, currently unused state safety grant.
- Leaf: No new local tax increase or bond measure is required.

**Fruit:** "Add this intersection to next year's capital improvement plan, with construction beginning next summer."

Both trees follow the same underlying logic as the budgeting-app example in Unit 5: a trunk stated as a single clear claim, three branches that each carry their own supporting leaves rather than relying on the trunk alone, and a fruit that names a specific, checkable next step rather than a vague call to "consider" something.

# Appendix: Organizational/Product Presentation Models — Business (Product/Service Launch)

## "Introducing Ledgerline" — Launch Address to Small-Business Partners and Press

Good morning, everyone, and thank you for making time to be here. Some of you have traveled a considerable distance for this, and I don't take that lightly. What I want to do over the next fifteen minutes is walk you through why we built the product we're launching today, what it actually does, and what we've already learned from the businesses who tested it before any of you saw it.

Let me start with the problem, because that's really where this began.

Over the past two years, our team spoke with more than three hundred small-business owners across retail, hospitality, and light manufacturing. We asked them a simple question: what part of running your business costs you the most time for the least value? The answer, in seven cases out of ten, was the same — reconciling cash flow across multiple systems. A typical small business we spoke with was using one tool for invoicing, a second for inventory, and a spreadsheet, usually built by someone who has since left the company, to try to make the first two talk to each other. One retailer we interviewed in Manila estimated she spent six hours a week just moving numbers between systems by hand. A restaurant group in Nairobi told us their finance lead had, at one point, three different "final" versions of the same monthly report circulating by email.

That's the gap we built Ledgerline to close. It's a single platform that connects your invoicing, your inventory, and your cash-flow forecasting in one place, so the numbers only have to be entered once and everything downstream updates automatically.

Here's what that looked like in practice during our pilot. We worked with forty-two small businesses over a six-month period, in five countries, across a deliberately varied set of industries — retail, food service, small-scale manufacturing, and professional services. Every one of these businesses had been using at least two disconnected systems before the pilot began.

The results were more consistent than we expected. Average time spent on manual reconciliation dropped by 71% across the pilot group — from just over five hours a week to under ninety minutes. That's not a marginal improvement; it's most of a business day given back every week. And it wasn't just about time. Eleven of the forty-two businesses in the pilot identified cash-flow shortfalls at least two weeks earlier than they would have under their old process, because the forecasting module flags a shortfall automatically instead of waiting for someone to notice it in a spreadsheet. One of those businesses, a small hardware retailer, used that early warning to renegotiate a supplier payment schedule before a shortfall actually hit — something the owner told us directly she would not have caught in time under her previous process.

We also tracked what didn't work as well, because I think it's worth being honest about that in a launch like this rather than only presenting the highlights. Businesses with very high transaction volumes — above roughly two thousand transactions a month — saw a smaller time-savings benefit, closer to 40%, because at that volume some manual review is still genuinely necessary regardless of the tool. We're not claiming Ledgerline replaces financial judgment. We're claiming it removes the mechanical work that used to stand between a business owner and the moment they could actually exercise that judgment.

So where does that leave us today. Starting this quarter, Ledgerline is available to small businesses in eighteen countries, with a tiered pricing structure based on transaction volume rather than a flat fee, because we heard clearly in our interviews that a flat fee structure disadvantages the smallest businesses relative to the value they get. We're also committing to a data-portability guarantee: any business that adopts Ledgerline can export its full transaction history, in a standard format, at any time, with no additional charge. We built that commitment in because trust in a financial tool is earned slowly and lost quickly, and we didn't want cost of switching to be the reason someone stayed with us.

Over the next few months, you'll hear more from us about specific industry configurations, and we'll be publishing the full pilot data set for anyone who wants to review the methodology directly rather than take our summary of it. For today, I just want to leave you with this: the businesses in our pilot didn't get a more impressive piece of software. They got roughly four hours of their week back, and eleven of them got an early warning that mattered. That's the outcome we set out to build toward, and it's the outcome we intend to keep measuring ourselves against as this rolls out more broadly.

Thank you. I'm glad to take questions.

# Appendix: Organizational/Product Presentation Models — Government (Agency/Program Launch)

## Introducing the Regional Small Business Advisory Service — Remarks at Program Launch

Thank you all for being here today, including the business owners, community representatives, and colleagues from other agencies who took the time to join us. I want to use my time this morning to explain why we're launching the Regional Small Business Advisory Service, what it will actually do for the people who use it, and what we learned from the two-district pilot that led us to expand it region-wide starting this year.

I'll start with why this program exists at all.

Over the past eighteen months, our department reviewed licensing and permit applications from small businesses across the region, and one pattern stood out clearly enough that we couldn't ignore it. Nearly 40% of first-time applications were rejected or delayed, not because the underlying business was ineligible, but because of incomplete paperwork, missing supporting documents, or applications submitted to the wrong office entirely. On average, an applicant who was rejected on procedural grounds lost eleven weeks before their business could legally open. For a small business operating on limited startup capital, eleven weeks of delay is not a minor inconvenience. For several applicants we spoke with directly, it was the difference between opening and not opening at all.

So the problem wasn't a shortage of people wanting to start businesses, and it wasn't a shortage of legitimate businesses meeting our criteria. It was a navigation problem — a gap between what the process required and what applicants, especially first-time applicants, understood about how to meet those requirements.

That's the gap this service is built to close. The Regional Small Business Advisory Service provides every first-time applicant with a single point of contact — one advisor, assigned to their application from the day they submit initial paperwork through the day their license is issued or their application is formally resolved. That advisor's job is to review the application before formal submission, flag missing documentation immediately rather than after a multi-week processing queue, and route the applicant to the correct office the first time.

We piloted this model in two districts over the past year before deciding whether to expand it, and I want to walk you through what we found, including the parts that surprised us.

Across the two pilot districts, the procedural rejection rate for first-time applicants fell from 39% to 12%. Average time to a decision — approval or a clear, correctable rejection — dropped from eleven weeks to four. And in a survey we conducted independently of the advisory staff themselves, so as not to bias the results, 84% of applicants who used the service reported that they understood the licensing requirements clearly before submitting, compared with 46% of applicants in non-pilot districts surveyed over the same period.

We also want to be candid about the limits of what we found. The improvement was smaller for applications involving multiple agencies at once — for example, a business requiring both a health permit and a zoning variance — where the rejection rate fell from 44% to 27%, a real improvement, but a smaller one than for single-agency applications. That tells us the advisory model helps most where the core barrier is information and navigation, and helps less, though still meaningfully, where the barrier is genuine cross-agency coordination that a single advisor can smooth but not fully resolve alone. We're treating that as a next problem to solve, not a reason to hold back on what already works.

Based on these results, the service will be available in every district in the region by the end of this year, at no direct cost to applicants — it is funded through the department's existing operating budget, reallocated from processing capacity that this service is expected to free up over time, since a correctly filed application on the first attempt takes less staff time overall than a rejected and resubmitted one.

We'll be publishing district-level outcome data twice a year, so that the public, and frankly so that we ourselves, can see clearly whether this program is delivering the same results at full scale that we saw in the pilot. If it isn't, we intend to say so and adjust the model rather than simply continue running it as designed.

What this program is ultimately about is fairly simple. A business owner with a sound idea and the will to build it shouldn't lose eleven weeks, or lose the business entirely, because a form went to the wrong office. That's the outcome we measured in the pilot, and it's the outcome this expansion is built to deliver at the scale of the whole region.

Thank you, and I'm happy to take questions.

# Appendix: Persuasive/Policy Presentation Models — Business: "What's Behind the Return Rate"

Good afternoon, everyone. I want to walk you through what we've found over the last six weeks looking into our online return numbers. I'm here to explain what's happening and what the data shows — I'm not going to propose a fix today.

For the two years before this spring, our online return rate sat consistently between 4 and 5 percent. That's the baseline everything I'm about to say should be measured against.

Starting in March, our online return rate began climbing. April closed at 7.8 percent. May closed at 9.6 percent. As of last week, our trailing thirty-day rate is 11.4 percent.

We pulled return reason codes for every online return since January. Thirty-eight percent of returns in the affected categories cite "didn't fit as expected," concentrated almost entirely in SKUs added after our January vendor transition. The new supplier's size charts use a different fit standard than our previous vendors, but our product pages still display the same generic size guide.

Each of these returns costs us an average of $14.20 in reverse logistics and restocking. Extrapolated across current volume, this pattern is on pace to cost us just under $340,000 this quarter, against roughly $140,000 in the same quarter last year.

What we choose to do about the size charts or the vendor relationship is a decision for next week's session. I'm happy to take questions on the data itself.

# Appendix: Persuasive/Policy Presentation Models — Government: "Preventable Readmissions: What the Review Found"

Thank you for the opportunity to present the findings of the six-month review into hospital readmission rates. My mandate was to investigate and report, not to recommend a specific policy response.

For the decade leading up to 2023, the thirty-day readmission rate for patients over 65 discharged after treatment for heart failure, COPD, or pneumonia held steady at approximately 14 percent nationally.

Beginning in 2024, the national rate rose to 17.6 percent, and provisional data for this year puts it at 19.1 percent — roughly 22,000 additional readmissions annually. This increase is substantially steeper in regions with below-median primary care physician density.

Patients discharged in under-served regions waited a median of 19 days to see a primary care provider for follow-up, compared to 6 days in well-served regions. Twenty-nine percent of surveyed patients reported confusion about discharge medication instructions. Patients living alone without a caregiver present in the first week were readmitted at nearly twice the rate of patients with regular support.

What follows — whether that involves follow-up scheduling requirements, discharge staffing levels, or community support programs — is rightly a policy decision for this body to weigh, not one for this review to make on your behalf. I'm glad to take questions on the data and methodology.
