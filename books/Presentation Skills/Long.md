# Unit 1: Why Presentations Matter

## Long

### What is a presentation actually trying to do?

It's worth pausing on a question that's easy to skip past on the way to slide design: what is a presentation actually for? The honest answer is that it isn't primarily about transferring information — if that were the goal, a well-written email or report would usually do the job better, since it can be read at the recipient's own pace and referred back to. A presentation earns its place as a format when something needs to happen that a document can't easily do: build shared understanding in a room in real time, persuade people who might resist a cold written argument, or create a moment of shared attention and emotional weight around a decision. Thinking of your task this way — as an attempt to change something in your audience, whether that's a belief, a decision, or a next action — tends to produce a sharper, more useful talk than thinking of it as "covering the material."

That change doesn't happen in a vacuum; it's shaped jointly by three things, and it's worth treating all three as live variables rather than assuming your own expertise is the only ingredient that matters. You bring knowledge and a goal. Your audience brings its own prior knowledge, its expectations, and its needs, which may not match what you assume going in. And the occasion brings its own logic — why this talk is happening now, in this room or on this platform, in this amount of time, for this particular reason. A talk that's accurate and well-prepared can still miss badly if it ignores what the audience already knows, or misjudges what the occasion actually calls for. Worth asking yourself, before you prepare anything else: if I only got to choose one of "know my content," "know my audience," or "know my occasion" to get right, which would matter most for this specific talk? The answer isn't always the same, which is itself useful to notice.

### Are all speeches the same?

Clearly not, though it's tempting — especially early on — to reach for one general-purpose template and apply it everywhere. A wedding toast, a team status update, and a conference keynote share an underlying skill set: attention to audience, clarity of message, competent delivery. But they differ substantially in purpose, tone, expected length, and what "success" even looks like. A toast that succeeds by being warm and brief would fail badly as a keynote; a keynote that succeeds by being ambitious and wide-ranging would feel wildly out of place as a toast. Rather than asking "how do I give a good speech" as a single question, it's more useful to ask "what does a good version of this speech, in this context, actually look like" — and to let that answer, rather than a fixed template, drive your preparation.

### A framework for checking your work: the 6 Keys

One durable way of thinking about what makes a presentation succeed or fail is to break it into six components, each of which represents a distinct way a talk can go wrong even when the others are handled well. This unit will use these six terms throughout the course, so it's worth sitting with each one rather than skimming past the list:

- **Purpose** — the specific change you're aiming for in your audience by the end of the talk. Not "the topic," but the outcome: a decision made, a belief shifted, an action taken.
- **Content** — the material itself, but specifically the material that's relevant and useful to this audience, which is usually a subset of everything you know or could say.
- **Structure** — the order and shape the content takes, so the audience can follow your reasoning rather than simply receive a list of facts.
- **Language** — word choice suited to spoken delivery and real-time listening, which behaves differently from language written to be read and re-read.
- **Delivery** — the physical and vocal execution: voice, pacing, body language, presence.
- **Visual Aids** — slides, props, or other visual material, used to reinforce the spoken message rather than to replace or duplicate it.

It's worth being honest that this is a diagnostic lens rather than a strict sequence — you won't always work through the six in this order, and different talks will lean harder on some keys than others. A short informal update might live or die on Delivery and barely touch Visual Aids; a technical conference talk might succeed or fail almost entirely on Structure. The value of the framework isn't that it prescribes one process, but that it gives you six separate places to check when something about a talk isn't working, rather than vaguely sensing "this isn't landing" without being able to say why.

Think about a presentation you sat through recently that didn't quite work. Try running it against these six keys — was the purpose actually clear, or was the speaker really just "covering the topic"? Was the content right for that audience, or pitched for a different one? Often you'll find the weak key fairly quickly once you have the vocabulary to look for it.

### Purpose and audience analysis change shape online

Two of the six keys — Purpose, and the audience analysis that feeds directly into Content and Structure — deserve specific reconsideration when a talk moves online, because it's a mistake to treat an online audience as simply an in-person audience that happens to be viewing through a screen. In a room, you have a continuous, if imperfect, feedback loop: you can see confused expressions, restless shifting, or genuine engagement, and adjust your pacing or emphasis accordingly, often without consciously deciding to. Online, most of that signal disappears — cameras may be off, reactions are delayed or absent, and you're competing directly with email, chat, and the general fatigue that comes from back-to-back video calls.

This doesn't mean the underlying purpose of a talk needs to change, but it does mean your assumptions about attention and retention should. It's reasonable to assume shorter sustained focus, more fragmented attention, and a real risk of losing people silently, with no visible cue that it's happening. Some presenters respond by building in more frequent structural signposting online, or shorter segments with more explicit checkpoints — not because the content is different, but because the audience's capacity to track a long unbroken argument is often lower in that setting. The practical habit worth building now, at the start of the course, is to ask explicitly for every talk you prepare: is this in person or on a screen, and what does that actually change about what this audience can take in? Different formats will call for different answers, and there's no single fixed adjustment that covers every case — it's a question to ask fresh each time, not a rule to apply mechanically.

### Activity: Presentation history inventory and goal-setting

Set aside fifteen to twenty minutes for this, ideally at the very start of the course, since the value of the exercise partly comes from having something concrete to look back on later.

1. Write down the presentations you've given so far, in any context — work, school, community, informal. If your experience is limited, three or four is a perfectly reasonable list; if you've given many, pick the ones that stand out for good or bad reasons.
2. For each one, reflect honestly on what worked and what didn't. Push past generic answers ("it went fine") toward something specific enough to be useful ("I lost people around the third slide because I'd stopped looking up from my notes").
3. Look across the whole list for a pattern rather than treating each talk as an isolated event. Is there a strength that keeps showing up? A weakness? Patterns across several talks are usually more diagnostic than any single talk's outcome, since a one-off stumble might just be circumstance, while a repeated one usually points at something worth deliberately working on.
4. Write one personal goal for the course — a single sentence describing what you want to be genuinely different about your presenting by the end. Try to make it specific enough that you'd know if you'd hit it, rather than something as broad as "be more confident."
5. Share your pattern and your goal with a partner or small group, and listen to theirs in return. This isn't just a social warm-up — hearing someone else narrate their presentation history often surfaces something useful about your own that you hadn't quite put into words, and starting the course by speaking out loud, even briefly, is itself a small first rep of the habit you're here to build.

Hold onto this inventory. Later units will ask you to return to it, both to track whether the pattern you identified is shifting and to check your goal against what you're actually practicing.

# Unit 2: Structure of a Speech (+ Theme vs. Message)

## Long

### The Three-Part Structure

If you've sat through many talks, you've probably noticed that the ones which stick with you tend to have a shape you could redraw from memory afterward — not because the content was simple, but because the structure did some of the remembering for you. The classic three-part shape for a speech — Introduction, Body, Conclusion — is often summarized as "say what you're going to say, say it, then say what you said," and the first time you hear that phrase it can sound almost too repetitive to be good advice. But the repetition is deliberate, and it's solving a real problem: unlike a reader, a listener can't flip back a page to check what they missed, so the structure has to do the work a table of contents and an index would otherwise do.

The **Introduction** carries more weight than its short length suggests. Its job is to state your message clearly and preview the points that will support it, and doing this early matters because audiences are, consciously or not, deciding in the first thirty seconds or so whether this talk is worth their full attention. There's a temptation to warm up first — a joke, some context, a bit of throat-clearing before getting to the point — and there's nothing inherently wrong with a little of that, but it's worth being honest with yourself about whether it's serving the audience or just easing your own nerves. If it's the latter, consider trimming it.

The **Body** is where the previewed points actually get developed, and its main structural challenge is one that print doesn't have: a listener can't see a paragraph break or a bolded heading, so you have to build those transitions out loud. Verbal signposting — "my second point is...", "that brings me to..." — sounds mechanical in isolation, but it's doing real cognitive work for your audience, giving them a place to "file" what they're about to hear before they hear it.

The **Conclusion** restates the message and briefly reviews the supporting points, and it's tempting to treat this as pure repetition — haven't they already heard this? They have, but audiences reliably remember openings and closings better than the middle of a talk, a pattern that shows up across a lot of everyday listening, not just formal speeches. So using your conclusion to say the message one more time isn't padding; it's placing your central point where it's most likely to actually land and stay.

It's worth being honest that this three-part shape isn't the only way to structure a talk — a narrative arc, a problem-solution frame, or a chronological account can all work well depending on the occasion. But as a default, especially for shorter or more formal talks, the three-part structure earns its place because it's built specifically around how spoken information gets lost and how repetition helps recover it.

### Theme vs. Message

One of the more common reasons a speech falls flat isn't a delivery problem at all — it's that the speaker never actually settled on a message, and was really only ever talking about a theme. The distinction is worth being precise about, because it's easy to think you've done the work of finding a message when you've really just described your topic more specifically.

The **theme** is the subject area — "remote work," "the new expense policy," "customer retention." The **message** is your specific opinion or proposal about that theme, and the defining feature of a real message is that it contains evaluative content: a stance someone could push back on, a recommendation, a judgment call. "I want to talk about customer retention" is a theme wearing the clothes of an introduction — it tells the audience the topic but gives them nothing to actually agree or disagree with.

A useful test, and one worth applying to your own speeches before you build them out: could a reasonable person disagree with this statement? "Our retention rate is 62%" fails the test — it's a fact, and facts aren't up for debate. "Our 62% retention rate means we're underinvesting in onboarding" passes it, because that's an interpretation, and someone in the room might genuinely think the real problem is pricing, not onboarding. That disagreement potential isn't a flaw in the message — it's what makes it a message rather than a data point.

Think about a talk you've sat through recently that felt like it never quite arrived anywhere, even though the speaker clearly knew the subject. There's a decent chance what was missing wasn't information — it was a message. All the theme-level material was there; nobody ever committed to a position on it.

### Online Delivery: Pacing and Front-Loading

Delivering a speech through a screen changes the structural calculus in ways that are worth thinking through rather than just applying as a checklist. The core issue is attrition: a viewer on a video call or a recording can disengage with almost no social cost — muting a tab, checking a second screen, or simply tuning out doesn't carry the visible friction that walking out of a physical room does. That means the patient build-up that might work fine in person is a riskier bet online, where you may be losing part of your audience before you've even stated your point.

The practical implication is to front-load the message more aggressively than you might in a live setting — getting it into your opening sentence or two rather than easing into it through context-setting. This isn't a universal rule so much as a reflection of where the risk actually sits: an in-person audience that's committed to sitting in a room is a more captive audience than one on a call, so the online context simply raises the cost of a slow start.

Pacing through the body deserves the same reconsideration. It's often worth trimming to two well-developed points online rather than three, not because three points are inherently too many, but because each transition on video is a small moment where attention can drift, and a screen doesn't carry the same ambient momentum a live room does — no shared energy in the space, no visible reactions from nearby audience members to keep you anchored. None of this means the three-part structure changes; it means the online context tightens how patiently you can afford to move through it.

Worth reflecting on: think about a video presentation you found yourself drifting away from. Was it the content, or was it that you didn't know, twenty or thirty seconds in, what the speaker was actually arguing? That's usually a front-loading problem, not a content problem, and it's a useful thing to check for in your own recorded or live-online talks before you assume the issue is something else entirely.

### Activity: Critique, Draft, and Peer Review

**Step 1 — Critique.** Theme: "Employee training budgets." Look at the three candidate messages below and think through which one actually works as a message, and why the other two don't quite get there.

- A) "Training budgets are a topic worth discussing."
- B) "We spent a significant amount on training this year."
- C) "We should double the training budget, because it cut our error rate by 20% this year."

Option C holds up as a genuine message — it takes a specific position (double the budget) and attaches evaluative reasoning to it (the error-rate outcome), which means someone could reasonably push back on it. A and B, by contrast, are both theme dressed up as message: A gestures at the topic without committing to anything, and B states a fact that nobody could meaningfully disagree with. Worth noticing: B is the trickier of the two failures, because it feels substantive — it has a number in it — even though it doesn't actually take a position.

**Step 2 — Build your own theme/message pair.** Pick a theme from your own work context and write two versions: a theme-only statement, and a real message that takes an evaluative position on it. Run it through the disagreement test from earlier in this unit — could someone reasonably argue the other side? If not, it's probably still a theme.

**Step 3 — Draft your outline.** Using the self-introduction model in Appendix A as a reference point rather than a template to copy exactly, draft your own Introduction/Body/Conclusion outline for a self-introduction:

- **Introduction:** your message, plus a preview of the two or three points that support it.
- **Body:** each point, developed enough to support the message without wandering.
- **Conclusion:** a restatement of the message and a brief review of your points.

**Step 4 — Peer review.** Swap outlines with a partner, if you're working through this with someone else, and read each other's drafts specifically for the theme-vs-message distinction: does the introduction actually state a message, or does it describe a theme and leave the position implicit? It's a common gap even once you know the difference in theory — people often draft a perfectly reasonable-sounding introduction that never quite commits. Give each other one piece of feedback on whether the message is genuinely evaluative, and one on whether the body's points visibly trace back to it. If you're working alone, set your draft aside for a few minutes and reread it cold, asking the same two questions as if it were someone else's.

# Unit 3: Flag Expressions

## Long

### What a Flag Expression Is, and Why It Matters More Than It Sounds Like It Should

There's a useful image behind the term "Flag Expression": think of a tour guide walking through a crowded place, holding up a small flag so the group can track where the guide is and stay together. Take the flag away, and the group doesn't necessarily get lost immediately — but it starts to drift, second-guess which way to go, and lose confidence that it's actually still following. A Flag Expression does the same job inside a speech: it's a short phrase that marks a structural transition — moving from the introduction into the body, moving from one point to the next, signaling that you're heading into the conclusion — so that your audience always knows where they are in the talk without having to work it out themselves.

It's worth pausing on why this matters more in spoken language than in writing. A reader who loses track of your argument's structure can flip back a page, glance at a heading, or reread a paragraph. A listener can't do any of that — once a sentence is spoken, it's gone, and if they miss the moment you moved from your first point to your second, they may spend the next thirty seconds quietly trying to figure out whether you're still on point one or already somewhere else. That's attention spent on bookkeeping instead of on your content. Flag Expressions exist to prevent that: they do a small amount of verbal "housekeeping" so the audience's attention stays on what you're actually saying, not on where you are in saying it.

Think about a talk you've listened to recently where you genuinely lost the thread — not because the content was too hard, but because you weren't sure whether the speaker was still developing the same idea or had quietly moved to a new one. There's a decent chance the underlying problem was exactly this: missing or unclear signposting, not missing content.

### The Flag Expression Table, Organized by Part

Flag Expressions aren't a single undifferentiated list — they're organized by which part of the speech they serve, because the Introduction, Body, and Conclusion are each doing different structural work and need different kinds of signals.

**Part 1: Introduction**

| Purpose | Flag Expression |
|---|---|
| Appreciate the introduction | "Thank you for the introduction, M. Chair." |
| Greet the audience | "Good evening, everybody." |
| Give your theme | "Today, I am going to tell you about..." |
| Preview your main points | "I have three main points. First... Second... Third..." |

**Part 2: Body**

| Purpose | Flag Expression |
|---|---|
| Start the body | "Let me begin with my first point." |
| Move to the second point | "Now, I'll move on to my second point." |
| Move to the third point | "Finally, I will tell you my third point." |

**Part 3: Conclusion**

| Purpose | Flag Expression |
|---|---|
| Signal the review | "In conclusion, let me review my main points." |
| Review the points | "First... Then... Finally, I talked about..." |
| State your message | "I think that..." |
| Close | "Thank you. And now, I will be happy to answer your questions." |

Treat this table as a starting vocabulary rather than a rigid script. Once these patterns feel natural, you'll likely start varying the exact wording — "turning now to," "that brings me to," "the second thing I want to cover is" — without losing the underlying function. The specific words matter less than the fact that some clear signal is present at each transition.

### Three Usage Errors Worth Understanding, Not Just Memorizing

These three points come up often enough in practice that they're worth slowing down on — not because the rules themselves are complicated, but because the underlying reasoning helps you apply them correctly in cases the table doesn't cover directly.

**"First, second, third" belongs to the Introduction and Conclusion — not the Body — and the reason is about what the words are actually doing.** In the Introduction, you're previewing a list ("I have three main points: first... second... third..."), and in the Conclusion you're reviewing that same list. In both cases, "first/second/third" is functioning as a counting device attached to a list the audience already knows the shape of. But once you're inside the Body actually delivering point two, "second" no longer has an obvious referent — second what? Your second point, or the second example within your first point? The ambiguity is exactly why the Body calls for transition expressions instead ("Now, I'll move on to my second point," "Finally, I will tell you my third point") — they signal forward motion without depending on the audience holding a numbered list in their head while you're mid-explanation.

**"At first" is not a more formal version of "firstly," even though it's tempting to reach for it that way.** The two phrases look related, but they're doing grammatically different jobs. "First" (or "firstly") counts items in a list. "At first" introduces a state of affairs that is about to change — it sets up a contrast, typically completed with "but" or "however": "At first it was difficult for me to speak English, but after a while it became much easier." If you use "at first" to introduce your first main point, you've accidentally set up an expectation of contrast that your speech never resolves, which can leave an attentive listener waiting for a "but" that isn't coming.

**"Let's" reads as informal, and formal proposals or conclusions generally need a different construction.** "Let's take a short break" or "Let's get started" are perfectly natural in a relaxed moment — nobody would flag them as wrong. But "Let's improve our products and advertising," used as the closing recommendation of a formal talk, tends to undercut the seriousness of the proposal it's carrying; it reads more like a casual suggestion than a considered recommendation. Something like "I think we need to improve our products and advertising" carries the same content with the register the moment actually calls for. The judgment call here is really about formality level, not a hard grammar rule — in a genuinely informal setting, "let's" might be exactly right, so it's worth reading the room rather than banning the word outright.

One more point on register, which applies throughout this table rather than to any single line: where the older source material used "Mr. Chairman," this unit uses the gender-neutral "M." (M. Chair, M. Chairperson) instead. The logic is simple — a Flag Expression is a piece of functional language, and there's no reason for functional language to embed an assumption about who's in the room or who's presiding.

### Activity: Drill, Apply, Then Listen for It

**Step 1 — Drill.** Fill in each blank with the correct Flag Expression from the table above, and be ready to explain *why* that expression belongs there — which part of the speech it's in, and whether the moment calls for a counting word or a transition.

1. "___, I am going to tell you about our new onboarding process." *(Introduction — theme)*
2. "I have two main points. ___, I will cover the timeline. ___, I will cover the cost." *(Introduction — preview)*
3. "Let me begin with my first point. ___, I'll move on to my second point." *(Body — transition, not a counting word)*
4. "In conclusion, let me review my main points. ___, I talked about the timeline. ___, I talked about the cost." *(Conclusion — review)*
5. Rewrite this so it fits a formal recommendation: "___ improve our onboarding process."

**Answers:** 1. Today 2. First / Second 3. Now 4. First / Finally 5. "I think we need to improve our onboarding process."

**Step 2 — Apply Flag Expressions to your own outline.** Go back to the Introduction/Body/Conclusion outline you drafted for Unit 2's activity and work through it turn by turn, inserting a Flag Expression at each structural transition — the opening greeting and theme statement, the preview of your points, the move into each Body point, and the review and message in your Conclusion. Read it aloud once the expressions are in place. If a phrase from the table feels stilted in your specific outline, that's worth noticing rather than ignoring — try an alternative wording that keeps the same function (signaling the same transition) while sounding like something you'd actually say.

**Step 3 — Flag-spotting: a timed listening exercise.** Find a recorded model speech (a conference talk, a formal address, or a recording of a classmate's or colleague's presentation works well) and listen to the first five minutes with a timer running. Every time you hear a Flag Expression — or notice a moment where one is clearly missing — jot down the timestamp and a one-line note. Afterward, look back over your notes and ask two questions: Did the speaker mostly use transition expressions in the body and counting words only in the introduction and conclusion, or did they blur the two the way the usage notes above warn against? And were there any moments where you, as a listener, briefly lost track of the structure — and if so, was a missing or mismatched Flag Expression the reason? This exercise is less about grading the speaker and more about training your own ear: the more Flag Expressions you can catch in real time, in someone else's speech, the more naturally you'll start supplying them in your own.

# Unit 4: Delivery Skills (incl. webcam/online basics)

## Long

### Introduction

By this point you've done the work of shaping what you're going to say — Unit 2 gave you a draft to work from, and the units around it dealt with structure and argument. This unit turns to something different: how you say it, and increasingly, how you say it through a screen. Delivery is easy to underrate, partly because it feels less substantial than content — surely what matters is the argument, not the hand gestures? — but audiences form judgments about credibility, confidence, and trustworthiness from delivery signals within the first few seconds, often before they've processed your first full sentence. It's worth taking that seriously without treating it as a performance you either nail or fail; delivery is a set of habits you can notice and adjust, the same way you'd revise a paragraph that wasn't landing.

This unit also folds in something that used to be treated as a separate topic: the mechanics of presenting online — platform controls, screen-share, recording, webcam framing. That separation never quite made sense, because a webcam presentation still involves your eye contact, your voice, your posture; the online layer changes how those things are expressed, not whether they matter. Think of the online mechanics as delivery skills wearing a different coat, not a different subject.

### Eye Contact

There's a common piece of advice that says to hold eye contact with one audience member for a full sentence before moving to the next — and it works, in the sense that it looks deliberate rather than scattered, but it isn't the only way to do this well. Some experienced speakers instead work the room in loose zones, letting their gaze rest on clusters of people rather than individuals, which can feel more natural if darting between specific faces makes you self-conscious. The point underneath either technique is the same: your eyes should be doing something intentional, not searching for an exit.

Online, eye contact gets stranger, because the thing that reads as eye contact to your audience — looking directly into the camera lens — feels, to you, like staring at a small circle of plastic instead of a person. Most speakers default to watching the screen, where the faces actually are, which is a reasonable instinct but tends to read to viewers as looking slightly down and away, more like someone reading notes than someone present in the conversation. The workaround most people land on is a compromise: look at the lens when you're making a key point or addressing the group directly, and allow yourself to glance at the gallery view between points, rather than trying to hold camera contact for an entire talk, which is fatiguing and, frankly, unnatural even for experienced presenters. Think about a video call you were on recently where the other person seemed present and engaged — was it really unbroken eye contact, or something closer to this rhythm of contact and release?

### Voice

Pace, pitch, and pause do a surprising amount of work in signaling confidence, often more than the content itself. Nerves tend to compress all three: speakers rush their pace, flatten their pitch into a monotone, and eliminate pauses because silence feels like exposure. None of these tendencies are moral failings — they're just default stress responses — but knowing that gives you something concrete to counteract rather than a vague instruction to "sound more confident."

Slowing your pace, particularly in your opening, does double duty: it reads as composed to the audience, and it genuinely calms your own physiology, since rushed speech and rushed breathing tend to reinforce each other. A falling pitch at the end of a sentence signals conviction, while a rising pitch — the "upspeak" pattern — can make a statement sound like a question you're not sure of yourself, even when the content is confident; this is worth listening for in a recording of yourself, since most people can't hear it happening live. And pauses are not empty space to be filled — a pause after a key point is doing rhetorical work, giving the audience room to register what you just said, which is a different function from the pause you take mid-thought while you're still formulating your next sentence. Both are legitimate, but conflating them (treating every pause as a mistake to be edited out) removes one of your more effective tools.

### Gestures

A reasonable default is to keep most of your gestures within a zone roughly between your waist and your chest, saving larger or more emphatic movement for genuine emphasis — a contrast, a turning point, a number worth landing. But treat that as a starting point rather than a rule to enforce rigidly on yourself; some speakers are naturally more expressive, and suppressing that entirely can read as stiff or withheld, which carries its own cost. The underlying principle is proportionality: gestures should scale with what you're saying, not run at a constant level regardless of content.

On webcam, the physical math changes because the frame is much tighter than a stage or a room. A gesture that would read as natural and visible in person can clip out of the shot entirely, or, conversely, look oversized in close-up because the camera is so much closer to you than a live audience would be. It's worth testing your own framing on a practice call and gesturing as you normally would, just to see what actually survives the frame — you may find you need to gesture smaller and closer to your body than instinct suggests, or reposition your camera slightly wider to give yourself room.

### Posture

In person, posture that's evenly balanced — weight on both feet, no locked knees, no rocking — tends to read as steady, while shifting or swaying reads as nervous energy even to viewers who couldn't articulate why. Online, the same signal has to be communicated from the waist up, since that's all the camera shows: sitting upright and leaning slightly toward the camera does the work that standing tall does in a room, while slouching back into your chair reads, somewhat unfairly, as disengagement even if you're perfectly focused. It's a good example of how the underlying principle (project steadiness and engagement) stays constant while its physical expression has to be translated for the medium.

### Notes-Handling

Cue cards with key words, rather than a full script, tend to serve most speakers better, because a full script invites you to read rather than speak, which flattens your vocal delivery and breaks the eye contact patterns discussed above. That said, a full script isn't always wrong — for a legally sensitive statement, or a moment where exact wording genuinely matters, reading verbatim may be the right call, and worth doing openly rather than pretending you're speaking off the cuff. For most presentations, though, treat your notes as a memory aid you glance at between points, not a document you narrate.

### Clothing

Dress-code advice used to collapse into a single instruction — wear a suit — that no longer reflects how varied presentation contexts actually are; a pitch to investors, a workshop with colleagues, and a conference talk to peers in your field call for different registers, and a rule built around one specific setting doesn't generalize well to the others. A more durable principle is to read the room you're walking into and dress slightly more formally than its baseline, which signals that you've made an effort without looking like you've misjudged the occasion. Worth asking yourself before any given talk: what would the most senior, credible person in this audience be wearing, and am I roughly matching or slightly exceeding that?

On webcam, the practical constraint is technical rather than social: solid colors and simple patterns render more reliably than fine stripes or busy prints, which can shimmer or distort under video compression. It's a small thing, but worth checking on an actual test call rather than assuming your normal wardrobe translates well to camera.

### Platform Controls, Screen-Share, and Recording

The mechanical layer of online presenting — muting, screen-share, recording — can feel like a separate skill from delivery, something closer to IT competence than presentation craft, but it's worth reframing: fumbling with your screen-share is a delivery failure in the same way that losing your place in your notes is, because it breaks the audience's confidence in you regardless of the cause. Treating platform mechanics as part of your rehearsal, not an afterthought handled five minutes before you go live, is the difference between these failures showing up in the room and being caught in practice instead.

A few things are worth building into that rehearsal: confirming your actual camera and microphone are selected (not a leftover device from your last call), staying muted until you're speaking, and knowing exactly where your screen-share control lives so you're not hunting for it while an audience waits. When you do share your screen, sharing a specific window rather than your full desktop limits what can go wrong — a stray notification or an unrelated tab suddenly become impossible to accidentally expose. And recording your session, where the context allows it, is worth doing as a matter of course, not just for high-stakes talks — it's the raw material for the self-review pass in this unit's activity, and useful well beyond it.

Backup planning deserves the same seriousness as your primary setup: a second device, a mobile hotspot, a PDF of your slides you could talk through even if screen-share failed entirely. None of this is about assuming the worst will happen — it's closer to the logic of rehearsing your opening line so you're not searching for it live: preparation that you hope not to need, but that changes how calm you are even if you never use it.

### Activity: Peer Observation, Webcam Self-Review, and a Second Round

This activity works from your Unit 2 draft and asks you to examine your delivery from two different vantage points — a partner's observation and your own recorded playback — before applying what you learned and trying again.

1. Working with a partner, deliver 60 seconds of your Unit 2 draft while they complete the full delivery-skills observation checklist, covering eye contact, voice, gestures, posture, notes-handling, and clothing.
2. Swap roles and observe your partner's 60 seconds using the same checklist.
3. Individually, record yourself delivering the same 60 seconds on webcam, then watch the recording back and complete the checklist again — this time against yourself.
4. Compare all three data points: your partner's live observation, your own recorded self-review, and whatever gap exists between them. It's worth pausing here and thinking about which gaps surprised you — the things a partner caught that you didn't notice in your own recording often point to habits that are invisible to you specifically, not delivery flaws in general.
5. Choose one or two specific adjustments based on that feedback — not a wholesale rebuild of your delivery, which is hard to execute in a single pass, but a targeted change or two you can actually hold in mind.
6. Deliver the same 60 seconds again, either to your partner or on webcam, applying those adjustments, and reassess using the checklist one more time.

The second round is doing something different from the first: the first pass is about noticing what's actually happening in your delivery, often for the first time, while the second is about testing whether a specific, chosen adjustment actually changes what an observer — or your own recorded self — perceives. That's a more realistic model of how delivery actually improves over time: not a single moment of correction, but a repeated cycle of noticing, adjusting, and checking again.

# Unit 5: The Logic Tree

## Long

### The Logic Tree

There's a reason so many presentations feel like they're wandering rather than building toward something: the speaker started writing sentences before they'd actually settled the shape of the talk. The Logic Tree is a way of getting around that — it's a visual planning structure you fill out before you draft any actual speech language, so the organizational work happens separately from, and before, the wordsmithing. It's worth noting up front that this is a planning tool rather than a delivery technique, which means it works identically whether you're speaking in a room or presenting on a screen; nothing about the medium changes what belongs in the tree.

The tree has five parts, and the metaphor is doing real work here — it's not just a cute label, because each part genuinely depends on the one beneath it, the way an actual tree's structure depends on its roots.

- **Roots** are your preparation: the audience research, the purpose statement, the background reading you've done before you start shaping content. Everything above this level draws on what you've dug up here, which means shallow roots tend to produce a shaky tree regardless of how polished the branches look.
- **Trunk** is your theme, compressed into a single line — the one idea the entire talk is actually in service of. A good test of the trunk is whether every branch you eventually add can visibly trace back to it; if a branch doesn't connect to the trunk, it usually doesn't belong in this talk, however interesting it is on its own.
- **Branches** are your main points, and three tends to be the number that works best, though it's worth understanding why rather than treating it as an arbitrary rule. Two branches often leaves a talk feeling underdeveloped or like it's missing an obvious third angle; four or more starts to tax what an audience can actually retain by the end, since most people struggle to hold more than a handful of distinct points in memory once the talk is over. Three isn't a law of nature, but it's a reasonable default, and departing from it should be a deliberate choice based on your specific content rather than an accident of not having narrowed things down.
- **Leaves** are the supporting material attached to each branch — examples, numbers, quotes, short anecdotes. A branch with no leaves is really just an assertion, and audiences are generally more skeptical of assertions than speakers expect; the leaves are what convert "trust me" into something a listener can actually evaluate.
- **Fruit** is the takeaway — the thing the audience is actually meant to walk away holding. It's worth writing this last rather than first, even though it feels like the "point" of the talk and therefore the natural starting place, because you often don't know exactly what your fruit should say until you can see the whole tree assembled and notice what it actually adds up to.

Working the tree in order — roots, then trunk, then branches, then leaves, then fruit — tends to produce a more coherent result than starting with your conclusion and reverse-engineering support for it, if only because it forces you to let the evidence and audience needs shape the message, rather than the other way around. That said, plenty of experienced speakers work the tree non-linearly in practice, sketching a rough trunk early and revising it once the branches reveal what the talk is really about. Treat the bottom-up order as the reliable default for a first pass, not an ironclad rule.

### Blank template

Here's the template in its bare form, worth keeping on hand for any talk you're about to plan:

```
FRUIT (the one takeaway):

BRANCH 1:                BRANCH 2:                BRANCH 3:
  Leaf —                   Leaf —                   Leaf —
  Leaf —                   Leaf —                   Leaf —

TRUNK (one-line theme):

ROOTS (audience, purpose, research notes):
```

### Worked example: a product launch

Templates are easier to trust once you've seen one filled in, so here's a populated tree for a talk introducing a new budgeting app to a room of small-business owners — a fairly ordinary product-launch scenario, chosen because most of the underlying moves transfer to other topics without much translation.

**Roots:** The audience runs small businesses and is generally frustrated with spreadsheet-based bookkeeping — this matters because it tells you what pain point to lead with. The purpose of the talk is to get attendees to sign up for a free trial before they leave the room, which is a concrete, checkable goal rather than a vague aim like "raise awareness."

**Trunk:** "This app gives you your evenings back." Notice this isn't a feature description — it's a promise about what changes in the audience's life, which is generally a stronger trunk than something like "introducing our new budgeting software," because it's built around the audience's stake in the outcome rather than the speaker's product.

**Branch 1 — Speed.** The app is fast to adopt and fast to use day to day.
- Leaf: Setup takes about four minutes from download to first use.
- Leaf: Bank data syncs automatically, removing manual entry entirely.

**Branch 2 — Accuracy.** The app is more reliable than the spreadsheets it's replacing.
- Leaf: It was built with input from working accountants, not developers alone.
- Leaf: Beta testing showed 30% fewer errors than manual spreadsheet tracking.

**Branch 3 — Affordability.** The cost is easy to justify against the alternative.
- Leaf: $12 a month, with no long-term contract.
- Leaf: Less than the cost of one hour of a bookkeeper's time.

**Fruit:** "Try it free for 30 days — sign up before you leave the room today." Notice how directly this loops back to the trunk's promise of getting evenings back: the fruit isn't a summary of the three branches so much as a call to action that cashes out the theme.

It's worth pausing on why this particular tree holds together: each branch is backed by two leaves rather than left as a bare claim, and an audience skeptical of "it's accurate" is far more likely to be moved by "30% fewer errors in beta testing" than by the adjective alone. That's really the functional purpose of the leaves — they're what an audience actually evaluates, even though the branches are what they'll remember.

Think for a moment about a talk you've sat through where the speaker's points felt unsupported — where you found yourself thinking "says who?" That's usually a leafless branch: a claim with nothing underneath it. It's a useful diagnostic to carry into your own planning, not just something to notice in others.

### Activity: Build your own tree — and try a tree swap

The Appendix B model is worth looking at closely before you start: it contains two full worked examples — a business speech and a government one — which between them show how the same five-part shape adapts to fairly different purposes and audiences. Use them to calibrate depth and tone, not as a script to imitate line for line.

1. Choose a topic for a talk you could genuinely give — something you're actually preparing is more useful here than a purely hypothetical exercise, since real stakes tend to sharpen your choices.
2. Copy the blank template and complete it yourself, working from Roots up through Fruit rather than starting with a conclusion and working backward.

```
FRUIT (the one takeaway):

BRANCH 1:                BRANCH 2:                BRANCH 3:
  Leaf —                   Leaf —                   Leaf —
  Leaf —                   Leaf —                   Leaf —

TRUNK (one-line theme):

ROOTS (audience, purpose, research notes):
```

3. Default to three branches, but treat that as a strong default rather than a fixed rule — if your content genuinely resolves into two sharper branches or a well-justified four, that's a legitimate call as long as you can explain why. What matters more than hitting exactly three is that each branch earns its place by connecting back to the trunk.
4. Give every branch at least two leaves. If you find yourself stuck for leaves on a branch, that's often a sign the branch itself is too abstract or too broad, and it's worth revisiting rather than pushing forward without support.
5. Once your tree is complete, swap it with a partner — not your notes, not an explanation, just the tree itself. Each of you should try to reconstruct a rough speech outline from the other's completed tree alone, without asking clarifying questions first.
6. Compare your reconstructed outline to what your partner actually intended. Where they match closely, that's a sign the tree communicated its own logic clearly. Where they diverge, it's worth asking why: was a branch label too vague, a leaf too disconnected from its branch, or the trunk too abstract to constrain what came above it?

The swap is doing more work than it might first appear. A tree you can only understand once its author explains it out loud hasn't actually done its job as a planning document — it's really just a private note to yourself. A tree that a partner can pick up cold and turn into a reasonably accurate outline is one that will also serve you well later, when you come back to your own tree a week from now and need it to still make sense.

Keep the finished tree. The next unit builds your full speech outline directly from it, so time spent now getting the branches and leaves right — rather than vague or overlapping — pays off directly in how smoothly that next step goes.

# Unit 6: Slide Design (Visual Aids)

## Long

### Why slide design deserves its own discipline

It's easy to treat slide design as a cosmetic afterthought — something you tidy up once the "real" work of writing the talk is done — but that gets the relationship backwards. A slide is a visual channel running in parallel with your spoken one, and when it's poorly designed, it doesn't just look unpolished; it actively competes with you for the audience's attention. Read too much text, and people default to reading rather than listening, since reading is the less effortful of the two. The goal of slide design, then, isn't decoration — it's traffic control: making sure the visual channel supports what you're saying rather than racing ahead of it or drowning it out.

Two rules do most of the load-bearing work here, and it's worth understanding not just what they say but why they hold up as well as they do.

### The Rule of 1

The Rule of 1 says that each slide should contain one idea, and no more. On the surface this looks almost too simple to be a "rule" — of course a slide should have a point — but in practice it's routinely violated, usually because a presenter is trying to be efficient by grouping related points together on one slide to save space in the deck. The trouble is that "related" and "the same idea" aren't the same thing: putting three sub-points on one slide is still asking the audience to track three separate things at once, just under a single header.

A more useful test than "does this feel related?" is: if you had to remove everything except one sentence, which sentence would survive? Whatever's left is your one idea; anything else belongs on a different slide, or in your spoken delivery rather than on the screen at all. It might feel wasteful to spend a whole slide on what seems like a small point, but a longer deck of clean, single-idea slides is almost always easier to follow than a shorter deck where each slide is doing double or triple duty.

### The Rule of 7

The Rule of 7 — no more than seven lines per slide, no more than seven words per line — is worth treating as a guardrail rather than gospel. There's nothing magical about the number seven specifically; you'll find it stated as "six by six" or "five by five" in other sources, and the exact figure matters less than the underlying discipline it enforces: keep text sparse enough that the slide functions as a visual anchor for what you're saying, not a transcript of it.

Where this gets interesting is in the exceptions. A slide with a single striking image and no text at all isn't violating the Rule of 7 — it's honoring the spirit of it even more completely, since zero lines is well under the ceiling. Conversely, a slide with exactly seven short lines can still fail if those seven lines are dense with jargon or numbers that need to be parsed rather than glanced at. So treat the rule as a proxy for cognitive load rather than a literal line-counting exercise, and when you're right at the edge of the limit, ask yourself whether the slide would still make sense to someone glancing at it for three seconds — because that's roughly how long you have their eyes before they should be back on you.

### The four-part structure

Most well-organized decks share a recognizable shape, and it's worth building to it deliberately rather than backing into it:

1. **Title** — establishes what the talk is about and often who's giving it.
2. **Index** — previews the sections ahead, giving the audience a mental map they can return to if they lose the thread partway through.
3. **Content** — the body of the talk, built slide by slide under the Rule of 1 and Rule of 7.
4. **Conclusion** — compresses everything down to the one thing you want the audience to walk away remembering.

You don't have to follow this shape slavishly — a very short, informal talk might not need a separate index slide, and a highly visual talk might fold the conclusion into a final image rather than a bullet-point summary. But it's a useful default to deviate from consciously rather than by accident, and building your deck in this order (rather than diving straight into content) tends to surface structural problems early, while they're still cheap to fix.

### Typography, color, and the templates you choose

Font and color choices are where a lot of presenters either over-invest (chasing a "designed" look) or under-invest (defaulting to whatever the software suggests) without thinking about what the choice is actually for. The functional goal is legibility at a distance or at a reduced size, so a clean sans-serif font, used consistently across the deck, will generally serve you better than a decorative typeface that looks distinctive up close but blurs into illegibility from the back of a room. Two fonts per deck — one for headings, one for body text — is a reasonable ceiling; beyond that, the deck starts to look like it lacks a design decision rather than reflecting one.

Color deserves more thought than most presenters give it, and this is a place where the older conventional wisdom on slide design — much of it written before accessibility was treated as a first-class concern — falls short. A meaningful proportion of any audience has some form of color vision deficiency, most commonly affecting the ability to distinguish red from green, which happens to be one of the most common color pairings presenters reach for (red for "bad," green for "good"). If that's the only signal carrying the meaning, a chunk of your audience simply won't receive it. The fix isn't to avoid color — it's to make sure meaning never depends on color alone: pair a color cue with a label, an icon, or a change in shape, and favor high-contrast combinations (dark text on light, or light on dark) over subtle tonal differences that look fine on your monitor but wash out on a projector or a compressed video stream.

Template choice follows the same logic. An elaborate template — heavy borders, background images, decorative gradients — might look considered in isolation, but on a live deck it competes with your content for attention, the same problem that too much text causes. Worth asking yourself here: does this template element help someone understand the content faster, or is it just visually busy? If you can't answer with a clear "it helps," it's probably worth cutting.

### Designing for two different rooms

Here's a distinction worth sitting with for a moment: a slide designed for in-room projection and a slide designed for a remote screen-share thumbnail are not automatically the same slide, even though presenters often treat them as interchangeable. In a physical room, your slide might be projected several meters wide, viewed by people sitting a comfortable distance away, often with the lights dimmed to improve contrast. In a remote meeting, that same slide might appear as one tile among several on a laptop screen, shrunk to a fraction of its original size, competing with your video feed, a chat panel, and whatever else is open on the viewer's desktop.

The practical upshot is that "legible" isn't a fixed property of a slide — it's relative to how and where it's going to be seen. Text that comfortably clears the Rule of 7 at full projector size can become an unreadable smear once compressed into a thumbnail. So it's worth building a habit of previewing your deck at reduced size — most presentation software offers a slide-sorter or grid view that approximates this — and treating anything that becomes illegible at that scale as a design problem to solve, not a minor inconvenience to shrug off. If you present regularly in both settings, you might even find yourself keeping two lightly different versions of a deck: one tuned for the room, one tuned for the screen. That's not overengineering — it's recognizing that the audience's actual viewing conditions are part of the design brief, not an afterthought bolted on at the end.

### A reflection before the activity

Think about a deck you've sat through recently that made you disengage — what specifically was on the slide when you stopped listening to the speaker and started reading instead? Was it too much text, poor contrast, a cluttered template, or something else? Naming the specific failure is often more useful than a general sense that "the slides were bad," because it gives you something concrete to check for in your own decks.

### Activity: Redesign a three-slide deck, twice

You'll be given a deliberately poor three-slide deck — dense text, low-contrast colors, no visible hierarchy between slides — and you'll redesign it twice, for two different viewing conditions.

**First pass — in-room projection.** Redesign the three slides as though they'll be projected on a large screen in a room where you're standing in front of the audience. Apply the Rule of 1 and Rule of 7 to break up dense content, fix any color combinations that would fail for a colorblind viewer, and make sure the deck maps cleanly onto the four-part structure (adding a title or index slide if needed).

**Second pass — remote screen-share thumbnail.** Now take the same three slides and redesign them again, this time assuming they'll be viewed as a small thumbnail in a video call alongside your webcam feed and a chat panel. Preview each slide at a reduced size as you work. You'll likely find you need to cut further than you did in the first pass, increase font size more aggressively, or simplify visual elements that read fine at full size but blur out when small.

When you're done, compare your two redesigns side by side. Where did they end up different, and why? Where did they end up the same? This comparison is the real point of the exercise: it surfaces which design choices are universal (like the Rule of 1) and which ones are genuinely dependent on how and where the slide will be seen.

# Unit 7: Chairing a Session

### Chairing a Session

There's a reasonable case that chairing is the hardest presentation-adjacent skill in this book, precisely because it looks like the easiest one. You're not the one giving the talk, you don't need slides, and on paper the job is just a few transitional sentences strung between other people's content. But those transitions are exactly where sessions go wrong — the fumbled introduction, the Q&A that either dies in silence or gets hijacked by one person, the ending that limps on for two extra minutes because nobody said the closing words. Chairing well means doing three things — introducing the speaker, managing questions and answers, and closing the session — and doing them so cleanly that the audience never really notices you did anything at all. That invisibility is the actual skill, and it's worth sitting with for a second: think about the last session you attended with a chair. Could you describe what they did? If you can't, they were probably good at it.

**Introducing the speaker**

The temptation when introducing someone is to be generous — to give a fuller account of their background, or even to preview a bit of what they're about to say, because it feels like a warm and helpful thing to do. Resist most of that instinct. An introduction has a narrow job: establish who this person is, why they're credible on this topic, and what they're about to talk about, then get out of the way as fast as is graceful. A workable shape is name, one line of relevant credential, the topic, and a clear handover phrase — something like "please welcome [name]" or "I'll hand it over to you" — and the discipline is mostly in what you leave out. If you find yourself summarizing their argument before they've made it, you've quietly taken something from them: the room's first exposure to that idea, which was supposed to be theirs to deliver.

**Managing questions and answers**

Q&A is the unscripted part of a session, and that's exactly why it needs the chair's attention more than the introduction or the close do — nobody has rehearsed what's about to happen. Opening it plainly ("we'll take questions now, who'd like to start?") signals the shift and invites the room in. From there the chair is doing several small jobs at once: choosing who speaks next rather than letting the most confident voice in the room dictate the order, repeating a question back in a large venue so the answer makes sense to people who didn't hear it asked, and — perhaps the trickiest one — turning a long, winding question into something answerable without making the questioner feel cut off. A line like "so if I can put that as: do you think X?" does that work, and it's worth practising because it's a genuine skill, not just a formula. As time runs down, a single clear signal — "we have time for one more question" — does a lot of work: it tells the room the window is closing without abruptly shutting it, and it gives whoever wants to ask something a fair last chance. It's also worth having a question of your own in your back pocket. Silence after "any questions?" is uncomfortable for everyone, and a chair who has one ready removes that risk entirely.

**Chairing online: the same job, extra controls**

Everything above still applies on a video call, but the mechanics multiply, because you're now managing attention across two channels — the visible room and the chat — plus a layer of platform controls that a live room doesn't have. It's worth naming the mechanism early, before anyone needs it: "if you'd like to ask something, use the raise-hand function and I'll bring you in," said once near the start, saves confusion later. When you do unmute someone, a short spoken acknowledgment — "you're unmuted, go ahead" — matters more online than it would in person, because without it people often aren't sure whether they're actually live. Chat questions raise a genuine design choice: do you read them aloud as they arrive, or batch them for a natural pause? Either can work, but whichever you choose, say the question out loud before the speaker answers it — anyone joining by audio only, or anyone who simply isn't tracking the chat, needs that context, and skipping it quietly excludes part of your audience. When hands and chat questions pile up together, which they often do, naming the queue out loud — "I've got two hands up and a couple in the chat, we'll go in order" — keeps things calm and visibly fair, which matters more online than you might expect, since participants can't see each other's body language to judge whether they're about to be overlooked.

**Closing the session**

Closings fail less often from getting something wrong than from leaving something out. A clean close needs three elements — thanks to the speaker, thanks to the audience, and an explicit signal that the session is over or what's coming next — and missing any one of them tends to leave the room in that slightly suspended state where people aren't sure if it's really finished. Something like "thank you, [name], for a genuinely useful session, and thanks to all of you for your questions — that brings us to a close" does all three in a single breath. It doesn't need embellishing, and in fact embellishing it — a long recap of everything covered — tends to undercut it, because it delays the one thing the room is actually waiting for, which is permission to stop paying attention.

### Activity: Full Session Cycle, With Live Video Practice

Work in small groups, and make sure every learner rotates through all three roles across the exercise — chairperson, speaker, and questioner — so nobody leaves having only watched someone else do the hard part.

- Draw on the Unit 3 phrase banks for this: the flag expressions there give the speaker a structure for a short talk, and the chairing phrases from this unit cover the introduction, the Q&A, and the close. Treat both as a starting toolkit rather than a script to recite verbatim — the point is to internalize the moves, not perform them woodenly.
- As chairperson, run one full cycle: introduce the speaker, open and manage a short Q&A (try to actually use the "we have time for one more question" signal, since it's a phrase that's easy to understand and surprisingly awkward to say naturally the first few times), and close using all three closing elements.
- As speaker, give a short mini-talk — two or three minutes — built on a flag-expression structure from Unit 3, then take questions.
- As questioner, ask at least one genuine question, and in one round, deliberately ask a long, unfocused one, so the chairperson gets real practice compressing it before handing it back to the speaker. This is uncomfortable to practice and that's rather the point — it's the moment that trips up chairs in real sessions.
- Because this is the Long-tier version of the activity, add a live video-call round: run one full cycle over a video platform, with the chairperson explicitly managing raise-hand and mute controls and reading out at least one planted "chat" question before the speaker responds. Afterwards, compare notes as a group — what felt different chairing on camera versus in person? Most learners find the mechanical layer (hands, mutes, chat) is the easy part to learn and the judgment calls — when to unmute, when to intervene, how to keep a chat question from feeling like an afterthought — are the part that actually takes practice.
- Close each rotation with brief peer feedback: was the introduction efficient without over-explaining, was the question queue handled fairly, did the close land cleanly, and — for the video round — did the online mechanics support the session rather than distract from it?

# Unit 8: Capstone Practice Activity (Timed Impromptu Circuit)

Every unit up to this point has isolated a single skill — the Logic Tree for structuring an argument, chairing for running a room, and whatever else you've picked up along the way — and let you practice it on its own, without much else competing for your attention. That's a reasonable way to learn a skill, but it's not a very good simulation of what presenting actually feels like, because in the real world nothing arrives one skill at a time. You get a topic you didn't choose, a clock you don't control, and questions you didn't prepare for, all at once. This unit is built to put you in that position on purpose, which is why it doesn't introduce anything new: it's a facilitation script for a timed impromptu circuit, and the circuit itself is the content.

**Why impromptu, and why timed.** It's worth pausing on why this exercise is structured the way it is rather than just running it because the plan says so. Working from a drawn, unseen topic tests something that rehearsed practice can't: whether you can organize a coherent response under real time pressure, using a structure you actually trust rather than one you've memorized for this specific talk. The clock matters for the same reason — a presenter who can only produce a good structure given unlimited prep time hasn't yet built the skill this course is aiming at, which is thinking clearly in front of people when the conditions aren't ideal. Think for a second about a time you were asked a question you hadn't prepared for, in a meeting or otherwise — what did you reach for instinctively? If the honest answer is "I panicked and rambled," this exercise is aimed squarely at you, and that's fine; it's what the circuit is for.

**Format: Two Rounds.**

*Round one* runs like this, and it's worth treating each step as a checkpoint rather than racing through it:

1. **Topic draw.** Blind, from a shared list. No trading, no peeking ahead — the constraint is the point.
2. **Prepare using the Logic Tree (Unit 5)**, in roughly two to three minutes. You'll notice the time pressure changes how you use the tree: there usually isn't room to consider five possible branches and pick the best three, so you commit early to a trunk claim and build outward from there. That's a feature of the exercise, not a flaw — real impromptu speaking rarely gives you the luxury of comparing alternatives.
3. **Present**, working from the tree rather than a script. Because the structural thinking is already done, this is where you can actually pay attention to delivery — pacing, eye contact, whether you're returning to your central point instead of drifting.
4. **Take questions**, at least two, run by the chair. This is arguably the part of the circuit that teaches the most, because it's the one moment where you can't fall back on preparation at all. How you recover from a question that catches you off guard says more about your presenting instincts than anything in the scripted portion did.
5. **Receive feedback**, structured by the chair as one strength and one area to change. Your job here is to listen, not to explain yourself — it's tempting to want to clarify why you made a choice, but that turns the feedback round into a defense, and it costs the group the time it needs to move to the next speaker.
6. **Rotate the chair.** The next learner takes over running the room, which means the chairing skills from Unit 7 — keeping time, managing the question queue, structuring feedback so it stays specific and usable — get exercised by everyone across the circuit, not just observed.

*Round two* uses a self-chosen topic instead of a drawn one, which is a deliberate change worth thinking about rather than skipping past. Presenting on a topic you picked is genuinely a different task from presenting on one you were handed: you already have opinions and material, so the interesting question isn't "can you generate content fast enough" but "can you still apply the same structure — trunk, branches, examples — when you're tempted to just talk, because you know the subject and structure feels less necessary." A lot of experienced speakers get looser exactly when they know their material best, which is often when structure matters most for the audience, even if it feels least necessary to the speaker. Round two is where you find out whether the Logic Tree is a genuine habit or something you only reach for under duress.

**On timing.** A full circuit runs about fifteen minutes per learner across both rounds combined, though groups vary in how tightly they can hold the clock, and that's worth naming rather than pretending the timings are exact. If a group runs long, the instinct is usually to cut the feedback round short to catch up — resist that. The prepare and present steps are useful, but the Q&A and feedback steps are where most of the actual learning in this unit happens, so if something has to give, look there last.

**A note on the chair's role.** Chairing this circuit isn't just administrative timekeeping, even though it can look that way from the outside. The chair decides, in real time, how firmly to enforce the clock, how to keep a rambling question from eating the round, and how to phrase feedback so it lands as useful rather than personal — all judgment calls, not a checklist. If you haven't chaired yet in this course, treat your turn in the rotation as its own mini-exercise in the skills from Unit 7, not just a break from being the speaker.

# Unit 9: Speaking Clearly (Pronunciation and Prosody)

## Long Tier

### Rethinking What "Clear Speech" Actually Means

It's worth starting with a distinction that a lot of presentation advice blurs: accent and clarity are not the same thing, and conflating them leads people toward the wrong fix. Accent is about which sounds you produce and how — the specific vowel quality, the particular consonant, the details that vary enormously depending on your first language and that audiences, in practice, adapt to within the first minute or two of listening. Clarity is mostly a different problem: it's about prosody, meaning the rhythm, pausing, and stress pattern that organizes speech into units a listener can process in real time. You can have a strong, easily identifiable accent and be extremely easy to follow, or you can have near-native pronunciation of every individual sound and still be exhausting to listen to because nothing is grouped, nothing is paced, and nothing signals which words matter. This unit is about the second problem, because it's the one that's actually fixable through practice in a way that transfers regardless of what your first language happens to be.

Think about a talk or lecture you found hard to follow recently, even though you understood every word individually — what was actually making it difficult? Often it's not vocabulary or accent at all; it's that the speaker never paused long enough for one idea to land before starting the next, or stressed every word with equal weight so nothing stood out as more important than anything else. That's a prosody problem, and it's worth diagnosing your own speech the same way before assuming the issue is your accent.

### Four Adjustments, and Why Each One Does Real Work

There are four adjustments worth building into how you speak in a presentation, and it's worth understanding what each one is actually doing for the listener rather than treating them as arbitrary rules.

**Speaking more slowly than feels natural** works because of an asymmetry between you and your audience: you already know what you're about to say, so every word feels obvious and immediate to you, while your listener is decoding it for the first time and needs more real time to do that. The pace that feels almost uncomfortably slow to you as the speaker is very often the pace that sounds normal, even engaging, from the audience's side. This is worth testing against a recording of yourself rather than trusting how it feels in the moment, because the gap between felt pace and actual pace tends to be larger than people expect.

**Lengthening the vowel in a stressed syllable slightly** — not dramatically, just perceptibly — gives the ear a fraction more time to register a word and simultaneously marks that word as important. It's a small technical move that does two jobs at once: it slows the word down locally without slowing your overall pace much, and it functions as a stress signal.

**Grouping words into short phrases separated by pauses** is arguably the highest-leverage of the four, because it's what lets a listener parse a sentence into manageable chunks instead of receiving it as one undifferentiated stream. The pauses roughly follow grammatical boundaries — after the subject, before a subordinate clause, around items in a list — and pausing between sentences, or between paragraphs of a talk, for slightly longer than feels natural gives the audience a moment to actually absorb one idea before the next one starts arriving. Skipping this is probably the single most common reason a technically fluent speaker is still hard to follow.

**Stressing the words that carry the information** — generally nouns, verbs, and numbers, rather than articles, prepositions, and auxiliary verbs — tells the listener where to direct their attention. Stress everything equally, or stress the wrong word in a sentence, and you've quietly handed the listener a harder job: they now have to work out on their own which word was the point.

None of these four is a fix for "sounding non-native" — they're adjustments that make spoken language easier to track for any listener, including native speakers listening to a native speaker who talks too fast with no pauses. Treating this as general spoken-clarity technique, not an accent-reduction exercise, is a more accurate — and considerably less discouraging — way to think about the work.

### A Notation System, and Why Writing It Down Matters

It's one thing to know intellectually that pausing and stress matter; it's another to actually produce them reliably under the pressure of standing in front of an audience, at which point most people's instincts default back to whatever pace and rhythm feels familiar. Marking your script up with an explicit notation turns these four adjustments from vague intentions into something you can rehearse and check against, which is why it's worth adopting even though it looks a little fussy on the page at first.

The notation uses four marks:

- **A single slash `/`** for a short pause, roughly comma-length, at a word-group boundary.
- **A double slash `//`** for a longer pause, between sentences or at a major structural shift — moving from your opening to your first point, for instance.
- **An underline** under a vowel, marking the syllable you intend to lengthen slightly.
- **Bold** on the word or words that carry primary stress in each phrase.

Here's a worked example:

> Th<u>i</u>s **quarter**, / our **sales** / **grew** by **twelve** perc<u>e</u>nt. // That's the **strongest** result / we've **seen** / in **three years**. //

Try reading that aloud slowly, actually pausing at each slash rather than glancing over it, and leaning slightly on the bolded words — then read the same sentence again with no marks, at your normal pace, and notice the difference. The marked version isn't just slower; it's organized into units the listener can process one at a time, with clear signals about which numbers and claims are the ones to remember.

A caveat worth flagging: this notation is a rehearsal tool, not a performance to recite mechanically. If you mark up a script and then deliver it exactly as marked with no variation, you'll sound stilted rather than clear — the point is to internalize the pattern through rehearsal so that pausing and stress become part of how you speak the material, not a set of instructions you're reading off in your head while talking.

### Online Delivery: Mic Technique and the Compression Problem

Presenting through a microphone — for a webinar, a recorded talk, or a video call — adds two considerations that don't come up when you're speaking to a room in person.

The first is straightforward mic technique: maintain a roughly consistent distance from the microphone, something like a hand's width for most laptop or headset mics, so your volume doesn't swing wildly as you move closer or further away. It's tempting to lean toward the mic for emphasis on an important point, the way you might lean toward an audience in person, but on most consumer mics that produces distortion rather than the intended effect — better to add emphasis through the stress and pausing you've already been practicing than through physical proximity to the microphone.

The second is less obvious: audio compression and network latency can clip or delay the very start of a word or sentence, which means a pause that's timed perfectly for an in-person audience can eat the first syllable of what comes next once it's been through a video-call codec. Building in a fractionally longer pause than you'd use in person — particularly after a double-slash pause, where you're about to start a new sentence or section — gives both the audio pipeline and any listener on a delayed connection room to catch the beginning of what you say next, rather than losing it to the gap between systems.

### Activity: Annotated Self-Practice

Start by marking up a short model passage — the worked example above, or one of your own choosing — independently, using all four symbols: slashes for pause length, underlines for lengthened vowels, bold for stress. Then compare your markup against a model version and think about where your instincts differed. Did you pause in different places? Stress different words? Neither version is necessarily wrong, but noticing the gap tells you something about your current default patterns.

Then apply the same notation to your own material: take the self-introduction you wrote back in Unit 2 and mark it up fully — phrase-boundary pauses, sentence-boundary pauses, lengthened vowels on your stressed syllables, bold on your key words. This is the point where the exercise stops being about a model text and starts being about your actual delivery.

Read your marked-up self-introduction aloud and record it, ideally through whatever microphone setup you'd actually use for an online presentation, applying the mic-technique adjustments above — consistent distance, slightly longer pauses at the double-slash marks to allow for compression and latency. Play the recording back against your markup and check: did the pauses you planned actually happen, or did they shrink once you were speaking? Did the words you'd bolded on paper come through as stressed, or did everything flatten out under the pressure of recording yourself? It's common for the gap between the planned markup and the actual recording to be larger than expected — that gap is useful information, not a failure, since it shows you exactly which of the four adjustments needs more deliberate rehearsal rather than assuming all four are equally solid. Revise the markup where needed and record a second pass, listening specifically for whether the revision closed the gap.

# Unit 10: Logic (Description + Reasoning)

## Long Tier

There's a reasonable case that structure matters more than content in a presentation — not because the content doesn't matter, but because even strong content gets lost if the audience can't tell how the pieces fit together. This unit is about three tools for building that structure before you worry about anything persuasive: Wh-questions for making sure you've covered the ground, connector language for showing how ideas relate, and a four-step reasoning chain for making sure each individual claim actually holds up under its own weight. Notice what's deliberately absent here — no appeals to emotion, no authority, no shared values. Those tools live in the next unit, and there's a reason they're kept separate: persuasion techniques applied to a structurally weak argument tend to make the weakness more visible, not less. Get the bones right first.

### Wh-Questions: A Completeness Check, Not a Content Formula

The six Wh-questions — Who, What, When, Where, Why, How — get taught early and often, sometimes to the point where they feel almost too simple to be useful. They're worth taking seriously anyway, because what they're actually good for isn't generating content so much as auditing it. Each question is a different angle a listener might approach your topic from, and a presentation that only answers two or three of them will feel complete to the person who wrote it — because that person already knows the other answers — while feeling full of gaps to everyone else in the room.

Take a presentation introducing a new internal process. Who does it affect? What's actually changing, in concrete terms rather than in principle? When does it take effect, and is there a transition period? Where does it apply — every team, or just some? Why is it changing at all, and how will someone's Monday morning actually look different? A talk that nails the "why" — the strategic rationale, the problem being solved — but skips "how" will land as thoughtful and directionless at the same time. The audience will agree with you and still not know what to do.

That said, treat the six questions as a checklist to run against a draft, not a template to fill in mechanically inside the talk itself. Forcing an explicit answer to all six questions into every section of a presentation makes for a stilted, repetitive structure — some answers are genuinely obvious from context, and belaboring them wastes time you could spend on the parts that need it. The useful move is to ask, section by section: if a listener asked me this question right now, could I answer it? If not, that's the gap to close, whether or not you say the answer out loud in the final version.

Worth pausing on: which of the six questions does your own draft tend to skip? Most presenters have a habitual blind spot — some over-explain "why" and under-explain "how," others do the reverse — and it's usually the same one across different talks, because it tracks how that person naturally thinks about a topic rather than being random.

### Connector Language: Making the Relationships Between Ideas Visible

Organizing content into the right categories solves only half the problem; the other half is showing your audience how those categories relate to each other, and that's the job connector language does. Four relationship types cover the bulk of what shows up in presentation content, though it's worth noting these aren't the only four possible relationships — just the ones that do most of the work in explanatory and reasoning-based talks.

**Comparison** connectors — *similarly, likewise, in the same way, both... and...* — signal that two things behave alike. **Contrast** connectors — *however, on the other hand, whereas, in contrast, unlike* — signal that they don't, and these tend to matter more than comparison connectors in technical or business content specifically, because an audience's default assumption is continuity: they'll assume the next point follows the same logic as the last one unless you tell them otherwise. A contrast connector is the signal that tells them to update that assumption, and leaving it out doesn't make the point disappear — it just means the audience discovers the contrast later, more confused than they needed to be.

**Sequence** connectors — *first, next, subsequently, finally, once... has happened* — signal order, and in process or timeline content the order isn't just a stylistic choice; getting it wrong changes what you're actually claiming happened. **Causal** connectors — *because, as a result, consequently, this means that, which leads to* — signal cause and effect, and in practice these are the ones most likely to go missing from a first draft. That's not carelessness so much as a natural byproduct of expertise: the presenter has been living with the material long enough that the cause-effect link feels self-evident, while an audience hearing it for the first time needs it stated.

A useful exercise, if you want to test a draft rather than just trust your instincts about it: go through a section and underline every connector you find. A section with none isn't automatically broken — sometimes a run of parallel, unconnected facts is the right choice — but it's worth asking deliberately whether that's the case, rather than discovering the gap only when someone in the audience looks lost.

### The Claim-Evidence-Analysis-Conclusion Chain

Here's a structure worth internalizing well enough that you reach for it without thinking: Statement, Evidence, Analysis, Conclusion. The idea is that any individual claim in a presentation earns its place not by being delivered confidently, but by being built out of these four parts in order.

- **Statement** — the claim itself, stated plainly and specifically enough that someone could actually disagree with it.
- **Evidence** — the concrete fact, data point, or example that backs it up.
- **Analysis** — the explanation connecting the evidence to the statement: what does this evidence actually demonstrate, and why does it demonstrate that rather than something else?
- **Conclusion** — what the statement, evidence, and analysis together establish, usually tied back into the larger argument of the talk.

The analysis step is the one that gets skipped most often, and it's worth understanding why, because it's not laziness — it's that the connection feels obvious to the person who did the thinking already. Take an example: Statement — "Response times for the support team have improved this quarter." Evidence — "Average first-response time dropped from six hours to ninety minutes." A presenter who stops there has technically supported the claim, but only technically. Analysis — "That's roughly a 75% reduction, and it starts right at the point the new ticket-routing system went live in April" — is what turns a number into a demonstrated point rather than an adjacent fact. Conclusion — "Since headcount stayed flat over the same period, the routing system is the more plausible explanation for the improvement than staffing changes" — is what makes the chain useful to the rest of the argument, because it rules out a competing explanation rather than just asserting the preferred one.

It's worth sitting with the difference between a chain that has analysis and one that doesn't, because the gap is often invisible to the person building it. Read the statement-evidence-conclusion version without the analysis line: "Response times improved. First-response time dropped from six hours to ninety minutes. The routing system caused the improvement." That's not unreasonable, exactly — but it's an assertion wearing the costume of a proof. The analysis line is what actually does the proving.

### Activity: Evidence-Chain Construction — and Setting Up Unit 11

Using the organizational or product presentation model you built for Appendix B, construct one full Statement → Evidence → Analysis → Conclusion chain for a claim that matters to that model. The template:

1. **Statement** — one sentence, specific enough to be arguable rather than just agreeable.
2. **Evidence** — the concrete support: a figure, a documented outcome, a specific case. If real evidence isn't available to you right now, note what would be needed and where you'd go to get it — that's a legitimate placeholder, not a cop-out.
3. **Analysis** — one to two sentences on how the evidence connects to the statement. Say what it demonstrates, not just what it is.
4. **Conclusion** — what the chain establishes, connected back to your presentation's larger point.

Hold onto this chain, because you're going to need it again. Unit 11 picks up exactly where this leaves off: it takes the same Statement-Evidence-Analysis-Conclusion chain you just built and asks a different question of it — not "is this sound?" but "how do I make this land with a room that isn't already inclined to agree?" The persuasive techniques in that unit — appeals to shared values, credibility, emotional stakes, framing — get applied on top of the structure you're building here, not instead of it. A persuasive technique wrapped around a chain with a missing analysis step will still feel hollow, no matter how well it's delivered; persuasion amplifies whatever structural soundness, or weakness, is already there.

So it's worth being genuinely rigorous with this chain rather than treating it as a warm-up exercise. Ask yourself: if a skeptical colleague read just this chain, with none of your delivery to help it along, would they find it convincing on the evidence alone? If the answer is no, that's worth fixing now — because in Unit 11, you'll be layering persuasive technique onto exactly what you hand yourself here, and technique built on a shaky foundation tends to expose the foundation rather than disguise it.

# Unit 11: Persuasion

You spent Unit 10 building something rigorous: a chain that runs from statement to evidence to analysis to conclusion, engineered so that anyone following it can see exactly how you got from a claim to a justified position. That chain is honest work, and if you built it well, it should be nearly impossible to find a gap in it. But here's the thing worth sitting with for a moment: a chain that's logically airtight doesn't automatically move anyone to act. You can hand someone a flawless argument and watch them nod, agree with every link, and still walk out of the room and do nothing differently. Logic earns agreement. It doesn't, by itself, earn action — and action is usually what a presentation is actually for.

This unit picks up exactly where Unit 10 left off, because persuasion isn't a replacement for logical structure — it's what you build on top of it once the structure is sound. Think of Unit 10 as the skeleton and this unit as what makes that skeleton move: the same bones, now given weight, direction, and a reason for the audience to care. If you tried to persuade without the logic underneath, you'd be manipulating rather than convincing — using pressure instead of substance. And if you stopped at the logic without ever building the persuasive layer on top, you'd be informing an audience rather than moving one. Neither half works alone. This unit is about the second half.

## The 3 Elements of Persuasion

Start with a question worth sitting with: when was the last time you were genuinely persuaded of something — not just informed, but moved to actually change your mind or your behavior? If you trace back what happened in that moment, you'll usually find three things were present together, not in sequence, but layered on top of each other the whole way through.

**Credibility and logic.** The audience has to believe the speaker knows what they're talking about, and the argument itself has to hold up — which is exactly the work you did in Unit 10. This element doesn't disappear in persuasion; it's the foundation the other two elements sit on. An emotionally compelling speaker with a broken argument might move a room for five minutes, but the persuasion doesn't survive contact with a single good question. Credibility is what makes the other two elements trustworthy rather than manipulative.

**Emotional connection.** People act on how a situation makes them feel a good deal more often than they act on what they've concluded intellectually is correct — which isn't a flaw in human reasoning so much as how reasoning actually works when a decision matters. A statement like "this process currently loses forty hours a month" is a fact. "Your team is spending a full work-week, every month, on something that shouldn't exist" is the same fact, aimed at how it feels to the people living it. Neither version is dishonest; the second one is just doing the work of connection that the first leaves undone.

**Sufficient information for acceptance.** This is easy to underestimate, because it sounds like it's just Unit 10 again. But there's a difference between having enough evidence to support a conclusion and having enough information for this specific audience to feel ready to say yes. A technical audience might need more detail on mechanism before they'll accept a proposal; a leadership audience might need less mechanism and more clarity on cost and risk. The evidence chain from Unit 10 gives you the raw material, but persuasion asks you to select and weight that material for what this room needs to hear before they'll move, not for what would satisfy an abstract standard of proof.

Worth noticing: all three elements have to be present together. Strong logic with no emotional connection reads as dry and forgettable. Emotional connection with weak logic reads as manipulative once anyone examines it closely. And either one without enough information for this particular audience leaves people interested but not ready to commit. The three elements aren't a checklist you tick off in order — they're three qualities your material needs to have simultaneously.

## The 5 Stages of Persuasion

If the three elements are the ingredients, the five stages are roughly the order you serve them in — though, as with most structures in this book, "roughly" is doing real work in that sentence, and we'll come back to why.

**1. Get attention.** Before anyone accepts an argument, they have to actually be listening to it, and attention at the start of a talk is not something you can assume you have just because people are seated and facing you. This might be a striking fact, a short story, a question that makes the room think for a second before you've said anything substantive — the mechanism matters less than the effect, which is to shift the audience from passive presence to active listening.

**2. Describe the situation and create a felt need.** This is where you lay out what's actually going on — and critically, where you help the audience feel why it matters to them, not just understand that it's true. This is close cousin to the emotional-connection element above: you're not just stating a problem, you're making its cost, or its opportunity, land somewhere the audience can feel. Skip this stage or rush it, and everything that follows lands on an audience that technically has the facts but hasn't yet decided the facts are their problem.

**3. Make a proposal.** Having established that something needs to change, you say clearly what you think should happen. This stage benefits enormously from the rigor of Unit 10 — a proposal backed by a sound evidence chain is a different animal from a proposal backed by enthusiasm alone. But notice that the proposal comes third, not first. Lead with the proposal before the room feels the need, and you're asking people to accept a solution to a problem they don't yet feel is theirs.

**4. Create an image of the results.** This is the stage persuasion adds that pure logical structure often skips: a concrete, specific picture of what things look like after the proposal is adopted. Not "efficiency will improve," but something the audience can actually picture — what a Monday looks like six months from now, what a customer notices, what a budget line no longer contains. Analysis in Unit 10 tells the audience the conclusion is sound; this stage tells them what accepting it will feel like to live inside.

**5. Make a specific call to action.** Persuasion that stops short of asking for something concrete leaves the audience agreeing with you in the abstract and doing nothing in particular. "Please consider this" is not a call to action; "I'd like your approval to pilot this with one team by the end of the quarter" is. The more specific and time-bound the ask, the more likely the persuasion actually converts into movement.

Now, the caveat promised above: these five stages describe a common and often effective shape, not a rigid sequence you owe every audience regardless of context. A room that already feels the need acutely — because they're the ones losing the forty hours a month — doesn't need three minutes convincing them the problem exists; spend that time there and you'll read as out of touch with what they already know, which costs you credibility rather than building it. Some experienced speakers compress stages two and three into almost the same breath when the audience is already primed. Others linger much longer in stage four than stage two, particularly when the proposal itself is uncontroversial but the payoff needs vivid imagining to feel real. Treat the five stages as a lens for checking whether you've done each piece of persuasive work somewhere in your talk — not as a form you fill in in strict numerical order.

A brief example, built just to illustrate the shape rather than to represent any particular evidence chain: imagine a five-minute internal pitch for switching a team's project-tracking software. Stage one might open with, "Raise your hand if you've missed a deadline because you didn't know a task existed" — attention, in the form of a question that makes people locate themselves in the problem. Stage two describes the current tool's gaps and names the felt cost: missed handoffs, the quiet frustration of finding out too late. Stage three proposes the specific replacement tool and why it fits. Stage four paints next quarter's stand-up meeting where nobody's surprised by a missed task. Stage five asks for a two-week pilot with one team, starting Monday. Notice that the logical bones — the evidence for why the current tool underperforms, the reasoning connecting the proposal to the fix — are exactly the kind of chain Unit 10 would have you build. This unit just asks where in the talk that chain needs to feel like something, not merely prove something.

## Learning Activity: The Persuasive Reframe

Here's where you put this to work directly on the chain you already built. Go back to the Statement → Evidence → Analysis → Conclusion chain you constructed in Unit 10 for your chosen Appendix B model speech — either the product or service launch, or the agency and program introduction. You're not starting over, and you're not inventing new content. The facts, the evidence, the reasoning: all of that stays exactly as sound as you left it. What changes is what you're building it toward.

Work through the following, using your own Unit 10 material as the raw content:

- **Identify your three elements.** Look at your Unit 10 chain and ask: where does credibility already live in this material (it should — that's largely what Unit 10 built), and where is the logic already solid? Now be honest about the other two. Where, right now, is there no emotional connection at all — just facts stated flatly? And is there information in your chain that a real audience would need before saying yes, which your Unit 10 version left implicit because it wasn't required for logical soundness? Note these gaps; you'll fill them in the stages below.

- **Reframe the chain into the five stages.** Take the same underlying content and rebuild it stage by stage:
  - Draft an attention-getting opening that didn't need to exist in your Unit 10 version, because Unit 10 didn't need to earn attention — it needed to earn agreement.
  - Take your Unit 10 "Statement" and rewrite it as a felt need: what was a claim now needs to land as something the audience feels is their problem or opportunity.
  - Take your Unit 10 "Evidence" and "Analysis" and compress them into a proposal — you already know it's sound; now say plainly what you think should happen and lean on that soundness rather than re-proving it from scratch.
  - Add a stage four that has no direct equivalent in your Unit 10 chain: a specific image of what results look like once your conclusion is acted on. If you're not sure what this looks like, ask yourself what a person affected by this would notice six months on.
  - Take your Unit 10 "Conclusion" and sharpen it into a specific, time-bound call to action — not "this approach is recommended," but who should do what, by when.

- **Compare the two versions side by side.** This is the part worth genuinely reflecting on rather than rushing past: read your Unit 10 chain and your persuasive reframe back to back. What did the persuasive version have to add that logic alone didn't require? What, if anything, did it cost you — did sharpening the emotional pull or the call to action put any pressure on the precision you had in Unit 10? Good persuasive work shouldn't have to sacrifice the rigor underneath it, and if you notice it did, that's worth going back and fixing rather than accepting as the price of persuasion.

There's no single correct reframe here, and two learners working from equally strong Unit 10 chains might reasonably produce quite different persuasive versions — because the right emotional register, the right level of stage-two urgency, the right call to action all depend on who you're picturing in the room. That dependence on audience and context isn't a weakness in the framework; it's the entire reason the five stages are a lens and not a form.

# Unit 12: Analyze (Problem-Solving / Reporting)

### Where this fits

Most people, once they've noticed something is wrong, want to skip straight to the fix — it's a natural instinct, and often a well-intentioned one. But a fix proposed before the problem has actually been explained tends to solve the wrong thing, or the right thing for a reason nobody in the room actually agrees with yet, which means you end up defending the solution instead of the diagnosis it was supposed to rest on. This unit deliberately doesn't let you get there. What you're building here is the discipline of stopping at diagnosis: explaining a problem clearly enough, and reporting your findings credibly enough, that someone else — a manager, a client, a committee, possibly a future version of you a week from now — could make a good decision about what to do next. The deciding part comes later, in a unit that picks up immediately where this one leaves off, using the same problem you analyze here. For now, treat that as someone else's job, even when it's tempting to do it yourself.

It's worth pausing on why that separation is hard to hold onto in practice. By the time you've investigated a problem thoroughly enough to report on it well, you usually already have opinions about what should happen next — and those opinions have a way of leaking into your language even when you don't intend them to. A sentence like "response times have gotten worse, especially since the new ticketing system went live" sounds like a neutral observation, but say it in a certain tone, in a certain meeting, and everyone in the room hears "we should ditch the new ticketing system," whether or not you meant to imply it. Part of what this unit teaches is noticing that leak and closing it, not because recommending is wrong, but because it's a different job, and doing both at once tends to do neither well.

### The Background–Situation–Results model

Background–Situation–Results, usually shortened to B-S-R, is one way — not the only conceivable way, but a genuinely useful one — of structuring an explanation so that someone unfamiliar with the problem can follow your reasoning rather than just nodding along to your conclusion.

**Background** sets the frame: what's the normal, expected, or baseline state of affairs, stated briefly enough that it doesn't turn into a history lesson. Its job is narrow — just enough context that the next section makes sense to someone who knows the general subject but not this specific issue.

**Situation** is the departure from that baseline, and it's the section where vague language does the most damage. "Things have been slower lately" is an impression; "average turnaround went from two days to five over the last six weeks" is a situation someone can actually evaluate. Naming the problem in concrete, checkable terms is most of the work of making a report credible, and it's also where a lot of first drafts quietly fall short, because the person writing them already knows what they mean and forgets that the audience doesn't.

**Results** is where you show your work: what you actually investigated, what patterns emerged, and what those patterns mean. This is the section that separates a report from an opinion — anyone can assert that a process is broken, but Results is where you demonstrate it, with evidence a skeptical listener could poke at and not have it fall apart.

### Where B-S-R deliberately stops

B-S-R, as used in this unit, ends at Results. It does not continue on to "and therefore we should..." — and that's not an accident or an oversight in how the model is taught here, it's the entire point of treating analysis as a distinct skill from recommendation, which gets its own unit immediately after this one for learners continuing on. Resist the pull to add a fourth beat even when the recommendation feels obvious to you; particularly when it feels obvious, actually, because that's exactly the moment your judgment is most likely to be quietly steering the "neutral" report toward a conclusion you'd already reached before you started investigating.

A useful check before you present: read back through your Results section and ask whether it contains a single recommendation, suggestion, or "so we should" dressed up as an observation. If it does, pull that sentence out — it belongs in a different conversation, one this unit isn't having yet.

### A short worked example

Here's a compact illustration of the shape, deliberately simple, just to show the mechanics rather than to model a full report.

Imagine you've noticed that fewer people are asking questions at the end of your team's weekly demo session than used to.

*Background:* "For roughly the first year of this demo's existence, each session closed with somewhere between five and eight audience questions, and the Q&A portion regularly ran long."

*Situation:* "Over the last two months, the average has dropped to one or two questions per session, and three of the last eight sessions had none at all."

*Results:* "I reviewed attendance logs and found that overall attendance hasn't changed — the same roughly twenty people are still logging in. What has changed is the format: two months ago we switched from an in-person session with an open floor to a screen-share call where questions go through a text chat rather than being asked aloud. Of the five people I spoke with informally, three said they have a question most weeks but don't feel it's worth typing out, and would ask if they could just unmute."

Notice what's absent: there's no line that says "so we should switch back to voice questions" or "we should require cameras on." That's genuinely tempting to add, and it might even be the right call — but this report doesn't make that argument. It hands over a clear, evidence-backed account of what changed and why, and stops there. Whoever decides what to do with the screen-share format gets to make that call with good information, rather than being handed the decision half-made and dressed up as a fact.

### Building the report, section by section

Background doesn't need to be long — two or three sentences is often enough, and padding it out to sound thorough usually just delays the point where the audience starts learning something new. Situation is where precision matters most: quantify wherever you can, and describe the change in terms of what's different now, not just how it feels. Results is where the real judgment call lives, and it's worth naming that plainly rather than pretending there's a formula for it: what counts as sufficient evidence, and what's just noise? Three data points and two anecdotes might be entirely convincing for an internal team update and thin for a report going to a client or a board — there's no fixed threshold that applies everywhere, and deciding you have "enough" is a judgment call you make based on the audience and the stakes, not a box you check.

If your investigation turned up something messy — findings that point in more than one direction, or a pattern you can't fully explain — say so rather than smoothing it over. A report that acknowledges "the data suggests X, though it doesn't fully explain Y" reads as more credible, not less, because it signals that you're reporting what you actually found rather than what would make the tidiest story.

Worth reflecting on for a moment: think about the last time someone reported a problem to you and you found yourself distrusting the report — not disagreeing with a recommendation, since there wasn't one, but doubting the diagnosis itself. What was missing? Often it's exactly this: too little acknowledgment of complication, too much confidence in a single tidy explanation. That's usually a more useful thing to notice than any rule about section length.

### Activity: Problem Analysis Report

Source your own problem — something real, current enough that you don't already have a tidy answer worked out in advance. That last part matters more than it might seem: a problem you've already mentally solved will pull your language toward the solution no matter how carefully you try to stay neutral, so pick something you're still genuinely curious about, not something you're already prepared to argue for a fix to.

Investigate it enough to have real Background, Situation, and Results material — this might mean pulling actual numbers, or it might mean a handful of honest conversations with people close to the problem, depending on what's available to you. Then draft a spoken report of five to seven minutes using Background–Situation–Results, and deliver it to a partner or small group.

After delivering it, ask two things, and listen carefully to the answers rather than moving straight past them: whether the problem was clear before you got to evidence — that is, whether Situation did its job — and whether your listener caught themselves thinking about solutions while you were still supposed to be reporting findings. That second question is diagnostic in a useful way: if your listener started problem-solving in their head partway through, some line of yours probably nudged them there, and it's worth going back to find it.

Keep what you produce. Hold onto the problem itself, your Background–Situation–Results analysis, and whatever reactions your listener gave you — you'll be picking this exact problem back up in the next unit, where the question changes from "is this understood?" to "what should be done about it, and can you defend that?" Nothing here goes to waste; the report you build now is the foundation the next unit builds its recommendation directly on top of.

# Unit 13: Propose (Recommended Actions, Including Argue & Defend)

## Long

Unit 12 left you somewhere deliberately unfinished. You walked a real problem through Background, Situation, and Results — you established what the norm was, what's actually happening now, and what the evidence shows once you dig into it — and then you stopped. That stopping point wasn't an oversight; it was the point. Diagnosis and prescription are genuinely different jobs, and a lot of presentations go wrong precisely because a speaker blurs them together, announcing a fix in the same breath as the problem before the audience has had a chance to absorb that there's a problem worth fixing at all. This unit is where you pick the thread back up and finish the job: turning your analysis into a recommendation, and then holding that recommendation up against someone who disagrees with it.

It's worth being honest about why this is its own unit rather than a paragraph tacked onto the end of Unit 12. Recommending well is a distinct skill from analyzing well — you can produce a flawless diagnosis and still propose a weak or unconvincing fix, because the two rely on different instincts. Analysis rewards thoroughness and neutrality; recommendation rewards judgment and nerve. You have to actually commit to something, which means opening yourself up to disagreement, which means you also need to know how to defend a position once someone pushes back on it. That third piece — defending your recommendation against a counterargument — used to be taught as its own separate genre in older presentation courses, under a heading like "argue and defend." It's folded in here deliberately, because in practice it was never really separate. The moment you recommend something worth doing, someone in the room is going to have a reason it shouldn't happen, and handling that well isn't a bonus skill bolted onto the recommendation — it's a normal, expected part of making one.

### From diagnosis to decision

Think about the last time someone presented you with a well-documented problem and then just... stopped, leaving you to figure out what they wanted you to do about it. It's a mildly frustrating experience, isn't it — you're left holding evidence with no instructions for what it's supposed to change. That gap between "here's what's happening" and "here's what we should do" is exactly where a recommendation lives, and audiences generally feel its absence even when they can't name it directly.

A recommendation earns its credibility from the diagnosis that precedes it — which is exactly why Unit 12's Results section matters so much here. If your Results showed that a specific factor is driving the problem, your recommendation should visibly target that factor; if it doesn't, an attentive listener will notice the disconnect faster than you'd expect. This doesn't mean every recommendation has to be the single most logically airtight option available. It means the connective tissue between what you found and what you're proposing needs to be visible, not assumed. You're not just telling the audience what to do; you're showing them that this particular action follows from this particular evidence, and that's a different, more demanding standard than simply having a reasonable-sounding idea.

There's a useful discipline in stating your recommendation early and plainly, rather than building slowly toward a reveal. Some speakers instinctively want to save the "answer" for the end, treating it almost like a punchline — but recommendations aren't punchlines, and audiences asked to sit through several minutes of setup before finding out what you actually want them to do tend to spend that time guessing, which pulls attention away from the reasoning you're trying to build. A more reliable shape is to state the recommendation, then spend your time explaining why it's the right one given what you found — front-loading the point rather than working up to it. That's not the only defensible structure, and a genuinely surprising or counter-intuitive recommendation might justify a bit more groundwork before you name it. But it's a reasonable default, and worth treating as one.

### What a recommendation needs to do

A recommendation that holds up under scrutiny is usually doing three things at once, even if it doesn't announce them as separate steps.

First, it names a specific action — not a direction or an aspiration, but something concrete enough that someone could plausibly go do it tomorrow. "We should take customer churn more seriously" isn't a recommendation; it's a restatement of the problem wearing a recommendation's clothes. "We should introduce a 48-hour follow-up call for any customer who cancels a subscription" is a recommendation, because it names an action, and you can picture someone actually doing it.

Second, it connects back to the evidence explicitly, rather than leaving the audience to make that connection unassisted. This is the part most easily skipped when you're confident in your own reasoning — it feels obvious to you, so you assume it's obvious to everyone. It rarely is. A sentence as simple as "because the Results showed that most cancellations happened in the two weeks after a support ticket went unresolved, this follow-up call targets exactly that window" does a lot of quiet work, because it reminds the audience that you're not just pattern-matching to a generic best practice — you're responding to what your own analysis actually found.

Third, it acknowledges cost or tradeoff honestly, rather than presenting the recommendation as a free win. Every real recommendation costs something — time, money, political capital, someone's comfort with the way things currently work — and audiences are generally more persuadable, not less, when a speaker names that cost upfront rather than leaving it to be discovered later by someone in the audience who's already thinking about it. Naming the cost yourself also does something useful for your credibility: it signals that you've actually thought this through rather than fallen in love with the first idea that occurred to you.

Worth pausing on: think about a recommendation you've received recently — from a colleague, a doctor, a mechanic, anyone — that you found convincing. Which of these three elements was doing the persuading? Often it's the second one, the visible link back to evidence, that separates a recommendation you trust from one you quietly resist even when you can't articulate why.

### Defending against a counterargument

Here's the part that tends to make people nervous, and it's worth naming that nervousness directly rather than pretending it isn't there: proposing something means someone might disagree with you, publicly, in the room, while you're still standing at the front of it. That's uncomfortable, but it's also just what happens when a recommendation is substantive enough to matter — a recommendation nobody could possibly object to is usually one that wasn't worth making in the first place.

The instinct to avoid is treating a counterargument as an attack to survive. A more useful frame is that a counterargument is a legitimate alternative reading of the same evidence, and your job is to show why your reading holds up better — not to prove the other person foolish for raising it. This distinction matters more than it might seem to on paper, because audiences can generally tell the difference between a speaker who's engaging with an objection and one who's merely tolerating it before returning to their script. The first builds trust; the second erodes it, often quite visibly.

A defensible structure for handling a counterargument runs something like this, though — as with most structures in this course — treat it as a starting point rather than a script to recite:

- **State the counterargument fairly, in its strongest form.** This feels counterintuitive the first time you try it — why would you make the other side's case well? — but a weak, straw-man version of a counterargument is transparent to anyone who actually holds that view, and it costs you credibility with everyone else in the room too. Stating it well signals that you've genuinely considered it, which is itself persuasive before you've said a single word in response.
- **Acknowledge what's true in it.** Most reasonable counterarguments aren't wrong so much as incomplete, or right about a cost while wrong about whether that cost outweighs the benefit. Saying so explicitly — "it's true that this adds a step to an already busy support team's workload" — costs you very little and buys you a great deal of credibility, because it shows you're not defending your position out of stubbornness.
- **Explain why your recommendation still holds despite that.** This is where you return to your evidence, ideally adding something the counterargument didn't account for — new information, a different weighting of the tradeoffs, or a cost of inaction that the counterargument hasn't priced in. "That's true, but the cost of not making this call is a customer we've already half-lost, versus fifteen minutes of a support agent's time" is a stronger response than simply repeating your original point more loudly.

That third step is where a lot of otherwise well-prepared speakers falter, mostly because that's the improvisational part — you can't always predict exactly which counterargument you'll get, and your prepared material won't cover every version of it. The best preparation for this isn't scripting a perfect rebuttal in advance; it's returning, honestly, to your own evidence and reasoning until you genuinely understand why you believe what you believe. If you know that cold, you can meet most counterarguments by explaining your actual reasoning again, from a slightly different angle, rather than needing a pre-written line for every possible objection. Speakers who've only memorized a response tend to freeze when the counterargument arrives in a form they didn't rehearse; speakers who understand their own reasoning tend to adapt.

### A brief illustration

To make this concrete, here's a short, self-contained example — not connected to any specific problem from your own Unit 12 work, just a way of seeing the shape in action.

*Recommendation:* "We should move our weekly team status meeting from a live 45-minute call to a written async update, submitted by end of day Thursday."

*Link to evidence:* "This targets what we found directly — the meeting's own attendance log showed engagement dropping steadily after the first ten minutes, and half the content covered each week duplicated what was already in the project tracker."

*Cost named upfront:* "The real cost here is that we'd lose the live back-and-forth for anything genuinely urgent — so this only works if we keep a short, optional live slot for anything that can't wait until Thursday's written update."

*Counterargument, stated fairly:* "The obvious objection is that written updates lose tone and nuance — people read intent into a terse written sentence that they wouldn't read into the same words said aloud, and that's a real risk for a team that's still building trust with each other."

*What's true in it:* "That's a fair concern, and it's exactly the kind of thing that goes wrong quietly, without anyone flagging it until damage is already done."

*Why the recommendation still holds:* "But the attendance data suggests the live format isn't actually delivering that nuance for most of the meeting's forty-five minutes anyway — people have visibly checked out. We're not trading a rich live exchange for a flatter written one; we're trading a live exchange that's already lost most of the room for a written one that at least gets read in full, with the option to escalate anything sensitive to a quick direct conversation instead of the group call."

Notice what that last move is doing: it's not dismissing the counterargument, and it's not simply restating the original recommendation more forcefully. It's going back to the evidence and showing that the counterargument's concern, while legitimate in general, doesn't actually apply as strongly to this specific situation as it might first appear — which is a more durable way to hold a position than simply repeating it with more confidence.

### Activity: Recommend and defend

This activity picks up directly from the problem you analyzed in Unit 12 — not a new topic, the same one. That continuity is deliberate: the real test of an analysis isn't whether it was thorough on its own terms, but whether it actually leads somewhere useful, and this is where you find out.

1. **Return to your Unit 12 analysis.** Reread your Background, Situation, and Results before you do anything else. Resist the temptation to jump straight to a recommendation from memory — you want your proposal to visibly grow out of what your Results section actually found, not out of whatever fix has been sitting in the back of your mind the whole time.

2. **Draft a recommendation, stated as a specific action.** One or two sentences, no more. Check it against the "restatement of the problem in disguise" trap described above — if your recommended action could apply to almost any version of the problem, it's probably too vague, and it's worth sharpening it until it names something a specific person could actually go and do.

3. **Write the link back to your evidence.** A short paragraph explaining, explicitly, which part of your Results section this recommendation responds to and why. If you find this step hard to write, that's useful information — it may mean the recommendation doesn't yet follow as tightly from your analysis as you'd assumed, and it's worth revising one or the other before moving on.

4. **Name the cost or tradeoff honestly.** What does this recommendation actually require — of time, money, comfort, or someone's existing routine? Write it in a sentence or two, resisting the pull to soften it into something that barely sounds like a cost at all.

5. **Write the strongest counterargument you can against your own recommendation.** This is the step people are most tempted to shortcut, usually by writing a weak counterargument that's easy to knock down. Don't. Argue against yourself as seriously as a genuinely skeptical colleague would, someone who has read your analysis and still isn't convinced.

6. **Write your defense**, using the three-part shape above: acknowledge what's true in the counterargument, then explain — with reference to your evidence — why your recommendation holds up despite it.

7. **Deliver the whole sequence aloud** — recommendation, evidence link, cost, counterargument, and defense — to a partner, small group, or recorded to yourself if you're working alone. Ask your listener, specifically, whether the defense felt like a genuine response to the objection or like a restatement of your original point in slightly different words. That distinction is often easier for a listener to hear than for a speaker to judge about their own delivery.

Once you've run through it, it's worth reflecting honestly on which step gave you the most trouble. For a lot of people it's step 5 — arguing seriously against their own idea feels almost unnatural, like working against your own interest. But it's usually the step that improves the final result the most, because a recommendation that's already survived your own best objection tends to survive the room's objections too.

# Unit 14: Delivering with Slides & Handouts

## Long Tier

### The Physical and Digital Geography of Presenting

There's a piece of stagecraft that gets surprisingly little attention in most presentation advice: where you physically stand, or where your face appears on screen, relative to your visual aids. It matters more than it seems like it should, because an audience's attention will always drift toward whichever element is easiest to look at, and a presenter standing in front of their own screen — or one who's disappeared entirely behind a full-screen slide share — has made themselves the harder option to watch. The general guidance is to stand beside the screen and angle your body toward the room, or, online, to keep your webcam tile visible rather than letting slides take over the whole frame. But it's worth thinking about why, not just following it as a rule: the audience is there for a person making an argument, and slides are supporting evidence, not the main act. Every choice about positioning is really a choice about which one you're asking the audience to treat as primary.

Online delivery changes the geography more than a lot of presenters expect going in. In a room, you have a body, a gesture, a physical relationship to the space that people read constantly whether or not you're actively pointing at anything. On a screen-share, most of that disappears unless you deliberately preserve it — through a visible camera, through verbal signposting that substitutes for a gesture, through checking that your presence hasn't been reduced to a disembodied voice narrating slides. Think of screen-share delivery not as in-room presenting with a technical inconvenience layered on top, but as its own mode with its own geography to manage, since for a lot of audiences today it's the default mode, not the exception.

### Sequencing Speech and Visuals: A Timing Problem, Not Just a Habit

One of the most common synchronization errors is talking and changing the slide at the same moment, and it's worth understanding why this is a problem rather than just a rule to follow: reading and listening both draw on working memory, and asking an audience to do both at once, about two different pieces of information, means something gets dropped. The practical fix is a small pause — change the slide, let it land, then speak — and the same logic extends to pointing: point, give it a half-beat, then explain what you're pointing at. It sounds like a trivial adjustment, and in isolation it is, but strung across an entire talk it's the difference between a presentation that feels effortless to follow and one that feels like it's constantly asking the audience to catch up.

It's also worth acknowledging that the "right" pause length isn't fixed — it depends on how dense the slide is, how familiar the content is to this particular audience, and how quickly people in the room tend to read. A single data-heavy chart might need three or four seconds of silence before you start narrating it; a slide with one short phrase might need barely any pause at all before the extra silence starts to feel awkward rather than useful. Rather than memorizing a fixed pause length, it's worth treating this as a judgment you calibrate slide by slide, based on how much visual processing you're actually asking the audience to do.

### Slide Changes as Rehearsed Cues, Not In-the-Moment Decisions

Advancing slides seems like the most mechanical part of presenting, which is exactly why it causes so many small derailments — presenters treat it as an afterthought rather than something to actually rehearse, and then find themselves either forgetting to click forward or clicking too early and giving away a point before they've built up to it. One approach worth trying is to tie each slide advance to a specific verbal cue in your script, a phrase that always means "now," so the click becomes automatic rather than a decision competing for your attention with everything else you're managing mid-talk. This isn't the only way to solve the problem — some presenters prefer to build a visual habit instead, always advancing on a particular type of transition sentence rather than a memorized phrase — but the underlying goal is the same either way: get the mechanical action out of your conscious attention so it can't compete with actually delivering your content.

Screen-share delivery adds a wrinkle worth flagging directly: there's often a small lag between when you click and when the audience actually sees the change, depending on connection quality and platform. What feels perfectly timed to you may look a half-second late to everyone watching, which is disorienting in a way that's hard to diagnose from the presenter's side because you never see the delay yourself. It's worth building in a slightly more generous pause after each click when presenting online than you would in person, and — if you can manage it — checking with someone in the first few minutes of an online session whether slides are landing when you expect them to, before you're deep into content and can't easily adjust.

### Handouts Have Changed Form, But Not Function

It's worth being explicit about something that's shifted in recent years: "handout" used to mean a printed page physically distributed around a room, but for most presenters today it means a shared document, a slide file, or a link dropped into a chat window before or during the session. That's a real change in the mechanics, but it hasn't changed the underlying skill much at all, which is pacing — making sure the audience is looking at the same part of the material as you're talking about it, rather than scattered across different sections at different points in your walkthrough.

The failure mode here is familiar even if the medium has changed: someone in your audience is three sections ahead because they started reading the moment you shared the link, while someone else is still finding the file. The fix is the same regardless of format — announce clearly where you are ("let's look at the second paragraph under the heading 'Timeline'"), and pause briefly after each transition so people can actually locate that spot before you keep talking. For a small audience, this doesn't need to be rigid or scripted; a quick check-in ("everyone found that section?") is often enough, and it lets you adjust your pace in real time based on what you're seeing rather than guessing.

One thing worth considering: if you've shared the material well in advance, some of your audience may already know it reasonably well by the time you present, which changes what your walkthrough is actually for. Rather than treating every handout session as a first exposure to guide carefully line by line, it's worth asking what this particular audience actually needs from you live — often it's less about walking through content they've already seen and more about surfacing the two or three points that matter most, or fielding the questions the material raised for them. That's a judgment call that depends entirely on how much lead time you gave people and how likely they were to actually use it, not a fixed rule you can apply the same way every time.

### Activity: Rehearsal with Feedback

Go back to the redesigned slides you produced in Unit 6 — you'll be using that same material for both parts of this activity, so there's no new content to prepare here, just a new way of delivering what you already built.

**Part one: the projected or screen-shared version.** Deliver the slides aloud to a partner, either in person or over a screen-share, applying the sequencing ideas from this unit: change the slide, let it land, then speak; point, pause, then explain. Run through the whole thing once, start to finish, resisting the urge to stop and restart when something feels off — the goal of a first pass is to see how the timing actually holds up under real delivery conditions, not to produce a polished performance.

**Part two: the handout-pacing variant.** Now deliver the same content again, but this time as if it were a shared digital handout rather than projected slides — walk your partner through the material section by section, announcing where you are and pausing to let them find the spot, the way you would with a small audience working from a document you'd sent ahead. Notice what changes when you're not relying on a projected visual to carry the pacing for you: you have to do more of that signposting work verbally, since there's no slide advance to mark the transition for you automatically.

After both passes, ask your partner for feedback focused specifically on synchronization — not on your content or your confidence, but on whether your speech and your visual (or verbal) transitions were actually lining up. Where did they feel like they were reading ahead of you, or waiting for you to catch up? Which of the two formats felt better synchronized, and is that because of something about the format itself, or something about how you delivered each one? It's worth treating that last question as a real one rather than a rhetorical one — the answer might tell you more about your own habits than about slides versus handouts as formats.

# Unit 15: Team Presentations

Everything up to this point has assumed you're standing up there alone — one speaker, one voice, one thread of logic running from opening to close. Most of the presenting you'll actually do in a working life doesn't look like that. It looks like a project team walking a client through a proposal, three engineers explaining a system to a review board, or a group of students presenting a semester's research as if it were a single coherent argument rather than three separate ones stapled together. Team presenting is its own skill, and it's one that trips up plenty of speakers who are individually strong — because the thing that makes a team presentation work or fail isn't any one person's slide or delivery. It's the seams. The moments where one speaker stops and another starts are exactly where an audience either loses confidence in the group or gains it, and this unit is about making those seams disappear.

## 1. What makes a team presentation different

Think about a team presentation you've sat through recently — maybe a student group project, maybe a vendor pitch at work. What made it feel like one presentation rather than three people taking turns at a podium? Chances are it wasn't any single speaker's individual polish. It was more likely a sense that everyone knew what everyone else was doing, that the handoffs felt planned rather than improvised, and that by the end you had a clear sense of who owned what — without anyone having to stop and explain the org chart to you.

That last point matters more than it might seem. A team presentation is doing two jobs your solo presentations never had to do: it has to deliver the content, and it has to demonstrate that the team functions as a team. An audience evaluating a group — whether that's a client deciding whether to trust your firm, or an instructor grading collaborative work — is watching for coordination as a signal in its own right. Sloppy handoffs, overlapping content, or a speaker who clearly doesn't know what their colleague just said will read as a team that doesn't communicate well internally, and that's a credibility problem no individual speaker can fix through good delivery alone.

## 2. Structuring the team: roles and equal participation

A workable structure for most team presentations is two to three speakers, each with a genuine, substantial section — not a token thirty-second cameo designed to make sure everyone's name gets said once. There's a reason three tends to be a practical ceiling: beyond that, the handoffs start to dominate the presentation's rhythm, and audiences find it harder to track who's who. If your team is larger than three, it's worth asking whether everyone needs to present, or whether some members are better used in a strong supporting role — building slides, handling the Q&A on a specific topic, fielding questions from the audience — without taking a speaking segment.

Equal participation is worth treating as a real design principle rather than a nicety. It's tempting, especially under time pressure, to let your strongest speaker take the lion's share of the talk time while quieter teammates get the shortest or least interesting sections. Resist that instinct, or at least interrogate it before you act on it. Audiences notice imbalance, and in academic or professional evaluation settings, uneven participation is often read (fairly or not) as a sign that the work itself was unevenly shared. The more useful question isn't "who's the best speaker" but "how do we divide this content so each person owns a piece substantial enough to demonstrate real command of it" — which is really a Unit 2 structuring question, applied at the team level instead of the individual one.

That division of labor is also where Units 2 through 4 come back into play, just distributed across more than one person. Each team member is still responsible for structuring their own section clearly, choosing flag expressions that signal where their piece is going, and delivering it with the same attention to pacing, voice, and presence you'd bring to a solo talk. What's new here isn't the individual skill — it's making three individually well-built sections cohere into one presentation, which takes deliberate connective work that a solo speaker never has to do.

## 3. The first speaker's job: setting up the whole team

The opening speaker in a team presentation carries a slightly heavier load than an opening speaker working alone, because on top of the usual work of establishing the topic and earning the room's attention, they're also introducing the team itself. That means telling the audience, briefly and clearly, who's presenting and what each person is going to cover — not a formal résumé for each teammate, just enough of a map that the audience knows what to expect and can follow the logic of who's speaking when.

A line like "I'm going to start with the background on this problem, then hand over to Priya, who'll walk you through our proposed approach, before Marcus closes with the implementation timeline and cost" does a surprising amount of work in one sentence. It tells the audience the shape of the whole talk before any of the detail arrives, which means when the actual handoff to Priya happens ten minutes later, nobody's wondering who she is or why she's suddenly talking. You're essentially giving the audience a structural preview of the team the same way a solo speaker previews their agenda — the difference is that this preview is about people as well as content.

It's worth resisting the urge to make this introduction longer or more elaborate than it needs to be. One or two sentences per teammate is usually plenty; the audience doesn't need biographical detail at this stage; they need a map. Save the depth for when each speaker actually takes their section — that's where their expertise should show up, not in the introduction.

## 4. Handoff language: the seams that hold it together

If there's one skill this unit is really built around, it's the language of the handoff — how a speaker closes their section and passes control to the next person without the transition feeling like a hard stop and restart. Weak handoffs tend to sound like "okay, that's it for me" followed by an awkward pause and a new speaker starting cold. Strong handoffs do two things: they briefly close the current section with a sense of completion, and they actively introduce what's coming next, ideally connecting the two.

Consider the difference between "That's my section" and "So that covers the problem as we see it — I'll now pass over to Priya, who's going to walk you through how we'd actually solve it." The second version does real work: it names what's ending, names who's coming, and gives a one-line preview of what they'll cover, which means the next speaker doesn't have to spend their first ten seconds re-establishing context the audience already has. It also signals, subtly, that the team has actually talked to each other about the shape of this presentation — which loops back to the credibility point from Section 1.

The speaker receiving the handoff has a job too, and it's one that's easy to skip in the nervousness of stepping up. A brief acknowledgment — "Thanks, Sam" or "Building on what Sam just outlined" — costs almost nothing and buys a good deal. It confirms the handoff landed, it gives you a beat to settle before your content starts, and it reinforces that you were actually listening to your teammate rather than just waiting for your turn. Speakers sometimes worry this kind of acknowledgment sounds scripted or artificial, and it can, if it's delivered woodenly. But a short, genuine acknowledgment tends to read as confidence and cohesion rather than performance — it's really no different from a solo speaker briefly recapping their previous point before moving to the next one, just redirected toward a colleague's work instead of your own.

None of this needs to be memorized word for word, and it's worth being a little wary of treating handoff phrases as a script to recite. What matters is the function — close, connect, receive, continue — not the exact wording. Teams that over-rehearse the phrasing sometimes end up sounding stilted, whereas teams that understand the function can improvise a natural-sounding handoff even if something in the room doesn't go quite as planned.

## 5. Blending "we" and "I": whose voice is this, anyway?

One of the subtler challenges of team presenting is pronoun choice, and it matters more than it might initially seem. Lean too hard on "I" throughout and the presentation starts to feel like three solo talks that happen to share a stage — the opposite of the coherence you're trying to build. Lean too hard on "we" and individual speakers can end up sounding like they're hiding behind the group, unwilling to own their specific expertise or claim credit for their piece of the work.

The more useful approach is to blend the two deliberately, based on what's actually true at any given moment. When you're describing something the team decided or built together — the overall approach, the shared conclusion, the recommendation you're all standing behind — "we" is accurate and appropriate: "we looked at three possible approaches before settling on this one." But when you're in your own section, walking through analysis you personally did or a part of the system you personally built, "I" is often more honest and, frankly, more informative: "I ran the cost modeling on this, and what surprised me was how much the maintenance overhead outweighed the setup cost." That's your expertise on display, and diluting it into "we" doesn't make the team look more unified — it just makes it harder for the audience to know who actually knows what.

A rough guide, though it's genuinely just a guide and not a formula: use "we" for shared framing, shared decisions, and the conclusion; use "I" for your individual analysis, your specific findings, and moments where you're drawing on expertise that's yours in particular. Listen back to a recording of your own rehearsal, if you can, and notice which pronoun you default to — most speakers have an unconscious lean one way or the other, and it's worth correcting for if yours turns out to be lopsided.

## 6. The closing speaker's job: bringing the team back together

Just as the opening speaker has extra work introducing the team, the closing speaker has extra work bringing it back together. A solo closing has to summarize the argument and land on a clear takeaway. A team closing has to do that and also make the audience feel like they just watched one coherent presentation delivered by a coordinated group — not three good talks that happened to be adjacent.

Part of that is structural: a genuinely strong close will briefly reference what the other speakers covered, tying their pieces back into the single thread the first speaker promised at the start. Something like "so between the three of us, you've now seen the scale of the problem, the approach we'd take to solve it, and what it would cost to get there" does more than summarize content — it explicitly reminds the audience that this was a team effort with each person contributing a distinct, necessary piece, which reinforces exactly the coordination signal audiences are watching for. It's a small move, but skipping it is one of the more common ways a team presentation ends up feeling fragmented right at the moment it should feel most unified.

The other part is pronoun choice again, and here "we" typically belongs front and center: the recommendation, the ask, the next step — these are things the team is putting forward together, and the language should reflect that shared ownership even if one person happens to be the one speaking the words. A close that says "I think you should move forward with this" when three people did the work sends an odd signal; "we'd recommend moving forward, and we're glad to answer any questions as a group" sits better, both because it's more accurate and because it invites the audience into a group Q&A rather than directing them at a single spokesperson.

## 7. Handling questions as a team

A brief but practical note, since it tends to catch teams off guard even after a well-rehearsed presentation: decide in advance, even loosely, who's the natural first responder for which kinds of questions. You don't need a rigid assignment — audiences ask things you can't fully predict — but if a question clearly falls into someone's section, a small pause and a glance can invite them to take it rather than having two people accidentally start answering at once, or everyone looking at each other waiting for someone else to go first. If nobody's an obvious fit, it's entirely fine for the closing speaker, or whoever feels most confident, to take the first pass and invite a teammate to add anything. What you're avoiding here is the same fragmentation problem from earlier sections, just showing up live and unscripted instead of in the planned parts of the talk.

## 8. Learning activity: the group coordination project

This unit's learning activity asks you to run a full team presentation from planning through delivery, in a group of two or three, applying everything above rather than just discussing it in the abstract.

**Step one: divide the topic.** As a team, choose a topic substantial enough to support two or three genuinely distinct sections — not so narrow that you're each restating the same point, and not so broad that the sections feel unrelated. Spend real time on this division; it's the single decision most likely to determine whether your final presentation feels coherent or feels stapled together. A good test is whether you could describe each person's section in one sentence and have those three sentences clearly build on each other.

**Step two: draft individually, using Units 2 through 4.** Each team member drafts their own section independently, applying the structuring principles from Unit 2, choosing deliberate flag expressions from Unit 3 to signal their section's shape, and planning their delivery approach using the skills from Unit 4. This is still, at this stage, an individual presentation-building exercise — you're each responsible for your section holding up on its own, with a clear structure and a confident delivery plan, before anyone worries about how the pieces fit together.

**Step three: build the seams.** Come back together and work out the connective tissue this unit has focused on. Draft the first speaker's team introduction — who's covering what, in what order. Draft each handoff: how the outgoing speaker closes their section and hands to the next person, and how the incoming speaker acknowledges it before continuing. Draft the closing speaker's wrap-up, making sure it explicitly references what each teammate covered and lands on a shared recommendation or takeaway. Read back through everyone's sections together and check your pronoun use — where should "I" become "we," or vice versa, now that you can see the whole presentation as a single piece rather than three separate drafts?

**Step four: rehearse the transitions specifically.** It's worth running the whole presentation at least once focusing purely on the handoff moments rather than the content — the goal here isn't to re-polish each individual section again, it's to make sure the seams feel natural under actual speaking conditions, not just on paper. Notice where a handoff feels rushed or where an acknowledgment feels tacked on, and adjust.

**Step five: deliver as a team.** Present the full piece — team introduction, individual sections with handoffs, team-oriented close — and, if your setting includes it, handle questions using the light coordination approach from Section 7. Afterward, it's worth a few minutes of honest team reflection: did the presentation feel like one coherent piece to an outside listener? Where did the seams show, and what would you adjust next time?

The point of running the full cycle — division, individual drafting, seam-building, transition rehearsal, delivery — is that team presenting isn't really a separate skill bolted onto everything you've already learned. It's the same structuring, flagging, and delivery skills from Units 2 through 4, applied by multiple people at once and then deliberately stitched together. Most of what goes wrong in team presentations happens not because any individual section was weak, but because the stitching was skipped or rushed. Treat it as its own piece of work, worth its own planning time, and the rest tends to follow.

Good morning, everyone. I'm Elena Vasquez, and I'm joining the company as your new Head of Operations. Before I tell you about my background, I want to tell you what I actually believe, because I think it's more useful than a list of past job titles: I believe our biggest opportunity right now isn't cutting costs — it's cutting delay. **Body**

A shipment held an extra half-day at one node would ripple into missed windows three or four steps down the chain.

My second point is what we did about it there, because I don't want this to sound like an abstract theory. We ran a project focused entirely on identifying the three or four handoff points where delay was most likely to start, and we redesigned those specific points rather than trying to overhaul the whole system at once. Within eight months, our on-time delivery rate improved by eighteen percent, without adding headcount or budget — the gains came from fixing where the friction actually was, not from working harder everywhere.

My third point is what this means here. I've spent my first two weeks mostly listening — sitting in on your team meetings, walking the floor, asking questions rather than proposing answers. **Conclusion**

So to bring this back to where I started: I believe the fastest way to improve how we operate isn't a sweeping overhaul, it's finding the handful of places where delay starts and fixing those first. I've done this before, it worked, and I think the same approach applies here. Over the next month, I'll be reaching out to many of you directly to learn more about where you see friction in your own part of the process. Thank you for the warm welcome, and I'm looking forward to working with all of you.

# Appendix B — Model Speech: Business (Product/Service Launch)

## "Introducing Ledgerline" — Launch Address to Small-Business Partners and Press

Good morning, everyone, and thank you for making time to be here. Some of you have traveled a considerable distance for this, and I don't take that lightly. What I want to do over the next fifteen minutes is walk you through why we built the product we're launching today, what it actually does, and what we've already learned from the businesses who tested it before any of you saw it.

Let me start with the problem, because that's really where this began.

Over the past two years, our team spoke with more than three hundred small-business owners across retail, hospitality, and light manufacturing. We asked them a simple question: what part of running your business costs you the most time for the least value? The answer, in seven cases out of ten, was the same — reconciling cash flow across multiple systems. A typical small business we spoke with was using one tool for invoicing, a second for inventory, and a spreadsheet, usually built by someone who has since left the company, to try to make the first two talk to each other. One retailer we interviewed in Manila estimated she spent six hours a week just moving numbers between systems by hand. A restaurant group in Nairobi told us their finance lead had, at one point, three different "final" versions of the same monthly report circulating by email.

That's the gap we built Ledgerline to close. It's a single platform that connects your invoicing, your inventory, and your cash-flow forecasting in one place, so the numbers only have to be entered once and everything downstream updates automatically.

Here's what that looked like in practice during our pilot. We worked with forty-two small businesses over a six-month period, in five countries, across a deliberately varied set of industries — retail, food service, small-scale manufacturing, and professional services. Every one of these businesses had been using at least two disconnected systems before the pilot began.

The results were more consistent than we expected. Average time spent on manual reconciliation dropped by 71% across the pilot group — from just over five hours a week to under ninety minutes. That's not a marginal improvement; it's most of a business day given back every week. And it wasn't just about time. Eleven of the forty-two businesses in the pilot identified cash-flow shortfalls at least two weeks earlier than they would have under their old process, because the forecasting module flags a shortfall automatically instead of waiting for someone to notice it in a spreadsheet. One of those businesses, a small hardware retailer, used that early warning to renegotiate a supplier payment schedule before a shortfall actually hit — something the owner told us directly she would not have caught in time under her previous process.

We also tracked what didn't work as well, because I think it's worth being honest about that in a launch like this rather than only presenting the highlights. Businesses with very high transaction volumes — above roughly two thousand transactions a month — saw a smaller time-savings benefit, closer to 40%, because at that volume some manual review is still genuinely necessary regardless of the tool. We're not claiming Ledgerline replaces financial judgment. We're claiming it removes the mechanical work that used to stand between a business owner and the moment they could actually exercise that judgment.

So where does that leave us today. Starting this quarter, Ledgerline is available to small businesses in eighteen countries, with a tiered pricing structure based on transaction volume rather than a flat fee, because we heard clearly in our interviews that a flat fee structure disadvantages the smallest businesses relative to the value they get. We're also committing to a data-portability guarantee: any business that adopts Ledgerline can export its full transaction history, in a standard format, at any time, with no additional charge. We built that commitment in because trust in a financial tool is earned slowly and lost quickly, and we didn't want cost of switching to be the reason someone stayed with us.

Over the next few months, you'll hear more from us about specific industry configurations, and we'll be publishing the full pilot data set for anyone who wants to review the methodology directly rather than take our summary of it. For today, I just want to leave you with this: the businesses in our pilot didn't get a more impressive piece of software. They got roughly four hours of their week back, and eleven of them got an early warning that mattered. That's the outcome we set out to build toward, and it's the outcome we intend to keep measuring ourselves against as this rolls out more broadly.

Thank you. I'm glad to take questions.

# Appendix B — Model Speech: Government (Agency/Program Launch)

## Introducing the Regional Small Business Advisory Service — Remarks at Program Launch

Thank you all for being here today, including the business owners, community representatives, and colleagues from other agencies who took the time to join us. I want to use my time this morning to explain why we're launching the Regional Small Business Advisory Service, what it will actually do for the people who use it, and what we learned from the two-district pilot that led us to expand it region-wide starting this year.

I'll start with why this program exists at all.

Over the past eighteen months, our department reviewed licensing and permit applications from small businesses across the region, and one pattern stood out clearly enough that we couldn't ignore it. Nearly 40% of first-time applications were rejected or delayed, not because the underlying business was ineligible, but because of incomplete paperwork, missing supporting documents, or applications submitted to the wrong office entirely. On average, an applicant who was rejected on procedural grounds lost eleven weeks before their business could legally open. For a small business operating on limited startup capital, eleven weeks of delay is not a minor inconvenience. For several applicants we spoke with directly, it was the difference between opening and not opening at all.

So the problem wasn't a shortage of people wanting to start businesses, and it wasn't a shortage of legitimate businesses meeting our criteria. It was a navigation problem — a gap between what the process required and what applicants, especially first-time applicants, understood about how to meet those requirements.

That's the gap this service is built to close. The Regional Small Business Advisory Service provides every first-time applicant with a single point of contact — one advisor, assigned to their application from the day they submit initial paperwork through the day their license is issued or their application is formally resolved. That advisor's job is to review the application before formal submission, flag missing documentation immediately rather than after a multi-week processing queue, and route the applicant to the correct office the first time.

We piloted this model in two districts over the past year before deciding whether to expand it, and I want to walk you through what we found, including the parts that surprised us.

Across the two pilot districts, the procedural rejection rate for first-time applicants fell from 39% to 12%. Average time to a decision — approval or a clear, correctable rejection — dropped from eleven weeks to four. And in a survey we conducted independently of the advisory staff themselves, so as not to bias the results, 84% of applicants who used the service reported that they understood the licensing requirements clearly before submitting, compared with 46% of applicants in non-pilot districts surveyed over the same period.

We also want to be candid about the limits of what we found. The improvement was smaller for applications involving multiple agencies at once — for example, a business requiring both a health permit and a zoning variance — where the rejection rate fell from 44% to 27%, a real improvement, but a smaller one than for single-agency applications. That tells us the advisory model helps most where the core barrier is information and navigation, and helps less, though still meaningfully, where the barrier is genuine cross-agency coordination that a single advisor can smooth but not fully resolve alone. We're treating that as a next problem to solve, not a reason to hold back on what already works.

Based on these results, the service will be available in every district in the region by the end of this year, at no direct cost to applicants — it is funded through the department's existing operating budget, reallocated from processing capacity that this service is expected to free up over time, since a correctly filed application on the first attempt takes less staff time overall than a rejected and resubmitted one.

We'll be publishing district-level outcome data twice a year, so that the public, and frankly so that we ourselves, can see clearly whether this program is delivering the same results at full scale that we saw in the pilot. If it isn't, we intend to say so and adjust the model rather than simply continue running it as designed.

What this program is ultimately about is fairly simple. A business owner with a sound idea and the will to build it shouldn't lose eleven weeks, or lose the business entirely, because a form went to the wrong office. That's the outcome we measured in the pilot, and it's the outcome this expansion is built to deliver at the scale of the whole region.

Thank you, and I'm happy to take questions.
