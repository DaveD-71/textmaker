## 3.1. Green Finance Initiatives

### 3.1.1. Goal

Explain what makes a green bond different from an ordinary bond, and why standard-setters and regulators have moved to define the "green" label.

### 3.1.2. Reading

Two companies borrow the same amount of money to build the same wind farm. The first sells an ordinary bond and can spend the money however it likes; no one checks afterwards. The second sells a green bond, and that label is a promise with rules attached. The company must spend the proceeds only on defined environmental projects, keep that money in a separate account, and report later on where it went and what it achieved [1]. The wind farm is identical. What differs is the commitment about the use of proceeds and the disclosure that comes with it.

Green finance means money raised specifically for environmentally beneficial activity, mostly through green bonds. The European Investment Bank sold the first one in 2007, and the market grew slowly until common rules appeared [2]. Since 2014 most issuers have followed the Green Bond Principles, a voluntary standard from the International Capital Market Association. It rests on four duties: define the eligible projects, explain how they are chosen, track the money separately, and report on both spending and results [1]. By early 2025 the green bond market was worth about US$2.9 trillion, with roughly US$700 billion issued during 2024 [3].

Because "green" is otherwise a label an issuer picks for itself, regulators have begun to define it. The European Union's Green Bond Standard has applied since December 2024. A bond may be called a "European Green Bond" only if at least 85% of the proceeds fund activities on the EU's official taxonomy, its classified list of sustainable activities, with an external review before and after issuance [4]. The goal is to limit greenwashing, which means claiming an environmental benefit that the label does not deliver.

Japan has taken a distinctive step at government level. In February 2024 it became the first national government to sell climate transition bonds, in two parts of JPY 800 billion each, JPY 1.6 trillion together [5]. The full programme aims to raise about JPY 20 trillion over roughly a decade to help heavy industry, power and transport cut emissions. Japan plans to repay the bonds partly from future carbon-pricing revenue, such as a fossil-fuel levy due from 2028, rather than from general taxes [5]. Transition bonds are more debated than ordinary green bonds, because they fund high-polluting sectors that are trying to change rather than activities that are already clean.

Emerging economies use these tools too. Chile sold Latin America's first sovereign green bond in 2019 and by late 2025 had about US$56 billion of labelled sustainable debt outstanding, more than any other developing country [6]. Across all these issuers, the bond itself is ordinary borrowing; what investors are paying for is the enforceable promise to spend and disclose in a set way. That is why the argument over green finance now centres on the standards and the outside checks, not on the wind farms and rail lines the money builds.

### 3.1.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** green bond, use of proceeds, taxonomy, greenwashing, transition bond, external review

**Recycled terms:** bond, issuer, regulator

## 3.2. Infrastructure Investment

### 3.2.1. Goal

Explain the gap between the infrastructure the world needs and what it will pay for, and show who fills that gap besides national governments.

### 3.2.2. Reading

It is natural to think that roads, bridges, power grids and water systems are built and paid for by government. Governments plan and own most infrastructure, and often raise the money through government bonds, but they have never been its only funders. The shortfall between what the world invests and what it needs is now beyond public budgets alone. The Global Infrastructure Hub, set up by the Group of Twenty, estimates a need of about US$97 trillion of infrastructure investment worldwide by 2040, against roughly US$79 trillion likely to be spent, leaving an infrastructure funding gap of around US$18 trillion [1].

The gap has two forms. One is capacity that is never built. The other, common in richer countries, is old infrastructure that now needs expensive repair. In 2025 the American Society of Civil Engineers graded United States infrastructure at C, up from C-minus in 2021, but still identified a US$3.7 trillion shortfall over ten years [2]. A broader estimate puts the European Union's yearly gap on strategic investment, covering climate, digital and infrastructure needs together, at about EUR 481 billion [3].

Japan shows the ageing problem in sharp form. It built most of its expressways, bridges and tunnels between the 1950s and 1970s. Official projections say that by 2033 about 60% of its roughly 730,000 road bridges will be at least 50 years old, the point where damage spreads quickly and full repair or replacement becomes unavoidable [4]. Japan still spends heavily on public works, but a rising share now goes to maintaining and strengthening what exists rather than building new, and the country's shrinking workforce makes the repairs harder to staff, not just to fund.

So who fills the gap? One answer is private money through public-private partnerships, or PPPs. In a PPP a private company designs, builds, finances and operates a public asset for a fixed period, called a concession, earning tolls or fees before ownership returns to the state. Kenya's Nairobi Expressway, a 27-kilometre elevated toll road that opened in 2022, was built this way by a Chinese state contractor. The contractor paid about 80% of the roughly US$668 million cost and collects tolls for about three decades before handing the road over [5]. It was Kenya's first toll expressway.

Multilateral development banks are another source: lenders owned by many governments together. The World Bank Group committed US$118.5 billion in its 2025 financial year and helped attract a further US$69.9 billion of private capital alongside it [6]. Much of this is blended finance, meaning public money used to reduce the risk on a project until private investors, such as the pension funds and insurers that count as institutional investors, will put their own money in. Each source has a limit. Public budgets compete with health and pensions; PPP tolls can make an asset costly to use; development-bank loans add to national debt. Filling the gap is less about finding a single payer than about matching each project to the mix of money it can realistically carry.

### 3.2.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** public-private partnership (PPP), infrastructure funding gap, multilateral development bank (MDB), concession, blended finance, institutional investor

**Recycled terms:** government bond, capital, risk

## 3.3. Trade Policies and Tariffs

### 3.3.1. Goal

Explain who bears the cost of a tariff and how tariffs and trade agreements pull an open economy in opposite directions.

### 3.3.2. Reading

Who actually pays a tariff? Legally the answer is clear: a tariff is a tax on imported goods, and the importer of record, the company bringing the goods across the border, pays it to customs rather than the foreign seller [1]. It is a cross-border tax, collected where goods enter the country. Economically it is less clear. The cost can be split three ways: the exporter may cut its price, the importer may accept a thinner margin, or the final buyer may pay more. Studies of the tariffs the United States raised sharply in 2025 disagree on the split. One Federal Reserve analysis found about 90% of the cost fell on US firms and consumers [1]; another, which weighted goods by pre-tariff trade volumes, found foreign exporters absorbed close to half through lower prices [2]. This share, called the pass-through, depends on how much pricing power the exporter has.

Those 2025 tariffs were unusually large. The average effective tariff rate rose from about 2.4% in January 2025 to a peak near 28% in the spring, then settled around 17% by the autumn, the highest level since the 1930s [3]. In February 2026 the US Supreme Court ruled that the emergency law used for many of these tariffs did not authorise them. The government replaced them the same day under a different law, and the average rate fell to about 7% by mid-2026 [4].

Countries facing these tariffs responded in two very different ways. Japan negotiated. Under a framework agreed in July 2025, most US tariffs on Japanese goods were set at 15%, down from a threatened 25%, and the tariff on Japanese cars was cut from 25% to 15%. In exchange, Japan pledged up to US$550 billion of investment in US industries such as semiconductors and energy by 2029 [5].

India did not get a deal. In August 2025 the US placed a combined 50% tariff on Indian goods, a standard rate plus a 25% penalty tied to India's purchases of discounted Russian oil. The higher tariff hit an estimated US$48 billion of India's exports, and India's government called it "unfair, unjustified and unreasonable" [6]. The same tool, a border tax on imports, was used in one case as a bargaining chip and in the other as a punishment.

Japan's exposure explains why it bargained hard. In 2025 the United States became Japan's largest export market, taking about 18.6% of its exports, with cars the single biggest category [5]. A tariff is a barrier a government can raise at will. A free trade agreement does the opposite: it is a standing promise to keep barriers low. Japan belongs to one such agreement, the CPTPP, which since 2018 has removed tariffs on about 95% of the goods traded among its member countries, well below the standard most-favoured-nation (MFN) tariff each would otherwise charge [7]. None of the 2025 rates is fixed: levels moved several times within two years, and the US-China rate rose above 100% before a truce brought it down [3]. For an economy that sends nearly a fifth of its exports to one market, that risk is itself a cost, because firms must plan around a border tax that may change again before their goods arrive.

**Recycled terms:** cross-border, market, risk

## 3.4. Pension Reform

### 3.4.1. Goal

Explain why ageing populations force governments to change public pension systems, and compare the main levers they use.

### 3.4.2. Reading

In April 2023, after months of strikes, France passed a law raising its minimum retirement age from 62 to 64, phased in three months at a time by year of birth [4]. It was meant to be complete by 2030. Instead, in late 2025, under budget pressure and renewed protests, France's parliament voted to suspend the increase until after the 2027 presidential election, freezing the minimum age at 62 years and 9 months [4]. Even pausing a pension reform has a cost, and France's stop-start attempt shows how hard this lever is to pull.

Most public pensions are pay-as-you-go: today's workers' contributions pay today's retirees, with little capital saved in advance. A funded system, by contrast, invests contributions ahead of time, often in government bonds and shares. When retirees grow in number faster than workers, a pay-as-you-go system's sums stop balancing, and the risk of a future shortfall grows. Governments then have four levers: raise the retirement age, raise the contribution rate, cut the benefit formula, or bring more people into the system. Recent reforms around the world are variations on these four.

The United States acted early and then stalled. In 1983 it raised the statutory full retirement age from 65 to 67, phased in by birth year, a change still completing today [3]. No major reform has followed. Its main retirement trust fund is now projected to run down in late 2032, after which incoming payroll taxes would cover only about 78% of promised benefits unless Congress acts [3].

South Korea pulled a different lever. In March 2025 its National Assembly approved the first increase in the pension contribution rate in nearly three decades, raising it in yearly steps from 9% of wages toward 13% by 2033 [5]. Raising contributions spreads the cost onto today's workers rather than delaying their retirement, but it is politically easier to pass than a higher pension age.

Japan has been adjusting continuously for 30 years. It legislated a rise in the pension age from 60 to 65 in the 1990s, only finishing the phase-in for men in 2025, and lifted the contribution rate in yearly steps to a fixed 18.3% of salary by 2017. Since 2004 an automatic mechanism, nicknamed the "macroeconomic slide," quietly trims how fast benefits grow as the workforce shrinks. Japan's own projections show the replacement rate — pension income as a share of working-age income — falling from about 61% now toward a legal floor of 50% by 2057 [6]. Its most recent reform, in 2025, uses the fourth lever: widening coverage to draw an estimated 1.8 million more part-time workers into the system [1].

The pressure behind all these changes is the same. That measure, the old-age dependency ratio, stood at 33 people aged 65 or older for every 100 of working age across the OECD in 2025, up from 22 in 2000, and is projected to reach 52 by 2050 [2]. The retirement age is the lever that matches that arithmetic most directly, and the one governments find hardest to hold in place, as France has just shown.

## 3.5. Global Economic Recovery Post-COVID

### 3.5.1. Goal

Use the 2020 pandemic downturn to show how governments respond to a global shock, and why recoveries from the same shock can diverge so sharply.

### 3.5.2. Reading

In the first months of 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic shut down travel, trade and daily commerce across most of the world at once, producing a recession that reached almost every country in the same quarter. Global output fell about 3.3% that year, a deeper one-year drop than the 2008 financial crisis [1]. The fall was uneven from the start: the euro area shrank 6.8%, close to double the decline in the United States, because lockdowns in parts of Europe were stricter and longer [2]. What followed was not one recovery but several, at very different speeds, and the distance between them is the real subject six years on.

Governments and central banks responded on a scale never seen in peacetime. Governments used fiscal stimulus — extra spending and tax relief — running to trillions of dollars: the United States passed a large emergency package, and the European Union agreed its first jointly financed recovery fund. Central banks used monetary easing, making borrowing cheaper. The European Central Bank ran a bond-buying programme, a form of quantitative easing (QE), that grew from EUR 750 billion to EUR 1,850 billion, mostly buying government bonds to hold down borrowing costs [4]. The shared principle was to spend whatever it took to stop a health emergency becoming a lasting depression. In the countries that could afford it, that worked: the feared collapse did not happen.

The support was deeply unequal between countries. Rich economies could borrow cheaply and spend heavily; poorer ones could not, and spent far less as a share of their economies. By the World Bank's assessment in early 2026, roughly a quarter of developing economies were still poorer per person than in 2019, while almost every advanced economy had grown richer over the same period [5]. Economists call this a K-shaped recovery, in which some parts rise while others keep falling, and the lost ground in the developing world is an example of economic scarring — lasting damage a downturn leaves behind.

Even among wealthy countries the experience varied. The recovery brought a burst of inflation in 2022: United States consumer prices rose 9.1% over a year, the fastest in four decades [3], and the euro area's peak was higher still, above 10%. Japan's core inflation peaked much lower, at 4.2% in January 2023 [6]. After 25 years of near-zero or falling prices, even that felt sharp — Japan's fastest pace since 1981 — yet it was less than half the shock felt in Washington or Frankfurt. Japan also reopened its borders later and took longer to close its output gap, the distance between what an economy produces and what it could produce at full capacity: a slower cycle, not a sharper one.

The episode is now used as evidence that a fast, large response prevents the worst. But it left two unequal legacies: much higher public debt in the rich world, and lost ground in the developing world that no rebound has yet recovered. The open question for the next global shock is whether that speed and size can be repeated without again widening the gap between countries that can pay for a recovery and those that cannot.

## 4.1. Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Regulations

### 4.1.1. Goal

Explain how governments try to keep the proceeds of crime out of the financial system by making banks the front line of detection.

### 4.1.2. Reading

At nine in the morning an analyst on a bank's compliance team opens a flagged payment: EUR 4 million moving from a company account opened three weeks ago to three firms abroad that were themselves registered only days earlier. Nothing is plainly illegal, but the pattern fits money laundering — moving the proceeds of crime through the financial system until the money looks legitimate when it comes out. The analyst must decide whether to release the payment, hold it, and file a suspicious-transaction report with the national financial-intelligence unit. Multiply that decision by millions a day and you have the front line of anti-money-laundering (AML) policy.

The problem is large but only roughly measured. The UN Office on Drugs and Crime still cites an old IMF estimate that 2–5% of world output is laundered each year, very roughly US$800 billion to US$2 trillion [1]. Governments cannot watch every transaction themselves, so the law transfers the work to banks: identify each customer and the real person who controls a company, monitor for unusual activity, report what looks suspicious, and screen names against sanctions lists.

The global rulebook is set by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF), created by the G7 in 1989. Its standards shape law in more than 200 countries and territories, and it reviews each member through a peer "mutual evaluation," publishing a "grey list" of countries under increased monitoring [2]. A weak review raises the cost and friction of every cross-border payment a country's banks make, which gives even reluctant governments a reason to comply.

Japan's system shows that pressure at work. The FATF's 2021 review found Japan's controls were "delivering results" but placed the country in "enhanced follow-up," a status that requires regular progress reports. Tokyo responded with a burst of legislation, amending two laws to bring crypto-asset exchanges fully into the regime, including the "travel rule" that identifying information must accompany a transfer. Suspicious-transaction reports flow to JAFIC, a unit inside the National Police Agency, while the Financial Services Agency, Japan's main financial regulator, supervises the banks [3].

The sharpest pressure, though, is financial. Denmark's largest bank, Danske Bank, let about EUR 200 billion of suspicious, mostly non-resident money pass through one small Estonian branch between 2007 and 2015; in December 2022 it pleaded guilty in the United States and forfeited about US$2 billion [4]. In October 2024 TD Bank became the largest US bank ever to plead guilty to Bank Secrecy Act failures, paying around US$3 billion and accepting a legal cap on its US growth [5]. After its own run of scandals, the European Union set up a central Anti-Money Laundering Authority in Frankfurt, which from 2028 will directly supervise around 40 of the highest-risk cross-border firms [6]. Each scandal has been followed by a larger penalty or a new supervisor — an admission that the last line of defence was not enough.

## 4.2. Financial Literacy Programs

### 4.2.1. Goal

Weigh the evidence on whether organised financial-education programmes actually change how people manage money.

### 4.2.2. Reading

Does teaching people about money actually change what they do? For years the research said: barely. That answer is now contested, and the disagreement is the most useful thing about this topic.

Financial literacy programmes are organised efforts — school courses, adult workshops, national strategies, media campaigns — to raise people's ability to make sound money decisions. Governments run or coordinate them because surveys keep finding large gaps in basic knowledge. The OECD's 2023 survey of 39 countries and economies produced an average score of 60 out of 100, and in many countries only about a third of adults can correctly answer three simple questions on interest, inflation and spreading risk [1].

The sceptical case came from a 2014 review of many earlier studies, which found that financial education explained only a fraction of a percent of later financial behaviour, and that whatever effect it had faded within about two years. That fed an argument that better default options and "just-in-time" guidance — help delivered exactly when a person faces a real decision — would beat classroom teaching.

The more recent evidence is more positive. A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 76 randomised experiments covering more than 160,000 people across 33 countries. It found financial education has real effects on both knowledge and behaviour — at least three times as large as the 2014 estimate, comparable to teaching in other subjects, with the strongest results for budgeting, saving and borrowing [2]. The honest summary is not "it works" or "it does not," but that well-designed programmes delivered near a real decision show measurable effects, while poorly designed or badly timed ones show little.

Design and delivery show up in how countries act. In the United States, where the requirement is set state by state, the number of states guaranteeing every public high-school student a standalone personal-finance course rose from a handful around 2020 to 25 by 2024, covering just over half of students [3]. Studies of earlier state mandates find they improve young adults' credit scores and cut their use of high-cost borrowing [4]. The United Kingdom took a different route: its Money and Pensions Service runs a ten-year national strategy, launched in 2020, that sets numerical targets and coordinates banks, charities and schools rather than delivering all the teaching itself [5].

Japan made financial education a priority for two concrete reasons. In April 2022 the age of majority dropped from 20 to 18, so first-year university students can now take out loans and credit cards without a parent's consent, and revised school guidelines added household budgeting and "asset formation" to home-economics classes. Then in August 2024 the government launched a dedicated body, J-FLEC, jointly backed by the Financial Services Agency, Japan's financial regulator, and the Bank of Japan [6]. Its aim is to coordinate teaching as a form of consumer protection: education free of a seller's incentive to push its own products. Whether a new agency and a school requirement change behaviour is exactly the open question the research leaves.

## 4.3. Wealth Inequality and Redistribution Policies

### 4.3.1. Goal

Explain how wealth inequality is measured and which redistribution tools actually move the numbers.

### 4.3.2. Reading

The richest 10% of the world's adults own about 76% of all household wealth. The poorest half own about 2% [1]. The gap in what people own is far wider than the gap in what they earn, because wealth compounds over time and passes between generations, while a wage stops when the work does.

Inequality is usually summed up in one number, the Gini coefficient, which runs from 0 (everyone equal) to 1 (one person owns everything). In the United States, Federal Reserve data show the wealthiest 1% of households owned about 30.9% of roughly US$162 trillion in household wealth in 2024, while the bottom half owned about 2.5%, down from around 3.4% in 1989 [2]. Across the OECD, taxes and cash transfers narrow the income gap — by more in high-transfer systems such as France and the Nordic countries, and by less in the United States and South Korea.

The redistribution toolkit has four main parts: progressive income tax, which takes a larger share as income rises; transfer payments such as pensions and child benefit; taxes on capital gains and inheritance; and, more rarely, an annual tax on a person's total assets. That last tool has mostly been tried and dropped. The number of OECD countries levying an annual net wealth tax fell from 12 in 1990 to just three by 2020 — Norway, Spain and Switzerland [3]. The common reasons: the taxes raised little, were hard to value and enforce, and prompted wealthy residents to move assets abroad or leave.

Japan shows that a tax system's design matters more than its headline rates. Its income inequality is moderate by rich-country standards, but its effective tax rate actually falls for the very highest earners. Income from selling shares or receiving dividends is taxed at a flat 20%, while employment income is taxed progressively up to about 45%. The wealthiest take more of their income as investment returns. So the average rate can start dropping once annual income passes about JPY 100 million — the so-called "JPY 100 million wall." The government's answer, phased in from 2025, is a minimum tax: at least 22.5% on income above JPY 330 million, tightening further from 2027 [6].

The same problem is now on the international agenda. Brazil, one of the world's most unequal large economies, used its 2024 G20 presidency to commission a blueprint from the economist Gabriel Zucman for a coordinated minimum tax on the ultra-rich. Individuals worth more than US$1 billion would pay at least 2% of their wealth each year; applied to roughly 3,000 known billionaires, that would raise an estimated US$200–250 billion, against an effective rate today of about 0.3% [4]. In July 2024 G20 finance ministers issued a first joint statement on taxing the very wealthy, though it is non-binding and the US and Germany were among the holdouts [5]. Taxing wealth, the episode shows, is less about rates than about which kinds of income and assets are taxed lightly — and the wealthiest hold exactly those.

## 4.4. Corporate Governance and Accountability

### 4.4.1. Goal

Trace how corporate-governance rules developed — mostly after scandals — and explain the chain of accountability they try to enforce.

### 4.4.2. Reading

It is often said that shareholders own a company, so shareholders run it, and that a board of directors works for the chief executive. Both statements are wrong, and correcting them is the heart of the topic. Shareholders elect a board; the board is supposed to hire, monitor, pay and, if necessary, fire the management on the owners' behalf. Corporate governance is the set of rules and checks that tries to make that chain of accountability actually work, and it has developed mostly in response to failures.

The first modern code was Britain's Cadbury Report of 1992, written after a run of company collapses and frauds. It recommended separating the roles of chair and chief executive, using independent non-executive directors brought in from outside, and setting up audit committees; its lasting idea was "comply or explain" — a company may depart from the code, but must publicly say why [1]. The United States went further after Enron collapsed in 2001: the 2002 Sarbanes-Oxley Act made governance duties statutory, requiring chief executives and finance chiefs to personally certify the accounts and creating a new body to police the auditors.

The pattern — scandal, then rules — repeated elsewhere. In 2009 the founder of the Indian IT firm Satyam confessed to inflating its accounts for years, and the scandal fed directly into India's Companies Act 2013, requiring listed boards to include independent directors. In Germany, Wirecard, a payments company in the blue-chip DAX index, collapsed in June 2020 after admitting that about EUR 1.9 billion of cash on its books had probably never existed. Its auditor had signed off for a decade, while the regulator had spent its energy investigating the journalists who raised the alarm [2].

Japan has since run one of the most sustained governance-reform programmes of any large economy. Before 2014 many listed Japanese firms had few independent directors and held blocks of each other's shares to cement business ties, not to earn a return. A Stewardship Code in 2014 and Japan's first Corporate Governance Code in 2015, both on "comply or explain," pushed companies to add outside directors, disclose more, and unwind those cross-shareholdings [3]. In 2023 the Tokyo Stock Exchange went further, publicly pressing the roughly half of its top-tier "Prime" companies trading below the value of their own net assets to publish plans to improve capital efficiency [4]. The reforms are credited as one trigger for the strong 2023–24 rise in Japanese share prices.

Governance also works from the other direction, when shareholders act through the board. In 2021 a tiny activist fund, Engine No. 1, holding about 0.02% of ExxonMobil, won three of twelve board seats by persuading large institutional investors — index funds and public pension funds — to back its candidates [5]. It was the first board election decided on climate strategy, and a concrete demonstration that the owners-elect-the-board mechanism can change a company's direction [6]. Three decades of codes and laws have not prevented every scandal; what they have built is a chain of accountability that holds when someone chooses to pull on it.

## 4.5. Economic Diplomacy and International Cooperation

### 4.5.1. Goal

Show how governments use financial and trade ties as foreign-policy tools, and why doing so is costly to the country that tries it.

### 4.5.2. Reading

Economic diplomacy is the use of trade, investment, aid and financial ties — including the threat of cutting them off — as tools of foreign policy. It runs on two tracks: coercion, such as sanctions and asset freezes, and cooperation, such as coordinated crisis lending.

The clearest recent case of coercion is the response to Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. A coalition of all G7 economies and the EU froze roughly US$300 billion of the Russian central bank's reserves, cut major Russian banks out of SWIFT, the main messaging network for cross-border payments, and capped Russian seaborne oil at US$60 a barrel [1]. It was the largest coordinated use of financial sanctions ever applied to a major economy. About EUR 200 billion of the frozen reserves sit at a single securities depository in Belgium. Rather than seize the principal, the EU redirects the profits those assets earn to Ukraine; in 2024 the G7 agreed a roughly US$50 billion loan, repayable from that future revenue [1].

The episode works because deep financial connections are themselves leverage, concentrated in the US dollar. The dollar dominates reserves, trade invoicing and cross-border payments, so the United States can impose "secondary sanctions" — penalising firms in third countries for dealing with a sanctioned party. The cooperative track uses the same connections for shared problems: the G20 was raised to leaders' level during the 2008 financial crisis, and it later worked with the IMF and World Bank to restructure the debts of the poorest countries.

Climate finance is the clearest current example of that cooperative track, and of its limits. Rich countries pledged at Copenhagen in 2009 to mobilise US$100 billion a year for developing countries by 2020, a target met only around 2022 [2]. At COP29 in Baku in November 2024 they agreed a new goal — at least US$300 billion a year by 2035, within a broader aspiration of US$1.3 trillion — which many developing countries called far too low [3].

Japan's handling of the 2022 sanctions shows the balancing act involved. It joined the G7 measures despite importing almost all its oil and gas, but drew one line. The trading houses Mitsui and Mitsubishi kept their 12.5% and 10% stakes in the Sakhalin-2 gas project, which supplies about 9% of Japan's liquefied natural gas [4], and Tokyo secured a G7 carve-out exempting Sakhalin from the price cap [5]. The reasoning was blunt: solidarity with allies matters, but a country that cannot fuel its power stations has no foreign policy at all.

Every use of leverage has a cost to the side applying it. Europe absorbed a severe energy-price and inflation shock in 2022, and Russian oil was simply re-routed, often sold above the cap. Targets also gain a reason to build alternatives, as China has with the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank and a growing yuan-based payments network offered as an alternative to dollar clearing [6]. The 2022 sanctions imposed large costs without forcing a change of course — which is why economic diplomacy is best described as leverage, not control.
