## 1.1. Cryptocurrency Regulation

### 1.1.1. Goal

Explain why governments have moved to regulate crypto-assets, and how the main approaches around the world differ.

### 1.1.2. Reading

Cryptocurrencies are digital assets recorded on a shared database called a blockchain. They can be sent between people without a bank in the middle. That design has advantages, but it also makes some old problems harder to control. Without a bank, it is harder to check who is sending money, harder to stop fraud, and harder to protect people who do not understand the risk.

For years, most governments watched from a distance. Two kinds of event changed that. The first was theft. In 2014 the Tokyo exchange Mt. Gox collapsed after losing a large amount of customer bitcoin, and in 2018 another Japanese exchange, Coincheck, lost about US$530 million of a crypto-asset in a hack [1]. The second was collapse. In November 2022, FTX, then one of the world's largest exchanges, failed in a few days with at least US$8 billion of customer money missing; its founder was later sentenced to 25 years in prison for fraud [2]. In both kinds of case, firms that looked established turned out to be poorly supervised.

Governments have responded in different ways. Japan acted early: from 2017, a company running a crypto exchange has had to register with the Financial Services Agency and follow rules on customer protection [1]. The European Union chose a single large framework. Its Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, known as MiCA, applies across all member states; its rules for stablecoins started on 30 June 2024 and its rules for exchanges on 30 December 2024 [3]. The United States moved later and more narrowly: its first federal crypto law, the GENIUS Act, was signed in July 2025 and covers only payment stablecoins [4].

A stablecoin is a crypto-asset meant to hold a fixed value, usually equal to one dollar or one euro. All three approaches treat stablecoins carefully. Under MiCA and the GENIUS Act, an issuer must hold safe assets equal to every coin it puts into circulation and publish regular reports [3][4]. In Japan, only a licensed bank, a registered fund-transfer firm, or a trust company may issue one for the public [1]. The shared worry is that a coin advertised as "always worth one dollar" could fail if the assets behind it are not real or not safe.

The hard part is timing and balance. Rules that are too strict, or that arrive too late, can push activity into countries with weaker supervision. Rules that are too loose leave customers exposed, as Mt. Gox, Coincheck and FTX all showed. Because crypto moves easily across borders, regulators increasingly compare notes, even though their laws still differ.

## 1.2. Central Bank Digital Currencies

### 1.2.1. Goal

Explain what a central bank digital currency is, why most central banks are studying one, and why almost none have launched.

### 1.2.2. Reading

More than nine in ten of the world's central banks are working on a digital version of their national money. In a 2024 survey by the Bank for International Settlements, 85 of the 93 central banks that replied said they were exploring one [1]. Yet by 2026 only three small economies had actually launched one for everyday public use, and use in all three has been very low.

A central bank digital currency, or CBDC, is official money issued in digital form by the central bank itself. The cash in your pocket is already a claim on the central bank. The money in your bank account, by contrast, is a claim on a commercial bank. A "retail" CBDC would be central-bank money that ordinary people could hold directly, without a bank in between, and use for daily payments. A separate "wholesale" type, used only for settlement between financial institutions, is less controversial and further along.

The launches so far are small and disappointing. The Bahamas issued the world's first, the Sand Dollar, in October 2020. Jamaica followed in 2022, and Nigeria launched the eNaira in 2021, the first in Africa. Take-up has been weak in every case. The International Monetary Fund found that the large majority of eNaira digital wallets had never been used at all [2].

The larger economies are still testing. China has run by far the biggest trial. Its digital yuan had handled a cumulative 7 trillion yuan, about US$1 trillion, in transactions by mid-2024, across 17 regions [3]. But much of that use was pushed by the government, through things like staff payments, rather than chosen by shoppers, who already rely on Alipay and WeChat Pay. The European Central Bank ran a "preparation phase" from 2023 and moved to the next stage in October 2025. Even so, a digital euro cannot be issued unless EU lawmakers pass a new law, and a first issuance would not come before 2029 [4]. The Bank of Japan has run a pilot since 2023 and says no decision to issue has been made. Sweden's Riksbank studied an e-krona for years and then concluded there was not yet enough need for one.

The reasons given for the work are mostly defensive. Central banks want to keep a public form of money available as cash use falls, to make payments faster and cheaper, and to reduce dependence on a few large private payment firms and on privately issued stablecoins. The reasons for caution are just as practical: people might pull money out of banks in a crisis, a digital record raises privacy questions, and the public has so far shown little appetite for it.

### 1.2.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** central bank digital currency (CBDC), retail CBDC, wholesale CBDC, claim (on the central bank), pilot, disintermediation

**Recycled terms:** central bank, stablecoin

## 1.3. Financial Technology (Fintech) Regulation

### 1.3.1. Goal

Compare the main ways governments regulate fintech, and show why a firm licensed in one country usually cannot operate in another.

### 1.3.2. Reading

"Fintech" means financial services delivered mainly through software: app-based payments, online lending, digital-only banks. As these firms grew, governments had to decide how to supervise them. In 2016 the United Kingdom's Financial Conduct Authority tried a new answer, the world's first "regulatory sandbox" [1]. A sandbox lets a new firm test a product with real customers for a limited time, under tailored rules and close supervision. The idea spread fast. By 2020 the World Bank counted 73 sandboxes in 57 countries [2]. But a sandbox is only one tool, and countries have taken very different overall approaches.

The European Union chose broad, EU-wide law. Its Payment Services Directive, known as PSD2, took effect in 2018. It created the legal basis for "open banking" by requiring banks to let licensed third parties reach customer payment accounts, usually through a standard software connection called an API [3]. A firm authorised in one EU country can then offer services across all the others. The EU agreed a further update, PSD3, in late 2025.

The United States has no single fintech law or regulator. A money-transfer firm must be licensed separately in each state where it operates. A firm serving the whole country may therefore need dozens of licences, which raises the cost of launching and tends to favour large, well-funded companies. Attempts to write one national open-banking rule have so far stalled in the courts.

India built public infrastructure instead of a broad rulebook. Its Unified Payments Interface, launched in 2016 and overseen by the Reserve Bank of India, processed more than 240 billion transactions in the 2025–26 financial year [4]. That makes it the world's largest real-time payment system, and it is free to use for individuals. Private apps compete on top of it, but the underlying rails are public, so no single company controls access.

Japan's Financial Services Agency uses a lighter, cooperation-based model. A 2017 amendment to the Banking Act, in force from 2018, required banks to publish open-API policies and to register the payment and data firms they connect with. A 2020 change then split non-bank money-transfer providers into three licence tiers by transfer size. Only the tier for the largest transfers needs full FSA authorisation; the two smaller tiers need registration only.

Because each of these frameworks is written into national law, they do not travel. A payment firm licensed in London, Frankfurt, Mumbai or Tokyo must usually get a fresh authorisation before serving customers elsewhere. That is why fintech firms expand country by country, not all at once, and why a service that is normal in one market can be unavailable in the next.

## 1.4. Data Privacy and Protection

### 1.4.1. Goal

Explain how personal financial data is protected by law, and why moving that data between countries is the hardest part.

### 1.4.2. Reading

In 2023 an Irish regulator fined the technology company Meta €1.2 billion. It was the largest privacy fine ever imposed in the European Union [1]. Meta's mistake was not losing the data or selling it. It was moving Facebook users' personal information from Europe to the United States without enough legal protection against access by US authorities. The case shows what modern data-privacy law is mainly about. It is not just keeping data safe. It is controlling where the data goes and who can reach it.

Financial firms hold large amounts of sensitive personal data: account numbers, transaction histories, credit records, copies of identity documents. They want to use it, for credit scoring, fraud checks and new services. Privacy law sets the limits on how they can. It usually requires a clear reason for collecting each piece of data, the person's consent, and a duty to keep only what is needed.

The European Union's rules, known as the GDPR, have applied since 2018 and let regulators impose very large fines. By early 2025, EU regulators had issued close to €6 billion in GDPR fines in total, and companies were reporting hundreds of data breaches every day [2]. Other regions rely more on penalties after a specific breach. After a 2017 attack on the US credit bureau Equifax exposed the records of about 147 million people, the company agreed to pay up to US$700 million to settle claims from US regulators and states [3]. The United States still has no single national privacy law. California and several other states have written their own.

Japan was an early mover. Its Act on the Protection of Personal Information dates from 2003. Updates in 2017 and 2022 strengthened individual rights and made serious data breaches reportable to the Personal Information Protection Commission. In 2019 the EU and Japan recognised each other's systems as offering "adequate" protection [4]. Personal data can now flow both ways without extra paperwork. It was the first such mutual arrangement the EU concluded. Newer laws elsewhere, such as India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act of 2023, follow similar lines [5].

The unresolved problem is cross-border transfer. Data moves around the world in seconds, but privacy rules stop at national borders. A company can be careful and fully lawful at home and still break the law by sending the same data to a country whose protections, or whose government surveillance powers, are judged too weak. That is exactly what the Meta fine was about. For a bank or payment firm that runs its systems across several countries, deciding where customer data may legally be stored and processed has become a routine part of the job.

**New terms:** personal data, data breach, data protection authority, consent, cross-border data transfer, adequacy decision

**Recycled terms:** regulator, supervision, consumer protection

## 1.5. Financial Inclusion Initiatives

### 1.5.1. Goal

Explain what financial inclusion means, how developing countries have widened access to accounts, and why it means something different in a rich country.

### 1.5.2. Reading

About 1.3 billion adults worldwide still have no bank account of any kind. That is far fewer than a decade ago. The share of adults with an account rose from roughly half in 2011 to nearly 80% by 2024 [1]. But progress has been uneven, and most of the people still left out live in just eight countries.

"Financial inclusion" means giving people who are shut out of the formal system practical access to an account, payments, savings and credit. In lower-income countries the main tools have been mobile technology and public infrastructure.

Mobile money has been the biggest change in Sub-Saharan Africa. M-Pesa was launched by the telecoms company Safaricom in Kenya in 2007. It lets people hold and send money using a basic phone, with cash paid in and out through local shop agents rather than bank branches. Sub-Saharan Africa is now the only region where more adults have a mobile money account than a bank account [1]. In Kenya itself, the share of adults with access to formal financial services has reached about 85% [2].

Governments have also built the infrastructure directly. India's Jan Dhan programme, started in 2014, had opened more than 500 million basic bank accounts by the mid-2020s. Most are held by women and rural residents. Brazil's central bank launched an instant-payment system called Pix in 2020. Within about a year, two-thirds of Brazilian adults were using it, including millions who had never made an electronic transfer before [3].

Having an account, though, is not the same as using one. In India, about 16% of account holders have an inactive account — roughly four times the rate in other middle-income countries [1]. A programme can put a card in someone's hand and still not change how they manage money.

In a rich country the problem is different again. Japan is effectively fully banked, so inclusion is less about opening a first account. It is more about how people pay and who is left behind as things change. Japan has stayed unusually cash-reliant: the government reported that the cashless share of consumer spending reached about 43% in 2024, meeting its 40% target a year early [4]. The concerns are for elderly and rural customers as bank branches and cash machines close, and for foreign residents who face language and paperwork barriers to opening an account. The historical inclusion tool was the post office. Japan Post Bank, with roots in a savings system from 1875 and around 24,000 branches, long made sure that even remote areas had somewhere to save. Whether the goal is a first account in Kenya or keeping cash access in rural Japan, the harder part is not reaching people once but making the service one they will actually use.

## 2.1. Regulatory Responses to Market Volatility

### 2.1.1. Goal

Explain how exchanges and regulators try to slow a falling market, using circuit breakers and trading halts, and why these tools are debated.

### 2.1.2. Reading

On 5 August 2024 the Nikkei 225, Japan's main stock index, fell 4,451 points in a single day, a drop of 12.4% and the largest one-day points fall in its history [1]. Futures trading on the Osaka Exchange was halted for a short period when prices moved past a set limit. Markets in South Korea paused the same day. A day later, share prices in Tokyo recovered much of the loss. Sharp swings like this are what regulators call market volatility, and over the past forty years they have built tools to manage it.

The best known tool is the circuit breaker, a rule that stops trading for a fixed time when prices fall by a set amount. The idea came from the United States after 19 October 1987, a day known as "Black Monday", when the Dow Jones index lost 22.6% in one session. A government report, the Brady Commission report, recommended coordinated pauses so that buyers and sellers could catch up with the news [2]. The exchanges adopted matching rules the following year. Today the main United States market has three levels, set at falls of 7%, 13% and 20% in the S&P 500 index. The first two trigger a 15-minute halt; the third closes the market for the rest of the day. Before 2020 the mechanism had been used only once, in 1997. Then the 7% level was reached four times in a single fortnight in March 2020, during the first weeks of the pandemic [3].

Other markets use their own versions. After the "flash crash" of 6 May 2010, when United States prices fell and rebounded within minutes, regulators added limits that pause trading in a single stock if its price moves too far too fast. The European Union allowed national regulators to ban the short selling of some shares for several weeks in March 2020. Exchanges in Tokyo, Seoul, Shanghai and elsewhere in Asia set daily price limits on individual stocks, so a share can only rise or fall by a capped percentage before trading in it stops.

Not every attempt has worked. China introduced a market-wide circuit breaker in January 2016 and removed it after four days. Traders rushed to sell before the halt could take effect, which made the fall worse; on the fourth day the market closed just half an hour after opening. This points to the central argument against the tools, sometimes called the magnet effect: a pause meant to calm a market can instead pull prices toward the trigger as people race to trade first. Supporters reply that a short break gives firms time to check their systems and gives regulators time to spot errors, and that a planned stop is safer than a disorderly one. After more than thirty years the research is still mixed. Most large exchanges have kept their circuit breakers, while continuing to adjust the levels and the timing in the light of each new episode.

## 2.2. Credit Rating Agencies Oversight

### 2.2.1. Goal

Explain what a credit rating is, why the way rating agencies are paid creates a conflict of interest, and how regulators have responded since 2008.

### 2.2.2. Reading

It is easy to think of a credit rating as a fact, like a temperature reading. It is not. A credit rating is an opinion about how likely a borrower is to repay. It is produced by a private company and expressed on a letter scale, from the top grade of AAA down to grades that signal a high risk of default, meaning a failure to pay on time. Governments, companies and bundles of loans all carry ratings. The opinions matter because many large investors, by law or by their own rules, may only hold bonds above a certain grade. A downgrade can therefore force selling and raise a borrower's cost of new debt.

Three firms produce the great majority of these opinions. Standard & Poor's, Moody's and Fitch together accounted for about 94% of all outstanding ratings in the United States at the end of 2023 [1]. All three are paid by the borrower whose bond they rate. This is the conflict of interest at the centre of the industry: the agency's customer is the party that wants a high grade, and that party can take its business to a competitor. Before 2008, agencies gave top grades to large volumes of United States mortgage-backed bonds that later collapsed in value, which helped turn a housing downturn into a global crisis.

Regulators responded on both sides of the Atlantic. In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission was given clearer authority to oversee registered rating agencies, and in 2015 Standard & Poor's paid about US$1.375 billion to settle government claims over its pre-crisis mortgage ratings [2]. In the European Union, a 2009 regulation required agencies to register. Since 2011 the European Securities and Markets Authority has been their single supervisor, with the power to fine them, and several firms have paid penalties for control failures [3]. The rules also require agencies to publish their methods and their past accuracy, so that users can judge them.

Sovereign ratings, meaning ratings of national governments, show why the opinions still carry weight. When Standard & Poor's lowered the United States from AAA in 2011, and when Fitch and Moody's followed in later years, the decisions drew sharp official criticism but still moved markets. France was downgraded by more than one agency in 2025. Japan takes a slightly different path. Alongside the global firms, it has two home-grown agencies, Japan Credit Rating Agency and Rating and Investment Information. All agencies that operate in Japan must register with the Financial Services Agency under a regime in force since 2010. Oversight has not removed the basic tension in the business model. It has instead tried to make the opinions more transparent, more comparable and easier to challenge, so that investors treat a rating as one view among several rather than a guarantee.

## 2.3. Insurance Market Regulation

### 2.3.1. Goal

Explain why insurers are regulated differently from banks, what a solvency rule is meant to do, and how the main systems compare.

### 2.3.2. Reading

In April 1997 Nissan Mutual Life, a mid-sized Japanese insurer, was ordered to stop selling policies because it could not meet its future promises to policyholders. It was the first failure of a Japanese life insurer since the Second World War. Several more followed between 2000 and 2001, among them Chiyoda Life and Kyoei Life [1]. An insurer takes in premiums today and pays claims that may fall due decades later. Regulation of the industry is built around one question: will the company still be able to pay when the claim finally comes?

The tool for answering that question is a solvency rule. It requires an insurer to hold capital, meaning assets worth more than its expected future claims, so that it can absorb a bad year without failing. The European Union's system, known as Solvency II and in force since 2016, sets the required capital at the level needed to survive a loss expected only once in 200 years [2]. Japan's Financial Services Agency has used a solvency margin ratio since the late 1990s. A reading below 200% triggers official intervention, and the agency is moving to a newer, more risk-sensitive standard for the 2026 financial year [1].

The United States regulates insurance mainly at the state level rather than nationally. State regulators apply a shared formula, called risk-based capital, developed by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners. The formula sets action points where a regulator must step in as an insurer's capital falls [3]. If an insurer does fail, state guaranty associations, funded by the industry, cover policyholder claims up to set limits, such as US$300,000 for a death benefit in many states. The United Kingdom kept a version of the European rules after leaving the bloc and has since adjusted them under the name Solvency UK.

International bodies have tried to add a layer above the national systems. In 2008 the American insurer AIG came close to collapse and needed roughly US$180 billion of government support. The losses came from contracts written by one small division, and the episode showed that a large insurance group can threaten the wider financial system. In response, the International Association of Insurance Supervisors, a body whose members cover almost all of the world's insurance premiums, agreed a global Insurance Capital Standard in 2024 for the biggest cross-border groups. It is meant to give supervisors in different countries a common measure for the same group.

The common thread across all these systems is patience. Unlike a bank run, an insurer's trouble usually builds slowly, over years of underpricing risk or earning too little on its investments, and solvency rules are designed to catch that drift early. The Japanese failures around 2000 came after a long period of guaranteeing returns that the insurers' own investments could no longer earn.

## 2.4. Stress Testing and Risk Management

### 2.4.1. Goal

Explain what a bank stress test is, show one case where it worked and one risk it missed, and describe how the practice is used today.

### 2.4.2. Reading

Can a bank survive a bad year on paper before it has to survive one in real life? That is the question a stress test tries to answer. A regulator sets out a severe but plausible scenario, such as a deep recession with rising unemployment and falling house prices. Each large bank must then estimate its losses under that scenario and show how much of its capital buffer would remain. The capital buffer is the cushion of shareholders' money that absorbs losses before depositors are at risk. If the buffer falls too low, the bank must raise more capital or hold back dividends.

The practice was proven in the United States in 2009. In the depths of the financial crisis, regulators tested 19 large banks, published the results, and found that ten needed about US$75 billion of new capital between them [1]. Because the method and the numbers were made public, investors could see which banks were sound, and confidence returned faster than expected. The test became an annual fixture, and the European Union, the United Kingdom and Japan built their own versions. In the 2025 European Union exercise, 64 banks were tested against a scenario that wiped out roughly 370 basis points, or 3.7 percentage points, of their capital ratios on average [2].

The limits of the tool became clear in March 2023, when Silicon Valley Bank failed in the United States. The bank held about US$209 billion in assets, which put it below the size threshold that would have placed it in the annual stress test [3]. Its problem, large losses on supposedly safe government bonds as interest rates rose, was not the kind of shock the recent scenarios had focused on. Depositors withdrew more than US$40 billion in a single day, faster than any historical run the tests had modelled. A test only measures the dangers it is designed to measure.

Regulators have drawn two lessons. The first is to vary the scenarios, so that a test one year might centre on a property crash and the next on a sudden jump in interest rates or a technology shock. The 2023 European climate test was described by its own authors as a learning exercise rather than a pass-or-fail check. The second lesson is to treat the test as one instrument among several, alongside day-to-day supervision and the bank's own internal risk models. In Japan, the Bank of Japan and the Financial Services Agency run joint tests covering hundreds of institutions and publish the results in a twice-yearly report on the financial system [3]. The exercise is now a routine part of oversight in every major economy, valued less as a precise forecast than as a structured way to ask what could go wrong.

## 2.5. Sovereign Debt Management

### 2.5.1. Goal

Explain how a national government borrows, why it matters who holds the debt and in which currency, and what happens when a government cannot pay.

### 2.5.2. Reading

Japan's central government owes more than twice the value of everything the country produces in a year. By the International Monetary Fund's 2026 figures, Japan's general government gross debt is about 206% of national output, by far the highest of any large advanced economy [1]. Older estimates put the figure higher still, above 230%. Yet Japan has no debt crisis, low borrowing costs and no sign of panic. The reason is who lends the money. As of March 2026 the Bank of Japan alone held about 48% of all Japanese government bonds, and foreign investors held only about 8%; the rest sits with Japanese banks, insurers and pension funds [2]. The debt is almost entirely domestic and in yen, so there is no foreign creditor to lose confidence and no foreign currency to find.

Most governments borrow the same basic way. A treasury or a specialist debt management office sells bonds, which are tradable promises to pay interest and repay a fixed sum on a set date, and short-term bills of under a year. It decides how much to sell, at what mix of maturities, and by what method, usually an auction open to competing bidders. Two choices shape the risk. The first is currency: borrowing in your own money removes the danger that a falling exchange rate makes the debt harder to repay. The second is maturity: spreading repayment dates over many years reduces the risk of having to refinance a large amount at a bad moment. The United Kingdom's Debt Management Office, for example, keeps the average life of its bonds unusually long, at about 14 years [3].

The United States shows a different balance. About 31% of United States federal debt held by the public is owned by foreign investors, with Japan the largest single holder, and the government paid roughly US$282 billion in interest to those foreign holders in 2025 [4]. That reliance on outside lenders is manageable for a country whose currency the world wants to hold, but it would be dangerous for a smaller economy.

When debt genuinely cannot be paid, a government must restructure it. That means negotiating with creditors to stretch out repayment dates or reduce the amount owed, a reduction known as a haircut. Sri Lanka defaulted in 2022 and agreed a deal with bondholders in 2024 that cut the value of its bonds by roughly a quarter. Lower-income countries such as Zambia and Ghana have restructured through a shared process set up by the Group of Twenty, a body that brings together the world's largest economies. Zambia's 2024 deal wrote off about US$840 million of what it owed. These cases share a feature Japan lacks: debt owed to foreigners, often in dollars, which is the hardest kind to carry when trouble comes.
