## 4.3. Wealth Inequality and Redistribution Policies

### 4.3.1. Goal

Explain how wealth inequality is measured and which redistribution tools actually move the numbers.

### 4.3.2. Reading

The richest 10% of the world's adults own about 76% of all household wealth. The poorest half own about 2% [1]. The gap in what people own is far wider than the gap in what they earn, because wealth compounds over time and passes between generations, while a wage stops when the work does.

Inequality is usually summed up in one number, the Gini coefficient, which runs from 0 (everyone equal) to 1 (one person owns everything). In the United States, Federal Reserve data show the wealthiest 1% of households owned about 30.9% of roughly US$162 trillion in household wealth in 2024, while the bottom half owned about 2.5%, down from around 3.4% in 1989 [2]. Across the OECD, taxes and cash transfers narrow the income gap — by more in high-transfer systems such as France and the Nordic countries, and by less in the United States and South Korea.

The redistribution toolkit has four main parts: progressive income tax, which takes a larger share as income rises; transfer payments such as pensions and child benefit; taxes on capital gains and inheritance; and, more rarely, an annual tax on a person's total assets. That last tool has mostly been tried and dropped. The number of OECD countries levying an annual net wealth tax fell from 12 in 1990 to just three by 2020 — Norway, Spain and Switzerland [3]. The common reasons: the taxes raised little, were hard to value and enforce, and prompted wealthy residents to move assets abroad or leave.

Japan shows that a tax system's design matters more than its headline rates. Its income inequality is moderate by rich-country standards, but its effective tax rate actually falls for the very highest earners: income from shares or dividends is taxed at a flat 20%, while employment income is taxed progressively up to about 45%. Since the wealthiest take more of their income as investment returns, the average rate can start dropping past about JPY 100 million — the "JPY 100 million wall." The government's answer, phased in from 2025, is a minimum tax of at least 22.5% on income above JPY 330 million, tightening further from 2027 [6].

The same problem is now on the international agenda. Brazil, one of the world's most unequal large economies, used its 2024 G20 presidency to commission a blueprint from economist Gabriel Zucman for a coordinated minimum tax on the ultra-rich: at least 2% of wealth each year from individuals worth over US$1 billion. Applied to roughly 3,000 known billionaires, that would raise an estimated US$200–250 billion, against an effective rate today of about 0.3% [4]. In July 2024 G20 finance ministers issued a first joint statement on taxing the very wealthy, non-binding, with the US and Germany among the holdouts [5]. Taxing wealth is less about rates than about which kinds of income and assets are taxed lightly — and the wealthiest hold exactly those.

**New terms:** wealth (net worth); Gini coefficient; progressive / regressive tax; transfer payment; net wealth tax; effective tax rate

**Recycled terms:** capital; risk; cross-border

### 4.3.4. Reading Questions

1. Why is the gap in what people own wider than the gap in what they earn?
2. What does a Gini coefficient of 0 mean, and what does 1 mean?
3. What happened to the number of OECD countries with an annual net wealth tax between 1990 and 2020, and why?
4. Why can the average tax rate fall for Japan's highest earners?
5. What would the Zucman blueprint require of the world's billionaires, and how much would it raise?

### 4.3.5. Discussion Questions

1. Annual wealth taxes have mostly been repealed. Does that mean they are a bad idea, or that they were badly designed? Explain.
2. The reading says tax design matters more than headline rates. Do you agree? Use the Japanese example.
3. A global minimum tax on billionaires needs many countries to act together. Why is that so hard, and is it achievable?
4. Compare taxing income and taxing wealth. Which is easier for a government to do well, and why?
5. You advise a government that wants to reduce wealth inequality without driving wealthy residents abroad. Recommend two measures from the toolkit and explain why you chose them over the others.
