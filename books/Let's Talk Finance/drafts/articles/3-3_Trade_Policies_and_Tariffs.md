## 3.3. Trade Policies and Tariffs

### 3.3.1. Goal

Explain who bears the cost of a tariff and how tariffs and trade agreements pull an open economy in opposite directions.

### 3.3.2. Reading

Who actually pays a tariff? Legally the answer is clear: a tariff is a tax on imported goods, and the importer of record, the company bringing the goods across the border, pays it to customs rather than the foreign seller [1]. It is a cross-border tax, collected where goods enter the country. Economically it is less clear. The cost can be split three ways: the exporter may cut its price, the importer may accept a thinner margin, or the final buyer may pay more. Studies of the tariffs the United States raised sharply in 2025 disagree on the split. One Federal Reserve analysis found about 90% of the cost fell on US firms and consumers; another found foreign exporters absorbed nearly half through lower prices [1]. This share, called the pass-through, depends on how much pricing power the exporter has.

Those 2025 tariffs were unusually large. The average effective tariff rate rose from about 2.4% in January 2025 to a peak near 28% in the spring, then settled around 17% by the autumn, the highest level since the 1930s [2]. In February 2026 the US Supreme Court ruled that the emergency law used for many of these tariffs did not authorise them. The government replaced them the same day under a different law, and the average rate fell to about 7% by mid-2026 [3].

Countries facing these tariffs responded in two very different ways. Japan negotiated. Under a framework agreed in July 2025, most US tariffs on Japanese goods were set at 15%, down from a threatened 25%, and the tariff on Japanese cars was cut from 25% to 15%. In exchange, Japan pledged up to US$550 billion of investment in US industries such as semiconductors and energy by 2029 [4].

India did not get a deal. In August 2025 the US placed a combined 50% tariff on Indian goods, a standard rate plus a 25% penalty tied to India's purchases of discounted Russian oil. The higher tariff hit an estimated US$48 billion of India's exports, and India's government called it "unfair, unjustified and unreasonable" [5]. The same tool, a border tax on imports, was used in one case as a bargaining chip and in the other as a punishment.

Japan's exposure explains why it bargained hard. In 2025 the United States became Japan's largest export market, taking about 18.6% of its exports, with cars the single biggest category [4]. A tariff is a barrier a government can raise at will. A free trade agreement does the opposite: it is a standing promise to keep barriers low. Japan belongs to one such agreement, the CPTPP, which since 2018 has removed tariffs on about 95% of the goods traded among its member countries, well below the standard most-favoured-nation (MFN) tariff each would otherwise charge [6]. None of the 2025 rates is fixed: levels moved several times within two years, and the US-China rate rose above 100% before a truce brought it down [2]. For an economy that sends nearly a fifth of its exports to one market, that risk is itself a cost, because firms must plan around a border tax that may change again before their goods arrive.

### 3.3.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** tariff, importer of record, pass-through, most-favoured-nation (MFN) tariff, free trade agreement, effective tariff rate

**Recycled terms:** cross-border, market, risk

### 3.3.4. Reading Questions

1. Who is legally responsible for paying a tariff, and why does the reading say the economic cost is "less clear"?
2. By how much did the average US effective tariff rate change between January 2025 and the autumn of 2025?
3. What did Japan agree to give in exchange for lower US tariffs?
4. Why did the US place a combined 50% tariff on Indian goods?
5. What does the reading mean when it calls a free trade agreement "a standing promise to keep barriers low"?

### 3.3.5. Discussion Questions

1. The reading says a tariff's cost is shared between exporter, importer and buyer. Who do you think ends up paying most in practice, and why?
2. The same tariff tool was used against Japan as a bargaining chip and against India as a punishment. Is it reasonable for a country to use trade taxes this way? Explain.
3. Japan sends nearly a fifth of its exports to the US. What are the risks of depending so heavily on one market, and what could Japan do to reduce them?
4. Compare a tariff with a free trade agreement as ways of managing trade. Which serves a mid-sized exporting economy better?
5. Advise a manufacturer that exports to the US on three ways to protect its business against sudden tariff changes.
