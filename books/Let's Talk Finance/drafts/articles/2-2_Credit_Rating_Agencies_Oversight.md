## 2.2. Credit Rating Agencies Oversight

### 2.2.1. Goal

Explain what a credit rating is, why the way rating agencies are paid creates a conflict of interest, and how regulators have responded since 2008.

### 2.2.2. Reading

It is easy to think of a credit rating as a fact, like a temperature reading. It is not. A credit rating is an opinion about how likely a borrower is to repay. It is produced by a private company and expressed on a letter scale, from the top grade of AAA down to grades that signal a high risk of default, meaning a failure to pay on time. Governments, companies and bundles of loans all carry ratings. The opinions matter because many large investors, by law or by their own rules, may only hold bonds above a certain grade. A downgrade can therefore force selling and raise a borrower's cost of new debt.

Three firms produce the great majority of these opinions. Standard & Poor's, Moody's and Fitch together accounted for about 94% of all outstanding ratings in the United States at the end of 2023 [1]. All three are paid by the borrower whose bond they rate. This is the conflict of interest at the centre of the industry: the agency's customer is the party that wants a high grade, and that party can take its business to a competitor. Before 2008, agencies gave top grades to large volumes of United States mortgage-backed bonds that later collapsed in value, which helped turn a housing downturn into a global crisis.

Regulators responded on both sides of the Atlantic. In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission was given clearer authority to oversee registered rating agencies, and in 2015 Standard & Poor's paid about US$1.375 billion to settle government claims over its pre-crisis mortgage ratings [2]. In the European Union, a 2009 regulation required agencies to register. Since 2011 the European Securities and Markets Authority has been their single supervisor, with the power to fine them, and several firms have paid penalties for control failures [3]. The rules also require agencies to publish their methods and their past accuracy, so that users can judge them.

Sovereign ratings, meaning ratings of national governments, show why the opinions still carry weight. When Standard & Poor's lowered the United States from AAA in 2011, and when Fitch and Moody's followed in later years, the decisions drew sharp official criticism but still moved markets. France was downgraded by more than one agency in 2025. Japan takes a slightly different path. Alongside the global firms, it has two home-grown agencies, Japan Credit Rating Agency and Rating and Investment Information. All agencies that operate in Japan must register with the Financial Services Agency under a regime in force since 2010. Oversight has not removed the basic tension in the business model. It has instead tried to make the opinions more transparent, more comparable and easier to challenge, so that investors treat a rating as one view among several rather than a guarantee.

### 2.2.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** credit rating, credit rating agency, default, conflict of interest, sovereign rating, investment grade

**Recycled terms:** regulator, bond, financial crisis

### 2.2.4. Reading Questions

1. Why does the reading say a credit rating is an opinion rather than a fact?
2. What share of United States ratings do the three largest agencies produce, and how are those agencies paid?
3. What did rating agencies do before 2008 that made the financial crisis worse?
4. What did Standard & Poor's agree to in 2015, and why?
5. How does oversight of rating agencies in the European Union work, and who is the supervisor?

### 2.2.5. Discussion Questions

1. The borrower pays the agency that rates it. What other ways of paying for ratings can you think of, and what would go wrong with each?
2. Some investors are required to hold only highly rated bonds. Is that rule sensible, or does it give the agencies too much power? Explain.
3. Governments have criticised agencies for lowering their ratings. Should a private firm be able to judge a national government's debt? Give reasons.
4. Japan supports its own rating agencies alongside the global firms. What are the advantages and drawbacks of having national agencies?
5. You advise a regulator that wants investors to rely less on ratings. Suggest two practical steps it could take.
