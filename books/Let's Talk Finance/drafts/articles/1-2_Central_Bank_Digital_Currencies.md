## 1.2. Central Bank Digital Currencies

### 1.2.1. Goal

Explain what a central bank digital currency is, why most central banks are studying one, and why almost none have launched.

### 1.2.2. Reading

Nearly nine in ten of the world's central banks are working on a digital version of their national money. In a 2024 survey by the Bank for International Settlements, 85 of 93 central banks said they were exploring one [1]. Yet by 2026 only three small economies had actually launched one for the public, and use in all three has been very low.

A central bank digital currency, or CBDC, is official money issued in digital form by the central bank itself. Today, the cash in your pocket is a claim on the central bank, but the money in your bank account is a claim on a commercial bank. A retail CBDC would be central-bank money that the public could hold directly and use for everyday payments. A separate "wholesale" type, for settlement between financial institutions, is less controversial and further advanced.

The launches so far are small. The Bahamas issued the world's first, the Sand Dollar, in October 2020. Jamaica followed in 2022, and Nigeria launched the eNaira in 2021, the first in Africa. In each case take-up has disappointed: the International Monetary Fund found that the large majority of eNaira wallets had never been used [2].

The larger economies are still testing. China has run the biggest trial: its digital yuan had handled a cumulative 7 trillion yuan (about US$1 trillion) in transactions by mid-2024, across 17 regions [3] — though much of that use was encouraged by the government rather than chosen by shoppers, who already rely on Alipay and WeChat Pay. The European Central Bank ran a "preparation phase" from 2023 and in October 2025 moved to the next stage, but a digital euro cannot be issued unless EU lawmakers pass a law, and first issuance would not be before 2029 [4]. The Bank of Japan has run a pilot since 2023 and says no decision to issue has been made. Sweden's Riksbank studied an e-krona and concluded there was not yet enough need for one.

The common reasons given for the work are defensive: to keep a public form of money available as cash use falls, to make payments faster and cheaper, and to reduce reliance on a few large private payment firms. The common reasons for caution are that people might move money out of banks in a crisis, that a digital record raises privacy questions, and that, so far, the public has shown little demand.

### 1.2.3. Vocabulary Focus

**New terms:** central bank digital currency (CBDC), retail CBDC, wholesale CBDC, claim (on the central bank), pilot, disintermediation, legal tender, payment system

**Recycled terms:** central bank, stablecoin, crypto-asset, consumer protection

### 1.2.4. Reading Questions

1. What is the difference between the cash in your pocket and the money in your bank account, according to the reading?
2. What is a "retail" CBDC, and how does it differ from a "wholesale" one?
3. What does the reading say happened with Nigeria's eNaira after it launched?
4. Why is China's 7 trillion yuan figure described with some caution?
5. What conditions must be met before the European Central Bank can issue a digital euro?

### 1.2.5. Discussion Questions

1. The reading says central banks give "defensive" reasons for this work. Are those reasons strong enough to justify the cost and effort? Give your view.
2. Compare the risks of issuing a CBDC with the risks of not issuing one. Which set of risks is more serious?
3. If a digital yen were introduced in Japan, what would make you use it instead of cash or a payment app? What would put you off?
4. Several countries launched a CBDC and then saw very low use. What lessons should other central banks take from that?
5. Advise a central bank that is deciding between launching a CBDC now, running a longer pilot, or stopping the project. What should it weigh?
