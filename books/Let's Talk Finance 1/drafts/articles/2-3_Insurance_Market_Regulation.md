## 2.3. Insurance Market Regulation

### 2.3.1. Goal

Explain why insurers are regulated differently from banks, what a solvency rule is meant to do, and how the main systems compare.

### 2.3.2. Reading

In April 1997 Nissan Mutual Life, a mid-sized Japanese insurer, was ordered to stop selling policies because it could not meet its future promises to policyholders. It was the first failure of a Japanese life insurer since the Second World War. Several more followed between 2000 and 2001, among them Chiyoda Life and Kyoei Life [1]. An insurer takes in premiums today and pays claims that may fall due decades later. Regulation of the industry is built around one question: will the company still be able to pay when the claim finally comes?

The tool for answering that question is a solvency rule. It requires an insurer to hold capital, meaning assets worth more than its expected future claims, so that it can absorb a bad year without failing. The European Union's system, known as Solvency II and in force since 2016, sets the required capital at the level needed to survive a loss expected only once in 200 years [2]. Japan's Financial Services Agency has used a solvency margin ratio since the late 1990s. A reading below 200% triggers official intervention, and the agency is moving to a newer, more risk-sensitive standard for the 2026 financial year [1].

The United States regulates insurance mainly at the state level rather than nationally. State regulators apply a shared formula, called risk-based capital, developed by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners. The formula sets action points where a regulator must step in as an insurer's capital falls [3]. If an insurer does fail, state guaranty associations, funded by the industry, cover policyholder claims up to set limits, such as US$300,000 for a death benefit in many states[4]. The United Kingdom kept a version of the European rules after leaving the bloc and has since adjusted them under the name Solvency UK.

International bodies have tried to add a layer above the national systems. In 2008 the American insurer AIG came close to collapse and needed roughly US$180 billion of government support. The losses came from contracts written by one small division, and the episode showed that a large insurance group can threaten the wider financial system. In response, the International Association of Insurance Supervisors, a body whose members cover almost all of the world's insurance premiums, agreed a global Insurance Capital Standard in 2024 for the biggest cross-border groups[5]. It is meant to give supervisors in different countries a common measure for the same group.

The common thread across all these systems is patience. Unlike a bank run, an insurer's trouble usually builds slowly, over years of underpricing risk or earning too little on its investments, and solvency rules are designed to catch that drift early. The Japanese failures around 2000 came after a long period of guaranteeing returns that the insurers' own investments could no longer earn.

### 2.3.4. Reading Questions

1. What happened to Nissan Mutual Life in 1997, and why was it significant?
2. What is a solvency rule meant to ensure?
3. How does the European Union's Solvency II decide how much capital an insurer must hold?
4. How is insurance regulated in the United States, and what happens to policyholders if an insurer fails?
5. What did the near-collapse of AIG in 2008 show, and how did international regulators respond?

### 2.3.5. Discussion Questions

1. The reading says an insurer's trouble usually builds slowly, unlike a bank run. Why might slow trouble be harder for a regulator to act on, not easier?
2. The United States regulates insurance state by state; the European Union uses one shared system. What are the strengths and weaknesses of each approach?
3. Guaranty associations repay failed insurers' customers up to a limit. Who should pay for that protection, and should there be a limit at all?
4. Should the largest insurance groups face an extra global standard on top of national rules? Give reasons for and against.
5. You advise a regulator worried that insurers are earning too little on safe investments. Suggest two things it could watch for as warning signs.
