## 2. Email Do’s and Don’ts

Make sure your emails are free of errors by paying attention to the details.

### Tone, Style and Organization

\[\[SECTIONBREAK\]\]

##### Common Style Errors

Avoid using the following in standard emails. They are not necessary, too strong, or could cause confusion.

1.  Words in ALL CAPITALS.

2.  Bold font.

3.  Emojis.

4.  Unusual abbreviations.

5.  Color.

6.  Graphics.

7.  Fancy fonts and different-sized fonts.

##### Plain Text or HTML?

Write your e-mail in plain text without colour or fancy fonts.

1.  Plain text is quick and easy to use, and easy to read.

2.  Plain text is business-like, and all computers can read it.

3.  Don’t use HTML. HTML provides colour, highlighting, and graphics. However, none of these features are necessary in standard business email.

4.  Everything in your message is important so there is no need to highlight anything.

5.  Good simple formatting is enough to highlight the information.

##### Short Paragraphs

Make your message easy to understand.

1.  Write in short complete paragraphs with one idea and related details in each paragraph.

2.  Use lists where possible.

##### Tone and Courtesy

Tone and courtesy are important issues in all communication including e-mail. Tone is created by choice of words, sentence structure, format, and brevity. Be respectful of the person and the relationship.

1.  Simply having good format sets a respectful business-like tone to your e-mail.

2.  A conversational tone for face-to-face communication might be appropriate but the same words written in an e-mail might seem inappropriate or rude.

3.  Being too casual or too formal might seem rude to your reader.

##### Three Tones for Business Email

Tone is the level of formality in your message.

1)  **Formal**

<!-- -->

1.  For first time and occasional email.

2.  Similar to a business letter.

3.  To those of a higher rank.

4.  or making special requests, dealing with difficult situations and offering apologies.

<!-- -->

2)  **Collaborative**

<!-- -->

1.  For those with whom you exchange information regularly.

2.  Business-like, but more relaxed than formal.

<!-- -->

3)  **\[\[LINEBREAK\]\]Familiar and Frequent**

The familiar tone must be used with care as misunderstandings can happen from too much informality.

1.  Can be quite informal but still business-like.

2.  Similar tone to speaking.

3.  Salutations, appreciations and closings can be brief.

4.  Can include personal comments.

5.  Sometimes a simple one-line message only with nothing else.

6.  If in doubt use the more formal collaborative tone.

7.  It is better to be too formal than too informal.

### The Parts of an Email

\[\[SECTIONBREAK\]\]

##### Subject Line

**The subject line is very important.**

1.  The subject needs to be simple and easy to understand.

2.  The subject tells the reader about your message before opening it.

3.  A meaningful subject helps to locate e-mail in your inbox.

**Make your subject simple and easy to understand.**

1.  Begin with a key word.

2.  ‘Request for the Three Rivers Progress Report’—Avoid articles, prepositions and conjunctions. Instead, use colons (:), dashes () or slashes (/):

3.  ‘Three Rivers Progress Report (Request)’—Don’t use parentheses.

**Avoid common problems with subjects.**

1.  ‘Report’—Too short and vague. Not enough specific information.

2.  ‘Request for the Progress Report of the Three Rivers Project’—Too long. Too many unnecessary words.

##### Salutation

**The standard salutation for business e-mail is: ‘Dear Mr. Jones,’.**

1.  For quick routine messages you may begin with: ‘Mr. Jones, / Dear Bob, / Bob’

2.  For very informal messages with people you correspond with often you may begin with: ‘Hi Bob, / Hello Bob, / Good morning Bob,’

3.  If you are writing to the familiar members of a department or team you can begin with: ‘Dear All’.

4.  If you do not know the name of the receiver of the email, you can begin with: ‘Dear Sir or Madam’.

5.  If you are unfamiliar with a person’s name and you do not know how to address your message, you can use the person’s full name: ‘Dear Parvenu Armenjan’.

6.  Don’t use ‘To Whom It May Concern’. It could seem impersonal, cold and distant.

**Avoid these common errors:**

1.  ‘Dear Mr.Jones’—There is always a space between ‘Mr.’ and the name.

2.  ‘Dear Mr . Jones’—There is never a space before punctuation.

3.  ‘Dear Mr. Bob’— ‘Mr.’ is not used with just the first name.

##### Paragraph 1

**Make your message clear from the beginning.**

1.  Always introduce the reason for writing the email briefly and clearly. For example: ‘I am writing about our meeting on Friday, January 29.’

2.  The subject line is not part of your message. Do not introduce your topic by referring to the subject line. For example: ‘Regarding the subject above,’ is not correct.

##### Paragraphs 2 & 3

**Give the necessary details and deadlines in the second paragraph.**

1.  Keep your paragraphs short. Three or four short paragraphs are much easier to read than one long paragraph.

2.  State deadlines clearly and specifically with the day and date. ‘As soon as possible’ is not clear enough.

##### The Close

**The close always comes before your name. The close says goodbye to your reader.**

1.  The standard business closing is: ‘Best regards,’

2.  ‘Best Regards,’ is not correct. ‘Regards’ should not be capitalized.

##### Your Name

**For standard business email, write your full name:** Personal name and family name. For example, ‘Hiroshi Tanaka’.

1.  For people with whom you correspond regularly, you can be more informal and use just the name by which they call you. For example, ‘Hiroshi’.

2.  For a first-time email, capitalize your family name. For example, ‘Hiroshi TANAKA’. This is a courtesy to your reader who may not be familiar with Japanese names.

3.  After the first message, you do not need to capitalize your family name each time.
