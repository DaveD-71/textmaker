# Research Brief — Book A 4.5 Economic Diplomacy and International Cooperation

Prepared 2026-09-11 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts checked against at least two independent sources; where an official/IGO page blocked automated fetch, news and think-tank co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Economic diplomacy is the use of trade, investment, aid, and financial ties — including the threat of cutting them off — as tools of foreign policy. It runs along two tracks that pull against each other: coercion (sanctions, export controls, freezing assets) and cooperation (coordinated crisis lending through the IMF, debt relief for poor countries, climate finance, shared rules). The topic uses a recent large-scale case — the 2022 financial sanctions on Russia — to draw out the principle that deep financial connections are also leverage, and the trade-off that using that leverage is costly to the country applying it and pushes targets to build alternatives.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The scenario: the 2022 financial sanctions on Russia.** After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, a coalition including all G7 economies and the EU froze roughly US$300 billion of the Russian central bank's foreign-currency reserves, cut major Russian banks out of the SWIFT messaging network, and (from December 2022) imposed a US$60-a-barrel price cap on Russian seaborne oil. It was the largest coordinated use of financial sanctions ever applied to a major economy. Region: G7 / EU / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Council on Foreign Relations ("How to Use Russia's Frozen Assets") and Verfassungsblog / Courthouse News, all giving the ~US$300 billion figure and the February 2022 timing; the SWIFT and oil-price-cap elements are widely documented.

2. **The frozen-assets question.** About EUR 200 billion of the immobilised Russian reserves sit at Euroclear, a securities depository in Belgium. Rather than confiscate the principal outright — legally and financially fraught — the EU has redirected the "windfall" profits those assets earn to Ukraine (first transfers July 2024), and in 2024 the G7 agreed a roughly US$50 billion loan for Ukraine to be repaid from that future revenue stream. Region: EU / G7. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — CFR and Yahoo/Reuters explainers ("How the West uses Russia's frozen reserves to help Ukraine") and Verfassungsblog, consistent on the EUR 200 billion at Euroclear, the July 2024 first tranches and the US$50 billion G7 loan.

3. **The principle: the dollar system is leverage.** Because the US dollar dominates global reserves, trade invoicing and correspondent banking, the United States can impose "secondary sanctions" — penalising banks and firms in third countries for dealing with a sanctioned party — which multiplies the reach of any single country's measures. This structural power, administered by the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), is what makes financial statecraft effective and is also why other states seek alternatives. Region: United States / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — standard analysis across CFR, Atlantic Council and Treasury/OFAC descriptions of secondary sanctions and correspondent-banking chokepoints. (Connects to Book B 3.4's ~58% dollar reserve share — cite that topic's figure there, not here.)

4. **The cooperative track: coordinated crisis response.** The G20 was raised to leaders' level during the 2008 financial crisis specifically to coordinate a global response; in 2020 the G20 agreed the Debt Service Suspension Initiative (temporarily pausing debt payments for the poorest countries) and the "Common Framework" for restructuring the debts of low-income countries alongside the IMF and World Bank. This is economic diplomacy as collective problem-solving rather than coercion. Region: G20 / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — G20 / World Bank descriptions of the DSSI and Common Framework, widely summarised. **Ledger note:** individual Common Framework cases (Zambia, Ghana, Chad) are **primary** evidence in Book A 2.5 Sovereign Debt Management — refer to the Common Framework here only as a *mechanism*, without those country cases.

5. **Alternatives being built: China's parallel institutions.** China has promoted institutions that sit outside the US-led system: the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB), launched in 2016 and now with well over 100 approved members; the Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS), a yuan-clearing network offered as an alternative to dollar-based rails; and a web of bilateral central-bank currency-swap lines. None yet rivals the incumbent system in scale, but their growth is itself a diplomatic project. Region: China / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — AIIB membership figures (aiib.org, widely cited "100+ members") and analyses of CIPS growth after 2022 (Atlantic Council, Reuters); treat CIPS scale as "growing from a low base," not as a SWIFT rival.

6. **The cooperative track: climate finance.** Rich countries pledged at Copenhagen in 2009 to mobilise US$100 billion a year in climate finance for developing countries by 2020 — a target reportedly met only around 2022, two years late. At COP29 in Baku in November 2024, countries agreed a new goal for developed nations to take the lead in mobilising at least US$300 billion a year by 2035, within a broader aspiration of US$1.3 trillion a year from all sources; many developing countries called the US$300 billion figure inadequate. Region: global (UNFCCC). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — UN News and UNCTAD ("Countries agree $300 billion by 2035") and Carbon Brief's COP29 outcomes summary, consistent on the US$300 billion / 2035 goal, the tripling of the US$100 billion target, and the developing-country criticism.

7. **Japan: joining the coalition while protecting a core interest.** Japan joined the G7 sanctions on Russia in 2022 despite importing almost all its energy. But Tokyo decided Japanese trading houses Mitsui and Mitsubishi would keep their stakes (12.5% and 10%) in the Sakhalin-2 LNG project in Russia's Far East — which supplies roughly 9% of Japan's LNG — on national energy-security grounds, and Japan secured a G7 carve-out exempting Sakhalin from the oil-price-cap rules. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Reuters/Malay Mail ("Japan will not abandon Sakhalin-2 LNG stake, says PM Kishida," 31 March 2022) and France 24 ("Sakhalin exception: the Russian energy Japan can't quit"); the 12.5%/10% stakes, the ~9%-of-LNG figure and the G7 carve-out are consistently reported.

8. **The trade-off: costs, leakage and de-risking.** Sanctions impose costs on the countries applying them (Europe's 2022 energy-price and inflation shock), leak through third countries (Russian oil re-routed and often sold above the cap; parallel imports of restricted goods), and give targets a reason to move trade and payments out of the dollar and euro. Their effectiveness in changing a target's behaviour, versus merely constraining it, is genuinely contested among economists. Region: comparative / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — widely covered across IMF, Peterson Institute and central-bank commentary on sanctions leakage, the oil-cap's limited bite, and "de-dollarisation" incentives.

## The Japanese angle (short paragraph, per the shape plan)

Japan's handling of the 2022 Russia sanctions shows economic diplomacy as a balancing act. Japan joined the G7 measures — asset freezes, SWIFT cut-offs, the oil-price cap — even though it imports almost all of its oil and gas and had no domestic cushion. But the government drew one clear line: the trading houses Mitsui and Mitsubishi would keep their stakes in the Sakhalin-2 liquefied-natural-gas project in the Russian Far East, which supplies around 9% of Japan's LNG, and Japan obtained a G7 carve-out so Sakhalin oil and gas were exempt from the price-cap rules. Tokyo's reasoning was explicit and rooted in the 1970s oil shocks: solidarity with allies is important, but a country that cannot feed its power stations has no foreign policy at all. Japan also runs the cooperative side of economic diplomacy heavily — it is a long-standing top bilateral aid donor, hosted the G7 in 2023, and frames its overseas lending around "quality infrastructure" as an alternative to less transparent financing.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **economic diplomacy / economic statecraft** — using trade, investment, aid and finance (or their withdrawal) to pursue foreign-policy aims
- **sanctions** — government-imposed restrictions on trade or financial dealings with a country, entity or person
- **asset freeze** — blocking access to funds or property held within a jurisdiction
- **SWIFT** — the international bank-messaging network; being cut off from it isolates a bank from cross-border payments
- **secondary sanctions** — penalties on third-country firms for dealing with a sanctioned party
- **Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)** — the US Treasury body that administers US sanctions
- **price cap** — a ceiling on the price at which a good (Russian oil) may be traded using Western services such as shipping and insurance
- **export controls** — restrictions on selling specified goods or technology abroad
- **multilateral institution** — a body many governments jointly own and run (IMF, World Bank, G20, AIIB)
- **debt relief / debt restructuring** — reducing or rescheduling what a country owes, usually coordinated among creditors
- **climate finance** — funding transferred to developing countries to cut emissions and adapt to climate change
- **de-dollarisation** — efforts to reduce reliance on the US dollar in trade, reserves and payments
- **reserve currency** — a currency governments widely hold and use internationally (mainly the US dollar)
