# Research Brief — Book B 2.5 Banks: What They Do and How They Fail

Prepared August 2026. Research only — no article drafted.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

A bank takes in money that can be withdrawn at any time (deposits) and lends most of it out for
years at a time (mortgages, business loans) — turning short-term liabilities into long-term
assets, a trick called "maturity transformation". This is useful, but it makes every bank
fragile: it can never repay all depositors at once, so if enough of them ask for their money
together — a bank run — even a fundamentally sound bank can collapse. Governments try to break
that logic with deposit insurance (a promise that small savers will be repaid) and a central bank
willing to lend cash against good collateral (the "lender of last resort"); the topic looks at
how these worked, or did not, in the US, Switzerland, the UK and Japan.

  NCB) and today's DICJ with its ¥10 million limit.
- (Optional 5th) **cross-border framing** — Diamond-Dybvig / 2022 Nobel as the theory that ties
  all four together.

Natural structure: what a bank does (deposits in, long loans out) -> why that is fragile
(Diamond-Dybvig, the run) -> the two safety nets (deposit insurance; lender of last resort) ->
four real cases showing the nets working (Northern Rock, SVB depositors made whole) or a bank
being rescued whole (Credit Suisse) or a system overwhelmed (Japan 1997-98).

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan's safety net is the Deposit Insurance Corporation of Japan (DICJ, established 1971), which
guarantees general deposits up to ¥10 million of principal plus interest per person per bank, and
covers ordinary payment/settlement accounts in full. That limited-coverage rule is itself a
product of crisis: after the bubble burst, Japan ran a blanket guarantee of all deposits from
the mid-1990s and only returned to a capped system in the early 2000s. The crisis it was
responding to peaked in November 1997, when Hokkaido Takushoku Bank (a nationwide "city bank")
announced it would stop operating on 17 November and the big-four brokerage Yamaichi Securities
decided to wind up on 24 November; in 1998 the government nationalised Long-Term Credit Bank of
Japan (October) and Nippon Credit Bank (December) and, under a February 1998 law, began injecting
large amounts of public money to recapitalise the surviving banks — a slow, expensive clean-up
that is still the reference point for "what happens when a banking system, not just one bank,
gets into trouble." (Sources: DICJ "Introduction" page; Nippon.com, "Lessons of the 1997
Financial Crisis in Japan.")

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **deposit** — money placed in a bank that the customer can normally take out at any time
- **loan** — money a bank lends out, to be repaid with interest over time
- **maturity transformation** — funding long-term loans with short-term (on-demand) deposits
- **liquidity** — how easily an asset can be turned into cash without loss; a bank is "liquid" if
  it can meet withdrawals as they come
- **bank run** — many depositors trying to withdraw at the same time because they fear the bank
  will fail
- **deposit insurance** — a government-backed guarantee that depositors will be repaid up to a
  set limit if their bank fails
- **lender of last resort** — the central bank lending cash to a solvent but illiquid bank
  against good collateral, to stop a panic
- **insolvency vs illiquidity** — insolvent = the bank's assets are worth less than what it owes;
  illiquid = it has the assets but cannot turn them into cash fast enough
- **capital** — the buffer of shareholders' money that absorbs losses before depositors are hit
- **uninsured deposits** — money above the insurance limit; the funds most likely to flee in a run
- **systemic risk** — the danger that one bank's failure spreads through the whole financial system
- **wholesale funding** — money a bank borrows from other financial institutions rather than from
  retail depositors (Northern Rock's weak point)

## Notes / cautions

- **SVB's asset size and the size/speed of the run are precise numbers — take them from the
  Fed's 28 April 2023 review or FDIC documents, not from memory.** The Fed Executive Summary
  says "$212 billion in total assets when it failed" and "over $211 billion" for its 2021 peak;
  the FDIC and CRS use "$209 billion" (year-end 2022). The 9 March outflow is stated by the Fed
  as "over $40 billion", with "$42 billion" of withdrawal *requests* that day appearing in FDIC
  and congressional material. Pick one figure, attribute it, and don't blend them.
- **"Second-largest US bank failure" was true for SVB in March 2023 but not for long.** First
  Republic (~US$229bn) overtook it on 1 May 2023, making the current ranking: Washington Mutual
  (2008) > First Republic (2023) > SVB (2023) > Signature (2023). Phrase it as "the
  second-largest at the time" or add the First Republic update.
- **The FDIC US$250,000 and DICJ ¥10 million limits are current as of 2026 — confirmed.** FDIC:
  $250,000 per depositor/bank/ownership category, unchanged since 2008 (page last updated
  1 April 2024). DICJ: ¥10 million principal + interest for general deposits, settlement
  deposits fully covered. Both were verified for this brief (DICJ via search summaries of the
  live dic.go.jp page + a bank explainer, as dic.go.jp blocks automated fetch).
- **Japan's 1990s events are ~28-30 years old — date them clearly as historical context**, not
  as current events. The precise days (Hokkaido Takushoku 17 Nov 1997, Yamaichi 24 Nov 1997,
  LTCB Oct 1998, NCB Dec 1998) are well documented but the Nippon.com timeline gives months, not
  days — cross-check a second source if a specific date is printed.
- **Credit Suisse was a rescue, not a payout.** No depositor lost money and no deposit-insurance
  claim was triggered; the losers were shareholders and AT1 bondholders (~CHF 16bn wiped). It is
  a good contrast with SVB/Northern Rock: a bank "too big to fail" is merged away, a mid-size
  bank's depositors are made whole by the insurer, and — in Japan 1997-98 — a whole system needs
  public recapitalisation. FINMA's own release does not state the CHF 3bn price; that figure
  comes from the deal announcement and news coverage.
- **nobelprize.org, dic.go.jp and bankofengland.co.uk returned HTTP 403 to our research
  tooling** but are all reachable in a normal browser and are the correct primary citations. The
  quoted wording above came from search-engine summaries that reproduce those pages; the writer
  should open each in a browser and confirm the exact sentence before quoting.
- **Diamond-Dybvig is a model, not an event** — cite it as the standard theoretical framing
  (and flag the 2022 Nobel), not as a factual anchor with a number.
