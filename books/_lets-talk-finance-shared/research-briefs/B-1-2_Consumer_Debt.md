## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

This topic explains how consumer credit works: borrowing now and repaying later, with **interest** as the price of the loan, and **compounding** making unpaid balances grow faster over time. It distinguishes **secured** debt (a **mortgage** or car loan, backed by an asset the lender can take) from **unsecured** debt (credit cards, personal loans, buy-now-pay-later), and the common idea of "good" debt (low-rate, building an asset) versus "bad" debt (high-rate, funding consumption). It also covers **over-indebtedness** — when repayments outrun income — and the rapid rise of **buy-now-pay-later (BNPL)** and how regulators in several countries are now responding.

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan has historically carried lower household leverage than the US or UK, and Japanese cardholders overwhelmingly use **single-payment (lump-sum) credit** — paying the full balance the following month with no interest — rather than **revolving credit**; revolving balances are a small, though growing, share of the ~JPY 23 trillion in outstanding card credit (Japan Consumer Credit Association, data to 2025). The defining policy episode is the **2006 reform of the Money Lending Business Act** (fully in force 18 June 2010): after a January 2006 Supreme Court ruling against "grey zone" interest, the maximum rate on unsecured consumer loans was cut to **20%**, and a borrower's total borrowing from non-bank lenders was capped at **one-third of annual income**. This ended the 20–29.2% "grey zone" in which most of the roughly JPY 20 trillion non-bank consumer-finance industry had operated and is credited with sharply reducing Japan's "multiple-debtor" problem (people servicing loans from five or more lenders — about 2.3 million such borrowers around 2007). Verifiable facts + urls: FRBSF Asia Focus, February 2007 — https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/AsiaFocus-Feb07.pdf ; JCA credit-card statistics — https://www.j-credit.or.jp/en/sp/statistics/index.html ; FSA update on money-lending-business statistics (as of March 2025), 28 November 2025 — https://www.fsa.go.jp/en/news/2025/20251128-2/20251128-2.html (English page links the Japanese-language statistical tables for the multiple-debtor count).

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

1. **consumer credit** — borrowing by households to spend now and repay later (cards, personal loans, store finance), usually not for a house
2. **interest rate** — the price of borrowing, charged as a percentage of the amount owed, usually per year
3. **annual percentage rate (APR)** — the yearly cost of a loan including interest and set fees, so different offers can be compared
4. **compounding** — interest charged on unpaid interest as well as on the original amount, so a balance left unpaid grows faster and faster
5. **principal** — the original sum borrowed, separate from the interest added to it
6. **secured loan** — a loan backed by an asset (house, car) the lender can seize if you do not repay; usually a lower rate
7. **unsecured loan** — a loan with no asset behind it (most credit cards, personal loans, BNPL); usually a higher rate
8. **mortgage** — a long-term secured loan used to buy property, repaid over many years
9. **revolving credit** — a balance you can carry from month to month (a credit card), paying interest on what is left unpaid — contrast with a fixed instalment loan
10. **over-indebtedness** — owing more than you can realistically repay from your income, so debts pile up
11. **buy-now-pay-later (BNPL)** — splitting a purchase into a few interest-free instalments, offered at the checkout; often lightly regulated until recently
12. **default / delinquency** — falling behind on required payments; "delinquent" debt is a standard measure of financial stress
13. **"good" vs "bad" debt** _(informal)_ — a rule of thumb: low-rate borrowing that builds an asset or income (a mortgage, a student loan) versus high-rate borrowing for everyday consumption

## Notes / cautions

- **Interest rates and debt totals move every quarter — date every figure and name the specific report.** The US household-debt total is the NY Fed's Q2 2026 report (11 Aug 2026); the card APR is the June 2026 G.19 (7 Aug 2026); the mortgage rate is the Freddie Mac survey week of 27 Aug 2026. Do not blend vintages.
- **The CFPB BNPL interpretive rule was issued in May 2024 and withdrawn in 2025.** Present it as a policy that was proposed and then reversed — a genuine illustration of contested regulation — not as current US law. The UK FCA regime (from 15 July 2026) is the live, going-forward example.
- **"Household debt" is measured several ways** — as a dollar/yen total, as a share of GDP (BIS), or as a share of disposable income (OECD). The ratios differ a lot: on the OECD disposable-income basis Australia is ~223% and Norway ~236%, whereas on the BIS GDP basis Korea (~88.6%) tops the major economies. State which basis any comparison uses.
- **Several primary sites block automated access (HTTP 403): the NY Fed newsroom, Bank of England, FRED, the RBA, the OECD data portal and S&P.** Every figure taken from those is flagged above and backed by the institution's own PDF or by an established outlet (ABA Banking Journal, CNBC, Reuters-class wires). A writer with a browser should open the primary page and cite it directly; the numbers themselves are not in doubt.
- **Japan revolving-vs-lump-sum framing:** it is well documented that lump-sum (interest-free, pay-next-month) is the dominant Japanese card-repayment method and revolving is a minority, but a single clean official percentage is hard to pin down; the JCA statistics page gives outstanding card-credit totals (~JPY 23tn in 2025) rather than a lump-sum/revolving split. Phrase as "most" / "a small but growing share," not a precise ratio, unless the JCA detailed tables are consulted.
- **The "2.3 million multiple debtors around 2007" figure** for Japan appears in FSA statements and press coverage of the era; if used, attribute it to the FSA and note it refers specifically to borrowers with loans outstanding from five or more money-lending firms. The post-reform decline is well established qualitatively; for a precise "fell to X by year Y" a writer must open the FSA's Japanese-language money-lending-business statistics linked from https://www.fsa.go.jp/en/news/2025/20251128-2/20251128-2.html
- **UK "GBP 2.0 trillion total household debt"** is a round figure assembled from the BoE mortgage (~GBP 1.75tn) and consumer-credit (~GBP 253bn) series; cite the two component series, or the UK Parliament briefing that aggregates them, rather than presenting GBP 2.0tn as a single official number.
- **BNPL global market-size figures vary wildly** between research firms (from ~USD 20bn "market value" to ~USD 560bn "payment volume" for 2025) and none is a primary/official source — avoid a global BNPL dollar figure; use the country-level regulator numbers (UK GBP 13bn; US CFPB transaction data) instead.
