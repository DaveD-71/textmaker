# Research Brief — Book B 4.4 Philanthropy, Foundations and Impact

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Charity is direct help given now; philanthropy in the modern sense is something more structured — large sums, usually held in an endowed foundation that gives away only its investment returns, backed by tax advantages, and increasingly judged on measurable "impact." That structure raises real questions: money can sit in a foundation or a donor-advised fund for decades before reaching a cause; a handful of very rich donors can shape public priorities in health, education or science with little accountability; and critics argue tax-subsidised giving is no substitute for a fair tax system. The topic sets out how big philanthropy works and the responses to those criticisms — faster payout, unrestricted giving, spend-down foundations, impact measurement, and impact investing.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The misconception, corrected (definitional anchor).** A private foundation is a legal entity holding an endowment; in a typical year it distributes only a small slice of its assets and keeps the rest invested, so it can in principle exist forever. This is what separates institutional philanthropy from charity: it is endowed, perpetual by default, tax-privileged, and staffed to pursue a strategy — not simply "giving money away." Region: general / US model. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — standard definitions across the Philanthropy Roundtable, Council on Foundations and IRS descriptions of private foundations and the payout rule.

2. **The US 5% payout rule — and the donor-advised-fund gap.** US private foundations must distribute at least about 5% of their assets each year to keep their tax exemption. Donor-advised funds (DAFs) — accounts that give the donor an immediate tax deduction but let them recommend grants later — have **no** required payout timeline, and now hold very large sums; critics say money can be parked there indefinitely, and that foundations increasingly meet their 5% obligation by granting to DAFs. (DAF sponsors report donors voluntarily granting around 25% of assets a year on average.) Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — The Regulatory Review ("Reforming Donor-Advised Funds") and Baker Institute / Tax Notes analyses, consistent on the ~5% foundation floor, the absence of a DAF payout requirement, the "foundation → DAF" criticism, and the ~25% voluntary DAF grant rate; Biden budgets FY2023-25 proposed tightening this.

3. **The largest commitment ever: the Gates Foundation's spend-down.** In May 2025, on its 25th anniversary, the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation announced it would spend roughly US$200 billion over the following 20 years — about double the ~US$100 billion it gave in its first 25 years — and then close permanently by the end of 2045, rather than existing in perpetuity. It was described as the largest philanthropic commitment in modern history, and is the clearest example of the "spend-down" response to the perpetual-endowment critique. Region: United States / global health. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Gates Foundation press release ("Gates Foundation Will Double Spending Over Next 20 Years") and STAT / Inside Higher Ed / Philanthropy News Digest coverage, consistent on the ~US$200 billion figure, the 2045 sunset and the "double the first 25 years" framing.

3a. *(context)* Warren Buffett's pledged gifts have funded a large share of Gates Foundation grantmaking — a reminder that a few individuals drive a large share of US mega-philanthropy.

4. **A different model: MacKenzie Scott.** Since 2019, MacKenzie Scott (who received her wealth in a divorce settlement with Amazon's founder) has given away more than US$19 billion to over 2,000 organisations, in large **unrestricted** gifts — no application process, no strings, minimal reporting, recipients chosen by quiet research and told after the decision. A three-year study by the Center for Effective Philanthropy (2025) found the gifts were "transformational" and that feared downsides (organisations overspending or losing other donors) had not materialised. Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Center for Effective Philanthropy research release and Fortune / CBS News coverage, consistent on the >US$19 billion total, 2,000+ organisations, ~US$5 million median gift and the unrestricted, no-application approach.

5. **The critique: "big philanthropy" and accountability.** Political theorists and commentators (notably Rob Reich's *Just Giving*) argue that large-scale philanthropy is subsidised by all taxpayers through deductions, is accountable to no electorate, lets very wealthy individuals set public agendas in areas such as education and public health, and can crowd out or substitute for government provision that would be more democratic. This is the "problem" the topic's responses are answering. Region: general (US/UK debate). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — widely summarised across reviews of Reich's *Just Giving* and Anand Giridharadas's *Winners Take All*, and in Stanford Social Innovation Review coverage of the accountability debate.

6. **The "impact" turn: measurement and impact investing.** Modern philanthropy increasingly demands evidence of results — cost-per-outcome analysis (as popularised by GiveWell and the effective-altruism movement) and formal impact metrics. Alongside grantmaking, "impact investing" — deploying capital for a measurable social or environmental return as well as a financial one — had grown to an estimated US$1.571 trillion in assets under management by 2024 (Global Impact Investing Network), up at about 21% a year since 2019. Region: global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — GIIN "Sizing the Impact Investing Market 2024" (thegiin.org) as reported by Impact Investor and TodayESG, consistent on the ~US$1.57 trillion figure and the 21% growth rate.

7. **The UK model: regulated charity plus giant endowments.** The UK requires charities to demonstrate "public benefit" and regulates them through the Charity Commission; giving is encouraged through Gift Aid (the state adds basic-rate tax back to donations). It is also home to one of the world's largest foundations, the Wellcome Trust, whose roughly GBP 37.6 billion endowment funds scientific and health research and which has committed to spend about GBP 16 billion over the decade to 2032. Region: United Kingdom. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Wellcome Trust endowment figures via Alliance magazine and Wikipedia (GBP ~37.6 billion, fourth-wealthiest foundation) and standard descriptions of the Charity Commission and Gift Aid.

8. **Japan: a smaller philanthropic sector.** Individual giving in Japan was about 0.23% of GDP in 2020 (under US$20 billion) — roughly seven times lower as a share of the economy than in the United States (about 1.55% of GDP). A 2008 reform — the first overhaul of Japan's "public-interest corporation" law in more than a century — replaced wide ministerial discretion over which bodies could be recognised as public-interest with clear statutory criteria, and tax deductions for giving were expanded in the 2000s. Corporate philanthropy has a longer tradition, embodied in the Keidanren business federation's "1% Club" (companies aiming to give around 1% of profits). Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Indiana University Lilly School's 2022 Global Philanthropy Environment Index (Japan) and Alliance magazine ("Japanese philanthropy has fallen behind"), consistent on the 0.23%-vs-1.55%-of-GDP comparison and the December 2008 public-interest-corporation reform.

## The Japanese angle (short paragraph, per the shape plan)

Japan's philanthropic sector is small relative to the size of its economy. Individual giving was around 0.23% of GDP in 2020 — about a seventh of the US level as a share of output — and large private foundations of the Gates or Wellcome type are rare. Several reasons are usually given: weaker tax incentives until recent reforms, a cultural expectation that the state and companies, rather than individuals, handle social provision, and, until 2008, a legal system in which government ministries had wide discretion over which bodies could even be recognised as serving the public interest. That 2008 reform — the first rewrite of the relevant law in over a century — set clear statutory criteria for public-interest status, and tax deductions for giving were widened in the 2000s. Corporate philanthropy is the stronger tradition, formalised in the Keidanren business federation's "1% Club" of companies that aim to give roughly 1% of profits. Recent large personal pledges by founders of companies such as Fast Retailing and SoftBank suggest the picture may be changing, but from a low base.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **philanthropy** — organised private giving of money, time or assets for public benefit, usually at scale
- **charity** — direct assistance to those in need (a narrower, more immediate idea than philanthropy)
- **foundation** — a legal body holding an endowment and making grants from its returns
- **endowment** — an invested fund whose income (not usually its capital) is spent
- **payout rule** — the minimum share of assets a US private foundation must distribute each year (about 5%)
- **donor-advised fund (DAF)** — a charitable account giving an immediate tax deduction while grants are recommended later
- **spend-down (sunset) foundation** — one that commits to giving away all its assets and closing by a set date
- **unrestricted (general operating) grant** — money a recipient can spend as it judges best, rather than for a specified project
- **impact measurement** — assessing the social or environmental results of a grant or investment
- **impact investing** — investing for a measurable social/environmental return alongside a financial one
- **effective altruism** — a movement urging donors to fund the most cost-effective ways of doing good
- **Gift Aid** — a UK scheme adding reclaimed tax to eligible donations
- **public benefit** — the legal test a UK charity must meet
