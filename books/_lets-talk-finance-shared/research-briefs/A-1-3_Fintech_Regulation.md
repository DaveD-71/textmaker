# Research Brief — Book A 1.3 Financial Technology (Fintech) Regulation

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Fintech regulation is how governments and financial regulators supervise technology-driven financial services: digital payments and e-money, app-based "neobanks" or challenger banks, open banking (rules that let customers share their bank data with licensed third parties), online lending platforms, and the licences these firms must hold. A common tool is the "regulatory sandbox," a scheme that lets a firm test a new product with real customers for a limited time under relaxed or tailored rules and close supervision. Approaches differ sharply by country — the UK and EU built broad statutory frameworks, the US relies on a state-by-state patchwork plus stalled federal rules, and Asian regulators (Singapore, India, Japan) have each taken distinct routes — so a firm that is licensed in one market usually cannot operate in another without a fresh authorisation.

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan's Financial Services Agency (FSA) regulates fintech through a light-touch, disclosure-and-cooperation model rather than a single fintech statute. A 2016 amendment to the Payment Services Act brought crypto-asset exchanges under registration. The 2017 amendment to the Banking Act (in force 1 June 2018) created a registration regime for "electronic payment service providers" — payment-initiation and account-information/aggregation firms — and required banks to publish open-API connection policies and to conclude contracts with registered third parties, effectively an open-banking framework built on bilateral bank–fintech agreements instead of one mandated standard. Separately, the 2020 amendment to the Payment Services Act (bill passed 5 June 2020, in force 1 May 2021) split non-bank "funds-transfer service providers" into three tiers by transfer size: Type I for transfers above ¥1 million (requires FSA authorisation, and the previous per-transfer cap was removed), Type II for transfers up to ¥1 million, and Type III for small transfers up to ¥50,000 (both registration only). Verifiable facts: the 2017 Banking Act open-API amendment and electronic-payment-service-provider registration — FSA primary source https://www.fsa.go.jp/en/news/2018/20180717.html [VERIFIED OPENED]; the 2020 three-tier funds-transfer structure — Payment Services Act translation https://www.japaneselawtranslation.go.jp/en/laws/view/3965/en [VERIFIED OPENED via search; see also Morrison Foerster note https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/200715-reforms-japanese-regulations.html]. Context anchor: Japan's cashless ratio hit 42.8% in 2024, meeting the government's 40%-by-2025 goal early (METI).

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **fintech** — financial technology; companies using software and data to deliver financial services (payments, lending, banking) outside traditional banks.
- **regulatory sandbox** — a supervised scheme letting a firm test a new financial product with real customers for a limited time under relaxed or tailored rules.
- **open banking** — rules requiring banks to let customers share their account data, and allow payment initiation, through licensed third-party providers (usually via APIs).
- **application programming interface (API)** — a standard software connection that lets one system securely request data or actions from another; the technical backbone of open banking.
- **e-money (electronic money)** — monetary value stored electronically (e.g. a prepaid app balance) that is issued on receipt of funds and accepted as payment by parties other than the issuer.
- **neobank / challenger bank** — a bank that operates mainly through a smartphone app with no or few branches (e.g. Monzo, Starling); "challenger" also covers newer licensed banks competing with incumbents.
- **licensing / authorisation** — official permission from a regulator that a firm must obtain before offering a regulated financial service.
- **payment service provider (PSP)** — a regulated firm that enables payments — processing card transactions, initiating bank transfers, or issuing e-money.
- **money transmitter** — US term for a licensed business that transfers funds or issues payment instruments; licensed state by state.
- **strong customer authentication (SCA)** — an EU requirement that electronic payments be verified with at least two independent factors (e.g. password plus phone).
- **consumer protection** — regulatory safeguards for users: clear disclosure of costs, fair treatment, complaint channels, and liability rules for fraud or loss.
- **funds-transfer service provider** — Japanese licence category for non-bank firms that move money for customers, tiered by transfer amount.

## Notes / cautions

- **PSD3 / PSR is not yet law.** As of August 2026 it is a provisional political agreement (27 Nov 2025); publication in the Official Journal is expected H1 2026 and entry into force in 2027, with application (via the Regulation) later still. Write it as "agreed and expected to replace PSD2," not "in force." Source: European Parliament press release, 27 Nov 2025.
- **CFPB Section 1033 (US open banking) is in limbo.** Finalised Oct 2024, then enjoined by a Kentucky federal court; the CFPB reopened the rulemaking (ANPR 22 Aug 2025) and has said it views the rule as unlawful. It did NOT take effect on 1 April 2026. Do not state that the US "has" an open-banking rule. Some 2026 commentary notes individual US states (e.g. via state privacy/data laws) moving on data sharing in the federal vacuum.
- **Japan cashless figure — methodology change.** METI's 31 March 2025 release put 2024 at 42.8%. A later METI release (31 March 2026, https://www.meti.go.jp/english/press/2026/0331_005.html) reportedly puts 2025 at 58.0% after METI broadened the calculation following a December 2025 review, and set new targets (65% by 2030, 80% long-term). If the reading needs the latest number, use the 2025/58% figure but attribute it to the revised methodology; the cleaner, more stable anchor is "42.8% in 2024, meeting the 40%-by-2025 goal early." Both METI English pages returned HTTP 403 to the automated fetch tool — figures here come from METI's own search-result extracts and the CCI France Japon summary; a human should open the METI pages directly to confirm before print.
