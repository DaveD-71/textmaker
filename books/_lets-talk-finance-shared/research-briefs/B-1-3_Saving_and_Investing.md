# Research Brief — Book B 1.3 Saving and Investing for the Long Term

_Prepared August 2026. Research only — not a draft. Every anchor below was opened; status marked per item._

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

This topic distinguishes **saving** (holding money safely, usually in cash or deposits, for short-term needs and emergencies) from **investing** (putting money into assets such as shares or funds that can rise or fall in value but are expected to grow more over long periods). It explains **compounding** (returns earning further returns over time), why a long **time horizon** lets an investor ride out short-term **volatility**, and why **diversification** — spreading money across many holdings — reduces risk. It also compares low-cost **index funds** with **actively managed funds**, and surveys **tax-advantaged accounts** that governments use to encourage long-term investing (Japan's NISA, the UK ISA, the US 401(k)/IRA, Australian superannuation).

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japanese households have long held around half their financial assets in cash and deposits — far more than in the US or Europe. The Bank of Japan's Flow of Funds shows **currency and deposits at 51.0% of household financial assets as of end-March 2025** (versus 11.5% in the US and 31.8% in the euro area), and **47.2% of a record ¥2,386 trillion as of end-March 2026** (currency and deposits ¥1,126 trillion). The government's decades-old **"from savings to investment"** slogan was given new force by the **new NISA launched in January 2024**, which sharply raised the tax-free limits (¥3.6m a year, ¥18m lifetime) and made the tax exemption permanent. Take-up has been rapid: **NISA accounts reached 26.96 million by end-June 2025**, against a government target of 34 million by end-2027, and purchases by investors in their 20s and 30s have multiplied roughly 14-17 times since 2014. [Sources: https://www.boj.or.jp/en/statistics/sj/sjhiq.pdf ; https://www.boj.or.jp/en/statistics/sj/sjexp.pdf ; https://www.fsa.go.jp/policy/nisa2/about/index.html ; https://www.fsa.go.jp/policy/nisa/20250924.html ]

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

1. **saving vs investing** — holding money safely for short-term needs vs committing it to growth assets for the long term
2. **compounding** — earning returns on past returns as well as on the original sum
3. **return** — the gain (or loss) on an investment, usually expressed as a yearly percentage
4. **real vs nominal return** — return after inflation vs before inflation
5. **diversification** — spreading money across many assets so no single loss is severe
6. **index fund** — a fund that simply tracks a market index, at low cost
7. **actively managed fund** — a fund whose manager picks holdings to try to beat the market, at higher cost
8. **time horizon** — how long before the money is needed
9. **volatility** — how much an investment's value swings up and down
10. **tax-advantaged account** — a government-approved account (NISA, ISA, 401(k), super) where investment gains are tax-free or tax-deferred
11. **equity premium** _(optional)_ — the extra long-run return investors get from shares over cash or bonds

## Notes / cautions

- **Real vs nominal, and the period, must always be stated.** The 5.2% figure is *real* (after inflation), *global*, *annualized*, *1900-2024*. Nominal figures are much higher; single-country figures differ (US higher, Japan lower over the 20th century). Do not present any long-run figure without its source, its period, and the real/nominal label.
- **A long-run average is not a forecast.** The same UBS source shows the 2000-2024 real global equity return was only 3.5%. Good phrasing: "over the last 125 years… on average," not "you will get 5% a year."
- **Contribution limits change most years.** UK ISA £20,000 (2026-27), US 401(k) $24,500 / IRA $7,500 (2026), Japan NISA ¥3.6m/¥18m (since 2024), Australia super 12% (from 1 July 2025). Any article should date every limit.
- **SPIVA caveats:** the scorecard measures *funds*, counts funds that closed/merged as underperformers (survivorship adjustment), and is net of fees. Some academics dispute the methodology (Cremers et al.), so attribute the number to SPIVA rather than stating it as settled fact. Numbers vary by category (small-cap active funds did unusually well in 2024-H1 2025) and by country — SPIVA publishes separate Europe, Australia, Japan, etc. scorecards.
- **S&P's own domain blocks automated fetches (HTTP 403).** The SPIVA PDF used here is served from S&P's CloudFront CDN and is the identical document; if a writer needs the canonical link, cite the SPIVA hub page (https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/research-insights/spiva/) and note the mid-year 2025 edition.
- **FSA NISA graph PDF** opens but its text layer did not extract cleanly; the account-count and target figures are taken verbatim from FSA's own web page headings. For the cumulative-purchase yen figure, open the FSA Excel attachment linked from https://www.fsa.go.jp/policy/nisa/20250924.html before using it.
- **BOJ international comparison** is refreshed once a year (each June/August) and lags ~15 months; the end-March 2025 snapshot is the current one as of August 2026. The quarterly "basic figures" (sjexp.pdf) are more current (end-March 2026) but only cover Japan.
- Japanese household cash/deposit share dipped **below 50% for the first time in ~18 years** during 2025 on some quarterly readings (≈48.5-49.1%) — a nice narrative point, but note it fluctuates quarter to quarter and the annual BOJ comparison still shows 51.0%. State the exact date of whichever figure is used.
