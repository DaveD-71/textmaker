# Research Brief — Book A 3.3 Trade Policies and Tariffs

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

A tariff is a tax a government charges on goods crossing its border, collected from the importer, not the foreign seller directly — but the economic cost of that tax gets split between the foreign exporter (who may cut its price), the importing company, and the final consumer (who may pay more), and how that split works out in practice is the real "who pays" question. The article should show tariffs as a live, currently-used policy tool — a wave of new US tariffs since 2025 imposed on some countries and negotiated down with others — and set that against trade agreements, which do the opposite: countries agree in advance to remove tariffs between themselves. Japan sits on both sides of this: an exporter affected by other countries' tariffs, and a member of a large negotiated trade agreement of its own.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **Who legally pays, and where research says the cost actually lands.** Tariffs are legally paid by the importer of record at the border — the company bringing goods in pays US Customs and Border Protection when the shipment clears. But economically the cost can be shared three ways: absorbed by the foreign exporter (a lower price), absorbed by the importer (a smaller margin), or passed to the final consumer (a higher price). Estimates of the actual split for the 2025 US tariffs vary by study: a Federal Reserve Bank of New York analysis found roughly 90% of the burden fell on US firms and consumers; a separate 2026 study by trade economist Caroline Freund found foreign exporters absorbed about 47% of the cost through lower export prices, with US importers paying the remaining 53% — and that absorption was uneven, with larger/dominant suppliers absorbing more than smaller ones. 2. **The scale of the 2025-26 US tariff increase.** The US average effective tariff rate (tariff revenue as a share of import value) was about 2.4% in early January 2025, rose sharply through the year, peaked at roughly 28% after the April 2025 "reciprocal tariff" announcements, and settled at about 17-18% by September-October 2025 — the highest average US tariff level since the 1930s. Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Yale Budget Lab, "State of U.S. Tariffs" (rolling series; figures from the September 4 2025 and October 17 2025 editions), https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/state-us-tariffs-october-17-2025 ; Penn Wharton Budget Model, "Effective Tariff Rates and Revenues" (updated editions through 2026), https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/p/2026-02-20-supreme-court-tariff-ruling/ — both independent university-based tariff trackers report the same broad path and "highest since the 1930s" framing.

3. **A major 2026 legal reversal.** On 20 February 2026 the US Supreme Court ruled 6-3, in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not give the president authority to impose tariffs — striking down the "reciprocal" tariffs on most trading partners and the separate IEEPA tariffs tied to China, Canada and Mexico. The same day, the administration imposed a new 10% tariff under a different law (Trade Act of 1974, Section 122) for 150 days on nearly all imports, and kept other tariffs (for example, sector-specific Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminium and autos) that do not depend on IEEPA. The average effective US tariff rate fell from about 17% in late 2025 to about 7.1% by June 2026 as a result. Region: United States / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Congressional Research Service, "Supreme Court Rules Against Tariffs Imposed Under IEEPA," LSB11398, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11398 ; WilmerHale, "Supreme Court Strikes Down IEEPA Tariffs—What Now?" 4. **Japan-US negotiated framework (the "deal" side of the comparison).** In July 2025 the United States and Japan agreed a framework under which most US tariffs on Japanese goods were set at 15% — lower than the 25% the US had initially threatened — including a cut in the US tariff on Japanese cars and auto parts from 25% to 15%, which took effect 16 September 2025. In exchange, Japan committed to invest up to US$550 billion in strategic US sectors (semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, shipbuilding, energy, AI, and others), to be deployed as loans, loan guarantees and other financing (partly through Japan Bank for International Cooperation, JBIC) before January 2029, alongside purchases of US aircraft and defence equipment. Region: Japan / United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Congressional Research Service, "U.S. Tariffs and the 2025 U.S.-Japan Framework Agreement," IN12608, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12608 ; KPMG, "U.S.-Japan agreement on trade and investment," https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2025/07/tnf-united-states-japan-agreement-on-trade-investment.html (corroborated by contemporaneous news coverage, e.g. Gulf News, of the 15% rate and the $550 billion figure).

5. **EU-US negotiated framework (a second "deal" case).** On 21 August 2025 the EU and US published a joint framework setting a 15% US tariff on most EU goods, including cars, pharmaceuticals and semiconductors — cutting the US car tariff on EU-made vehicles from 27.5% to 15% — while the EU cut its own tariff on US-made cars from 10% to 0% and pledged to remove tariffs on US industrial goods and give US farm and seafood exports preferential access. Region: European Union / United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — European Commission, "Joint Statement on a United States-European Union framework on an agreement on reciprocal, fair and balanced trade" (21 Aug 2025), https://policy.trade.ec.europa.eu/news/joint-statement-united-states-european-union-framework-agreement-reciprocal-fair-and-balanced-trade-2025-08-21_en ; Congressional Research Service, "U.S.-EU Tariffs and Trade Framework Agreement," IF13107, https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF13107 (corroborated by CBC and Supply Chain Dive reporting the same 15% figure and auto-tariff terms).

6. **India — an imposed, not negotiated, tariff (the "imposed" side of the comparison).** In 2025 the US raised tariffs on Indian goods to a combined 50% — a 25% "reciprocal" tariff plus a further 25% penalty announced 6 August 2025 and effective 27 August 2025 — explicitly linked to India's continued purchases of discounted Russian oil. India's total goods exports to the US were about US$87 billion in 2024; the Indian government estimated roughly US$48 billion of exports would be affected by the higher tariff. India's government publicly objected, calling the tariffs "unfair, unjustified and unreasonable" and noting other purchasers of Russian oil were not penalised the same way. Region: India / South Asia. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Al Jazeera, "US raises India tariffs to 50 percent over Russian oil purchases" (27 Aug 2025), https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/8/27/us-imposes-50-percent-tariff-on-india-over-russian-oil-purchases ; EY Global, "US imposes additional tariffs on India for buying oil from Russia," https://www.ey.com/en_gl/technical/tax-alerts/us-imposes-additional-tariffs-on-india-for-buying-oil-from-russia (corroborated by Deccan Herald and India's own newsonair.gov.in state broadcaster coverage of the same 25%+25%=50% structure and dates).

7. **Vietnam — a negotiated deal built around a specific anti-evasion tool.** On 2 July 2025 the US and Vietnam agreed a framework setting a 20% US tariff on Vietnamese-origin goods, but a much higher 40% tariff on goods "transshipped" through Vietnam — that is, goods actually made elsewhere (aimed chiefly at China) and merely routed through Vietnam to dodge higher tariffs on their true origin. In exchange Vietnam agreed to give US goods zero-tariff access to its own market. Region: Vietnam / Southeast Asia. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Office of the US Trade Representative, "Fact Sheet: The United States and Viet Nam Reach a Framework for an Agreement on Reciprocal, Fair, and Balanced Trade," https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/fact-sheets/2025/october/fact-sheet-united-states-and-viet-nam-reach-framework-agreement-reciprocal-fair-and-balanced-trade ; CNBC, "Trump says U.S. struck trade deal with Vietnam that imposes 20% tariff on its imports" (2 Jul 2025), https://www.cnbc.com/2025/07/02/trump-trade-vietnam-deal.html (corroborated by Al Jazeera's same-day report of the 20%/40% split).

8. **China — the largest and most-layered tariff relationship.** US tariffs on Chinese goods stack several separate measures: the standard MFN rate, Section 301 tariffs on listed products (roughly 7.5-25%), and (until the February 2026 Supreme Court ruling) IEEPA-based tariffs tied to fentanyl-precursor chemicals and the broader "reciprocal" tariff. The reciprocal-tariff component alone briefly reached 145% in 2025 before a US-China truce (agreed in stages through late 2025, extended into 2026) brought it down sharply; by mid-2026, trackers put a substantial share of Chinese imports facing a combined rate of at least 17.5% once China-specific layers are added to the MFN base. 9. **CPTPP — a genuine multilateral trade agreement Japan belongs to.** The Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) was signed by 11 countries in March 2018 and entered into force on 30 December 2018 for its first six ratifiers, including Japan, alongside Australia, Canada, Mexico, New Zealand and Singapore. The agreement eliminates tariffs on the large majority of tariff lines traded among members — cited variously as about 95% of tariff lines immediately and a path toward roughly 99% over time. 10. **Japan's export exposure.** In 2025 the United States became Japan's single largest export destination for the first time in recent years, overtaking mainland China: about 18.6% of Japan's total exports (roughly US$137 billion) went to the US, against about 17% to China. Automobiles are Japan's largest single export category to the US — 28.3% of Japan's total exports to the US in 2024 — with Japan's US-bound car exports alone valued at about US$34.37 billion in 2025; Japanese automobile exports to the US fell noticeably (down roughly 22.7% year-on-year in the April-September 2025 period) after the new US auto tariffs took hold, before the rate cut to 15% under the July 2025 framework. ## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

Japan is an unusually clear example of how tariffs and trade agreements pull in opposite directions on the same economy. In 2025 the United States became Japan's largest export market for the first time in years, taking in about 18.6% of Japan's total exports (roughly US$137 billion), with automobiles — Japan's single biggest export category to the US, worth about US$34.37 billion in 2025 — especially exposed: US-bound car exports fell sharply in mid-2025 once new US tariffs took effect. That exposure is why Japan negotiated hard for its July 2025 framework agreement with the US, which set most tariffs on Japanese goods at 15% (down from a threatened 25%) and cut the US car tariff from 25% to 15%, in return for a Japanese pledge to channel up to US$550 billion into US strategic industries by January 2029. At the same time, Japan is a founding member of the CPTPP, a genuine multilateral free trade agreement that entered into force for Japan on 30 December 2018 and removes tariffs on the large majority of goods traded among its members — the reverse of a tariff: an advance agreement to charge little or nothing at the border. Read together, the two sit on either side of this topic's core question: a tariff is a government's unilateral (or negotiated-down) tax on trade, while a trade agreement like the CPTPP is a standing promise not to tax it at all.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **tariff** — a tax a government charges on goods entering the country
- **import duty** — a near-synonym for tariff, often used in customs contexts
- **trade barrier** — any government policy that makes it harder or costlier to trade (tariffs are one kind)
- **non-tariff barrier** — a trade restriction that is not a tax (a quota, a licensing rule, a technical standard)
- **pass-through** — how much of a tariff's cost an exporter or importer passes on to the next buyer, rather than absorbing itself
- **importer of record** — the company legally responsible for paying a tariff when goods enter a country
- **most-favoured-nation (MFN) tariff** — the standard tariff a WTO member charges other WTO members unless a trade agreement sets a lower rate
- **free trade agreement (FTA)** — an agreement between countries to remove or reduce tariffs between themselves
- **trade deficit / trade surplus** — when a country's imports exceed its exports (deficit) or its exports exceed its imports (surplus)
- **retaliatory tariff** — a tariff one country imposes in response to another country's tariff or trade action
- **transshipment** — routing goods through a third country before final export, sometimes used to disguise their true origin and avoid a tariff
- **effective tariff rate** — the actual average tariff collected across all imports, as opposed to the rate printed in a tariff schedule
- **sectoral tariff** — a tariff applied to one specific industry or product category (for example, steel or automobiles) rather than broadly
- **tariff exemption / carve-out** — a specific product, company, or country excluded from a tariff that otherwise applies broadly
