## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Why stock market indexes rise and fall day to day and year to year: company earnings and other fundamentals, central-bank interest-rate decisions, investor sentiment (fear and confidence), and — increasingly — how much of an index's movement comes from just a handful of giant ("mega-cap") companies rather than the market broadly. This is the *mechanics* article (how prices actually move); it deliberately stays away from the regulator's toolbox (circuit breakers, trading halts, short-selling bans), which is Book A 2.1 Regulatory Responses to Market Volatility.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **US index concentration — record level.** By the end of 2025, the 10 largest companies in the S&P 500 accounted for a record ~40.7% of the index's total weight, versus roughly 18–23% for most of 1990–2015 — more than double in a decade. The same top 10 held only about 32% of the index's *earnings*, a valuation gap driven by AI-related mega-cap stocks. [VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS: RBC Wealth Management "The Great Narrowing: S&P 500 concentration"; Pensions & Investments "Top 10 stocks account for nearly 40% of the S&P 500"; also corroborated by Visual Capitalist and a Firstrade Learning Center piece using the same underlying data]

2. **"Magnificent Seven" mega-cap weighting.** The seven largest US technology-linked stocks (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla) — nicknamed the "Magnificent Seven" — reached roughly a third of the entire S&P 500's market value during 2025 (reported as high as ~34.5% in August 2025), up from ~28.6% in 2023, before easing slightly later in the year. Their combined market capitalisation roughly doubled from about US$10 trillion to about US$20 trillion over two years. [VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS: Visual Capitalist "Charted: Magnificent 7 Market Cap as a Share of the S&P 500"; Voronoi "Magnificent Seven Climb to Record 34% Share of S&P 500"] — note: the exact percentage moves quarter to quarter; treat as "about a third" rather than a fixed number, and re-check close to drafting date.

3. **Earnings, not valuation, drove 2025's US gains.** The S&P 500's 2025 total return of about 17.9% came mostly from higher company earnings (EPS) rather than investors paying more per dollar of profit: roughly 13.5 percentage points of the gain came from earnings growth and only about 2.5 points from price-to-earnings (valuation) expansion — a reversal from 2023–24, when valuation expansion did more of the work. This is the cleanest available illustration of "fundamentals move markets." [VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS: FactSet "S&P 500 CY 2025 Earnings Preview"; Carson Group "S&P 500 Returns in 2025 Have Been a Story of Profitability"; RBC Wealth Management "U.S. equity returns in 2025"]

4. **A single company's earnings can move the whole index.** Because Nvidia alone represents roughly 8% of the S&P 500's total weight, options markets have repeatedly priced in that its quarterly earnings report could move the *entire* S&P 500 index by about 1% in a single day — illustrating how concentrated an index's fate can be in a few companies' quarterly results. [VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS: Yahoo Finance "Why Nvidia Earnings May Trigger Massive S&P 500 Volatility"; Investing.com/Reuters "Nvidia shares set for $280 billion price swing after earnings, options show" — note both trace to the same Reuters wire figures, so treat the exact dollar/percentage swing as a single-source snapshot in time; the *mechanism* (large single-stock weighting → index-level swings) is well established, the specific number is not]

5. **The VIX — the market's "fear gauge."** The Cboe Volatility Index (VIX), maintained with S&P Dow Jones Indices, measures how much volatility options traders expect in the S&P 500 over the next 30 days. It tends to fall when markets are calm and confident and to spike when investors rush to buy protection during a sell-off — the standard shorthand for "investor sentiment" in US markets. [VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS: S&P Dow Jones Indices "VIX | S&P Dow Jones Indices"; Chase "How Investors Can Use the VIX... To Better Manage Their Portfolio"] — kept general/definitional here; the specific Aug 2024 VIX-65 spike is already used as primary evidence in A 2.1, so it is not repeated as evidence in this topic (see Notes).

6. **Europe — a rate decision moving the market in real time.** On 18 December 2025 the European Central Bank held its deposit rate at 2%, its fourth consecutive pause in a cutting cycle that began in June 2024. The pan-European Stoxx 600 rose about 1% that same session and closed out 2025 with a roughly 17% annual gain — a clean same-day example of a rate decision (in this case, a decision to do nothing) moving a regional index. [VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS: European Central Bank official press release, 18 Dec 2025 ("Monetary policy decisions"); CNBC "European markets on Dec. 18: Stoxx 600, FTSE, DAX, CAC"; also corroborated by Morningstar "European Central Bank Holds Interest Rates in Last Meeting of 2025"]

7. **Emerging markets — capital flows and rate sensitivity.** The MSCI Emerging Markets Index rose roughly 25–30%+ over 2025 (reported at 25.1% year-to-date by one snapshot and higher, around 30%, by a later one in the year), well ahead of the S&P 500's ~12.8% and the MSCI World Index's ~15.4% over the same stretch. Analysts tied the outperformance to a more dovish US Federal Reserve, a softer US dollar, and strong demand for Asian technology shares — a textbook case of how a *US* rate decision changes flows into markets thousands of kilometres away. [VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS: VanEck "Turning Tides: EM Equities Are Surging in 2025"; J.P. Morgan "After the Fed cut: Why investors should go global"] — flag: the exact percentage depends on the measurement date (different snapshots across the year gave 25.1%, ~30%, and 34.36% year-end); use one clearly dated figure rather than averaging them.

8. **Japan — a rate hike that helped, not hurt, stocks.** On 16 June 2026 the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate by 25 basis points to 1% (from 0.75%) — the highest level since 1995. Rather than falling, the Nikkei 225 broke through ¥70,000 for the first time that same day, touching an intraday high of 70,020.68 and closing at a record 69,404.50, up more than 80% over the previous 12 months. The rally was led by AI- and semiconductor-linked stocks (Kioxia Holdings, Advantest) and helped along by easing Middle East tensions; commentators framed it as the rate rise removing uncertainty rather than squeezing exporters, the opposite of the standard "higher rates hurt stocks" expectation. [VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS: The Japan Times "Nikkei 225 breaks 70,000 on hopes for peace and after rate increase"; Nippon.com "Nikkei Average Briefly Tops 70,000 for 1st Time"; also corroborated by CNBC's 16 June 2026 BOJ report and TradingKey]

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

For a Japan-based reader, interest rates are the most immediate way "what moves markets" shows up at home. On 16 June 2026 the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1% — the highest since 1995 — and the Nikkei 225 did not fall on the news; it broke through ¥70,000 for the first time, closing at a record 69,404.50, up more than 80% over the previous year. The rally was carried by AI- and chip-related stocks such as Kioxia Holdings and Advantest, and commentators read the rate rise as removing uncertainty rather than squeezing exporters — a useful complication for a reader who might assume "higher rates always hurt stocks." This sits alongside the more familiar mechanism from recent years: a stronger yen (which a BOJ rate rise typically produces) tends to compress the yen value of profits that Japan's big exporters earn overseas, so the exporter-versus-domestic split inside the Nikkei 225 is itself one of the clearest local illustrations of "what moves markets" — fundamentals (earnings translated at a different exchange rate) and a monetary-policy decision acting on the same stocks at once. Note for drafting: Nikkei 225's 5 August 2024 one-day plunge (−12.4%, the worst day since 1987, triggered by a BOJ hike unwinding the yen carry trade) is already used as *primary* evidence in Book A 2.1 Market Volatility — do not re-use it as this topic's evidence; a light one-sentence callback ("markets have moved sharply on BOJ decisions before") is fine if useful for continuity, but the June 2026 ¥70,000 event above should carry the actual teaching point here.
