## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

In 2020 the COVID-19 pandemic caused the sharpest short-term contraction in the world economy since the Great Depression, as lockdowns shut down trade, travel and daily commerce almost everywhere at once. Governments and central banks answered with an unprecedented combination of fiscal stimulus (direct spending, loans, subsidies) and monetary easing (near-zero interest rates, large-scale bond buying) to keep households and firms afloat. The recovery that followed was fast in some places and slow in others — a pattern economists call a "K-shaped" or divergent recovery — and by the mid-2020s the story has shifted from "how fast did we bounce back" to "who was left permanently behind" and what lasting lessons this episode holds for handling the next shock.

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

Japan's post-COVID story is one of comparative caution, on both the health and the economic sides, and the two are connected. While the United States reopened its borders to vaccinated travelers in November 2021 and the European Union did so even earlier, in May 2021, Japan kept tight entry restrictions in place until 11 October 2022 — nearly a year longer than the US and about a year and a half longer than the EU — before dropping its daily arrivals cap and restoring ordinary visa-free travel. That later reopening lines up with a later economic recovery: Japan's real GDP did not climb back above its pre-pandemic (2019) peak until the April-June quarter of 2023, well behind the US recovery. Japan's inflation path was also markedly calmer. When the US and the euro area saw consumer prices spike to 9.1% and 10.6% respectively in 2022, Japan's core inflation peaked at 4.2% in January 2023 — a genuine shock for an economy that had spent roughly a quarter-century fighting deflation and near-zero price growth, but still under half the peak seen in Washington or Frankfurt. For a country whose households had grown used to stable, even falling, prices, even this milder increase felt significant; it was, notably, Japan's fastest pace of core inflation since 1981. The overall picture is a slower, gentler cycle rather than a sharper, faster one: Japan's fiscal year 2020 GDP fall of 4.6% sat between the sharper falls in Europe and the somewhat smaller US decline, and its subsequent recovery — in growth, prices and reopening alike — unfolded over a longer timeline than in the US or the EU.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **fiscal stimulus** — extra government spending or tax cuts meant to support the economy in a downturn
- **monetary easing** — a central bank cutting interest rates or buying assets to make borrowing cheaper
- **quantitative easing (QE)** — a central bank creating money to buy bonds on a large scale, to hold down borrowing costs
- **GDP contraction** — a fall in the total value of goods and services a country produces
- **economic rebound / recovery** — the return of output, jobs and spending to normal after a downturn
- **scarring effect** — lasting damage to an economy (lost jobs, closed businesses, weaker growth) that a downturn leaves behind even after recovery begins
- **supply-chain disruption** — a breakdown in the flow of parts, goods or materials between producers and buyers
- **pent-up demand** — spending that consumers delayed during a downturn and then release once conditions improve
- **K-shaped recovery** — an uneven recovery in which some parts of the economy or society bounce back strongly while others keep falling
- **output gap** — the difference between what an economy is actually producing and what it could produce at full capacity
- **per capita income** — average income per person, used to compare living standards across countries or over time
- **stimulus package** — a bundled set of government measures (spending, loans, tax relief) designed to boost the economy
- **interest rate cut / rate hike** — a central bank lowering or raising the cost of borrowing
- **inflation shock** — a sudden, sharp rise in prices that was not widely expected
- **divergence** (economic divergence) — countries or groups moving further apart in economic outcomes rather than converging
