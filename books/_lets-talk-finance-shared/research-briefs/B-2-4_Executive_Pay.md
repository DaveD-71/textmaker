# Research Brief — Book B 2.4 Executive Pay and Inequality Inside Firms

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)
This topic looks at how much the people at the top of a company are paid compared with a typical employee of the same firm, and how that top pay is built (base salary, an annual bonus, and stock-based awards that pay out over years). It also covers the rules and votes that have grown up around the question: mandatory disclosure of the CEO-to-median-worker pay ratio (US, UK), the shareholder "say on pay" vote (advisory in the US, binding in the UK and much of Europe), and Japan's rule that any director paid 100 million yen or more must be named individually. Running through all of it is the argument about whether very high executive pay is earned by performance or is simply what executives can extract when boards set their pay.

From 1978 to 2024, top CEO pay rose 1,094% against a 26% rise for the typical worker. Depending on method the 2024 ratio ranges from 169-to-1 to 380-to-1. Region: US. 3. **The US SEC pay-ratio disclosure rule: adopted 5 August 2015 (3-2 vote), first reported in 2018 for fiscal years beginning on or after 1 January 2017.** Item 402(u) of Regulation S-K requires each public company to disclose (a) the median annual total compensation of all employees except the CEO, (b) the CEO's annual total compensation, and (c) the ratio of (b) to (a). Smaller reporting companies, emerging growth companies and foreign private issuers are exempt; companies may use statistical sampling and reasonable estimates. Region: US. Source: Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance, "SEC Adopts Pay Ratio Disclosure Rules," 6 Aug 2015, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2015/08/06/sec-adopts-pay-ratio-disclosure-rules/ [VERIFIED OPENED].
   - SEC primary (cite; not machine-openable here): SEC press release 2015-160, https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2015-160 ; final rule Release 33-9877, https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2015/33-9877.pdf

4. **The UK requires quoted companies with more than 250 UK employees to publish CEO pay ratios — CEO total pay against the 25th, 50th (median) and 75th percentile UK employee.** Introduced by the Companies (Miscellaneous Reporting) Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/860), reg. 17, amending Schedule 8 of the directors' remuneration report rules; effective for financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2019, so first ratios appeared in 2020 reports. Companies must also show a 10-year trend and explain their chosen calculation method. Region: UK. Sources:
   - legislation.gov.uk, SI 2018/860 reg. 17, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2018/860/regulation/17/made [VERIFIED OPENED] — confirms the three ratios (X/Y25, X/Y50, X/Y75) and the "more than 250 UK employees" test.
   - Mercer, "UK's Executive Pay Ratio Disclosure Rules Take Effect," https://www.mercer.com/insights/law-and-policy/uk-executive-pay-ratio-disclosure-rules-take-effect/ [VERIFIED OPENED] — confirms effective date (accounting periods from 1 Jan 2019), scope, first reporting year 2020.

5. **"Say on pay": the UK vote on directors' remuneration policy is binding and must be held at least every three years; the US vote is advisory.** UK: Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013, ss. 79/439A and Companies Act 2006 ss. 226B-226E, in force 1 October 2013 — a quoted company may not pay a director outside the shareholder-approved remuneration policy, and payments made in breach "have no effect" and are held on trust; shareholders also get an annual advisory vote on the implementation report. US: Dodd-Frank Act s. 951; SEC rules adopted 25 January 2011 (Rule 14a-21), first votes at annual meetings on or after 21 January 2011, at least once every three years, non-binding. Region: UK + US. Sources:

6. **Notable pay revolt: Netflix shareholders rejected the executive pay package at the 2 June 2023 AGM (advisory vote).** A majority voted against roughly $166 million in named-executive compensation — co-CEOs Ted Sarandos (~$40m target) and Greg Peters (~$34m target) plus chairman Reed Hastings; the 2022 package had drawn only about 27% support. The board can override an advisory "against" vote. The Writers Guild of America, then on strike, had urged a no vote. Region: US. In November 2025 Tesla shareholders approved a new plan potentially worth about $1 trillion over a decade if milestones are met. Region: US. 8. **Switzerland — the "Minder initiative" (Federal Popular Initiative "against rip-off salaries"): approved by referendum on 3 March 2013 with 67.9% support.** It amended the constitution to require an annual binding shareholder vote on the total compensation of the board and senior management of listed companies and banned sign-on bonuses ("golden hellos"), severance ("golden parachutes") and advance payments; implemented via the Ordinance against Excessive Compensation, in force 1 January 2014. Region: Switzerland. Source: Wikipedia, "2013 Swiss executive pay initiative," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Swiss_executive_pay_initiative [VERIFIED OPENED] — corroborated by Library of Congress Global Legal Monitor, https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2013-03-27/switzerland-referendum-on-executive-pay/ (title/date confirmed via search; loc.gov returns 403 to automated fetch).

9. **EU — Shareholder Rights Directive II (Directive (EU) 2017/828), transposition deadline 10 June 2019, gave shareholders in all EU listed companies a say on pay.** Shareholders vote on the remuneration policy at least every four years (member states choose binding or advisory) and get an annual advisory vote on the remuneration report. France goes further (Sapin 2 law, from 2016): both the ex-ante policy vote and the ex-post pay vote are binding, and variable/exceptional pay is withheld if shareholders reject it. Region: EU / France. Source: AMF (French markets regulator), "French and European 'say on pay' regimes," 3 May 2017, https://www.amf-france.org/en/news-publications/news/french-and-european-say-pay-regimes [VERIFIED OPENED].

10. **Japan — 1,345 executives at 606 listed companies were paid 100 million yen or more in fiscal 2025 (year to March 2025), a sixth straight annual record; the median executive-to-employee pay ratio across all listed firms was 9.7 times.** The individual-disclosure rule (name any director paid >=100 million yen in the annual securities report) took effect for fiscal years ending on or after March 2010; fewer than about 300 executives crossed the threshold in FY2009. Highest paid in FY2025: Joseph DePinto (Seven & i Holdings / 7-Eleven) ¥13.4bn; Rene Haas (SoftBank Group) ¥6.1bn; Stacy J. Smith (Kioxia) ¥4.4bn; highest-paid Japanese national Hiroki Totoki (Sony) ¥2.8bn. Toyota chairman Akio Toyoda's pay was about 210 times the company's average employee salary including stock. Region: Japan. ## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)
Japanese executive pay sits far below US levels relative to ordinary workers: Tokyo Shoko Research puts the median executive-to-employee pay ratio across all listed companies at about 9.7 times, and even a high-profile case such as Toyota's chairman is around 210 times average pay including stock — against roughly 280-310 times for the average large US CEO. Since fiscal years ending March 2010, any director paid 100 million yen or more must be named individually in the company's annual securities report; fewer than about 300 executives crossed that line in 2009, but a record 1,345 did so at 606 companies in fiscal 2025, the sixth consecutive record. The rise is driven mainly by the spread of performance-linked and stock-based pay, and by foreign executives at Japanese firms, who took the top three spots in fiscal 2025 (Joseph DePinto at Seven & i on ¥13.4bn, Rene Haas at SoftBank, Stacy Smith at Kioxia) — echoing the earlier era when Nissan's Carlos Ghosn was Japan's most conspicuous high earner. Sources: Nippon.com / Tokyo Shoko Research, https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h02867/foreign-executives-prominent-in-japan%E2%80%99s-top-earners-list.html and https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h02049/

## Notes / cautions
- CEO-pay-ratio numbers vary widely by method: which firms are in the sample (EPI uses the top 350 by revenue; AFL-CIO uses the whole S&P 500), how equity is valued (granted value vs realised/exercised value), and which worker is the benchmark (EPI/AFL-CIO use an economy-wide typical worker; the SEC rule uses each company's own median employee). Always attribute a figure to a specific source and year: EPI 281-to-1 for 2024; AFL-CIO 312-to-1 (mean) / 198-to-1 (median) for 2025. The EPI report itself says the 2024 ratio ranges from 169-to-1 to 380-to-1 depending on method.
- Do not conflate the AFL-CIO's Musk-inclusive figures (a ~5,387-to-1 S&P 500 average, or Tesla's ~2.5 million-to-1) with the normal range — those are explicitly flagged as outliers driven by one package.
- The Musk/Tesla legal story has changed direction more than once: voided by Delaware Chancery Jan 2024, rejected again Dec 2024 despite a June 2024 shareholder re-vote, then restored by the Delaware Supreme Court in Dec 2025. State the position "as of the court's December 2025 ruling."
- Japan's low ratios are well documented but the exact multiple depends on the study and on whether stock-based pay is counted: Tokyo Shoko Research's cross-listed-market median is ~9.7x; older academic work cited figures around 10-70x depending on firm size and year. Use "single digits to low double digits for most firms" and cite the specific study.
- EPI (labour-aligned think tank) and AFL-CIO (trade union federation) both have a point of view; they are widely cited and methodologically transparent, but for balance the SEC rule text, legislation.gov.uk, and the AMF are cleaner regulator primaries.
