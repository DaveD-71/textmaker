# Research Brief — Book A 4.4 Corporate Governance and Accountability

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Corporate governance is the system of rules and checks meant to make sure the people running a company act in the interests of its owners and, increasingly, other stakeholders — not just themselves. Shareholders do not run a company; they elect a board of directors that is supposed to hire, monitor, pay and if necessary fire the management on their behalf, and governance rules try to make that chain of accountability actually work. The topic follows how those rules developed — mostly in response to scandals — from voluntary codes in the UK, to hard law in the US, to a decade of reform in Japan aimed at making boards more independent and companies more focused on shareholder returns.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10, in rough chronological order for the timeline shape)

1. **The first modern governance code: the UK's Cadbury Report, 1992.** After a run of UK corporate failures and frauds in the 1980s and early 1990s (BCCI, Polly Peck, the Maxwell pension scandal), a committee chaired by Sir Adrian Cadbury produced a code of good practice — separating the roles of chair and chief executive, using independent non-executive directors, and setting up audit committees. Its lasting innovation was "comply or explain": companies need not follow every provision, but must publicly explain any they do not. Region: United Kingdom. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Cambridge Judge Business School's Cadbury Archive and ICAEW's Cadbury Report page, corroborated by Wikipedia and ACCA's "Thirty years of corporate governance"; all credit Cadbury with the world's first comply-or-explain code and cite the BCCI/Polly Peck/Maxwell background.

2. **Hard law after Enron: the US Sarbanes-Oxley Act, 2002.** Following the collapses of Enron (December 2001) and WorldCom (2002), the US Congress passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, which — unlike the UK's voluntary approach — imposed statutory duties: chief executives and finance chiefs must personally certify the accounts, companies must document and test internal financial controls (Section 404), and a new Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) regulates the auditors. Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — standard account across SEC/PCAOB material and corporate-governance histories (ACCA, IoD); the Enron/WorldCom trigger and the CEO-certification / Section 404 / PCAOB elements are uncontested.

3. **An emerging-market case: Satyam, India, 2009.** Ramalinga Raju, founder and chairman of the Indian IT firm Satyam Computer Services, confessed in January 2009 to having inflated the company's accounts for years, including roughly US$1 billion of cash that did not exist — "India's Enron." The scandal fed directly into India's Companies Act 2013, which mandated independent directors (at least one-third of the board for listed companies), audit-firm rotation, and stronger board committees. Region: India. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — multiple Satyam case studies (SRCC, 5paisa, Kotak) and Indian public-administration summaries, all linking the 2009 confession to the Companies Act 2013's governance provisions.

4. **Japan's reform decade begins: the 2014 Stewardship Code and 2015 Corporate Governance Code.** As part of the "Abenomics" growth strategy, Japan introduced a Stewardship Code for institutional investors in 2014 and, in 2015, its first Corporate Governance Code for listed companies (revised 2018 and 2021), both on a comply-or-explain basis. The codes pushed companies to appoint independent outside directors, improve disclosure, and reduce cross-shareholdings (shares held between business partners mainly to cement relationships rather than for return). Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Japan Exchange Group "Corporate Governance Code" page (jpx.co.jp) and investor analyses (Man Group, Janus Henderson, Nikko/BNY), all dating the Stewardship Code to 2014 and the Governance Code to 2015 with 2018/2021 revisions.

5. **Governance can still fail on every level: Wirecard, Germany, 2020.** Wirecard, a payments company once seen as Germany's fintech champion and a member of the DAX blue-chip index, filed for insolvency on 25 June 2020 after admitting that about EUR 1.9 billion of cash on its balance sheet probably never existed. Its auditor, EY, had signed off the accounts without qualification for a decade; the regulator, BaFin, had focused on investigating the journalists and short-sellers who raised the alarm. "The Enron of Germany." Region: Germany / EU. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — CNBC ("'The Enron of Germany': Wirecard scandal casts a shadow on corporate governance"), Euronews and Oxford Law Blogs; the EUR 1.9 billion figure, the 25 June 2020 insolvency, EY's decade of clean audits and BaFin's misdirected scrutiny are all consistently reported.

6. **Shareholders acting through the board: Engine No. 1 vs ExxonMobil, 2021.** A tiny activist fund, Engine No. 1, holding about 0.02% of ExxonMobil, won three of twelve board seats at the company's May-June 2021 annual meeting by persuading large institutional shareholders — including BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street and major US public pension funds — to back its candidates. It was the first board election to turn on environmental and climate strategy, and a concrete demonstration that the shareholder-elects-the-board mechanism can actually change a company's direction. Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — CNBC ("Engine No. 1 wins at least 2 Exxon board seats"; "claims third Exxon board seat") and Skadden / NPR analysis; the 0.02% stake, the three seats, and the index-fund and pension-fund backing are all reported consistently.

7. **Japan pushes on returns: the TSE's 2023 "cost of capital" campaign.** In March 2023 the Tokyo Stock Exchange asked listed companies — targeting the roughly half of its top-tier "Prime" market that traded below book value (a price-to-book ratio under 1, meaning the market valued the company at less than its net assets) — to disclose concrete plans to improve capital efficiency and share price. It is widely credited as one trigger for the strong 2023-24 rally in Japanese equities. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance ("Tokyo Stock Exchange Initiative on Cost of Capital and Stock Price Conscious Management") and Dalton Investments / Janus Henderson notes; the March 2023 date, the P/B-below-1 target and the disclosure requirement are consistent across all.

8. **The two regulatory models (structural anchor).** Most of the world — the UK, the EU, Japan, and many others — governs listed companies mainly through codes on a "comply or explain" basis, backed by disclosure and shareholder pressure. The United States relies more on statute (Sarbanes-Oxley, Dodd-Frank), stock-exchange listing rules, and shareholder litigation. Neither model prevents every scandal; the topic can compare their strengths without declaring a winner. Region: comparative. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — standard distinction across governance literature (ICAEW, IoD, OECD Principles of Corporate Governance).

## The Japanese angle (can carry real weight, per the shape plan)

Japan has run one of the most sustained corporate-governance reform programmes of any large economy, and it is a clean arc to follow. Before 2014, many listed Japanese companies had few or no independent directors, held large blocks of each other's shares to cement business ties rather than to earn a return, and prioritised stability and stakeholder relationships over shareholder value. A Stewardship Code for investors in 2014 and Japan's first Corporate Governance Code in 2015 — both "comply or explain," both part of the Abenomics growth agenda — pushed companies to add outside directors, disclose more, and unwind cross-shareholdings. The codes were revised in 2018 and 2021 to raise the bar further. Then, in March 2023, the Tokyo Stock Exchange took the unusual step of publicly pressing the roughly half of its Prime-market companies that traded below the value of their own net assets to publish plans to improve capital efficiency and share price. The reforms are widely credited with helping drive the 2023-24 rise in Japanese share prices, though critics note that disclosure of a plan is not the same as delivering results, and that board independence on paper does not guarantee genuine challenge in the room.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **corporate governance** — the system of rules and checks by which a company is directed and controlled
- **board of directors** — the group elected by shareholders to oversee management on their behalf
- **executive vs non-executive (independent) director** — a director who also runs part of the business vs one brought in from outside to provide independent oversight
- **"comply or explain"** — a rule that lets a company depart from a governance code provided it publicly explains why
- **audit committee** — a board sub-committee that oversees financial reporting and the external auditor
- **external auditor** — the independent accounting firm that checks and signs off a company's accounts
- **agency problem** — the risk that managers (agents) act in their own interest rather than the owners' (principals')
- **shareholder activism** — investors using their voting rights or public pressure to change how a company is run
- **institutional investor** — a large shareholder such as a pension fund or asset manager (whose votes decide most contests)
- **cross-shareholding** — companies holding each other's shares mainly to cement business ties (a longstanding Japanese practice being unwound)
- **stewardship code** — a code of conduct for institutional investors on how they should engage with companies they own
- **price-to-book ratio (P/B)** — a company's market value divided by the net asset value on its balance sheet; below 1 means the market values it at less than its net assets
- **cost of capital** — the return a company must earn to justify the money invested in it
