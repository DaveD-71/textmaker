## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Credit rating agencies (CRAs) publish opinions — letter grades such as AAA or B — on how likely a borrower is to repay a bond, whether the borrower is a company, a bank, a structured product, or a national government. Almost all of that business runs on an "issuer-pays" model: the borrower selling the bond pays the agency to rate it, which creates a conflict of interest because the agency's customer benefits from a higher grade. After CRAs gave top ratings to US mortgage-backed securities that then collapsed in 2007-2008, the United States, the European Union, Japan and others moved from light or informal oversight to formal registration and supervision regimes, and later extracted large legal settlements from the two biggest agencies.

## Regional spread available

At least five usable regions:

- **United States** — SEC NRSRO registration (Credit Rating Agency Reform Act 2006), Dodd-Frank's Office of Credit Ratings (2010), annual SEC examinations and a yearly report to Congress; DOJ/state settlements with S&P (2015, $1.375 bn) and Moody's (2017, ~$864 m); three US sovereign downgrades (2011, 2023, 2025). Deepest primary-source trail.
- **European Union** — CRA Regulation 1060/2009 (applies since Dec 2009); ESMA as sole direct supervisor since July 2011, with a public register (~30 CRAs) and a track record of fines against Fitch (2019), S&P (2023) and Scope (2024); France downgraded twice by the Big Three in 2025.
- **Japan** — FSA registration regime under the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act since 1 April 2010, replacing an older "designation" system; two large home-grown agencies (JCR, R&I) alongside the Japanese arms of the Big Three. See Japanese angle below.
- **Emerging-market / non-US sovereign** — France 2025 (an advanced economy but a clean "not the US" case); or Russia (Fitch cut to 'C', default "imminent," March 2022). France is the cleaner, better-sourced option.
- **China** — domestic agencies (China Chengxin, Dagong, China Lianhe) plus the phased 2019-2020 entry of S&P, Moody's and Fitch into China's onshore market. Optional; academic sourcing only.

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan regulates credit rating agencies through a registration-and-supervision regime run by the Financial Services Agency (FSA) under the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act. It took effect on 1 April 2010 and replaced a "designation" system that had operated for roughly 20 years. Registered agencies must observe a duty of good faith, maintain systems to prevent conflicts of interest and ensure fair ratings, are barred from rating securities they themselves hold, and must publish their rating policies and explanatory documents, all under FSA supervision. By 30 September 2010 five agencies were registered, including two Japanese firms — Japan Credit Rating Agency (JCR) and Rating and Investment Information (R&I) — alongside the Japanese entities of Moody's and S&P; Fitch Ratings Japan was later added. JCR is also one of the small number of non-EU agencies "certified" by ESMA for use in the EU. Verifiable source: FSA Newsletter No. 88, July 2010, https://www.fsa.go.jp/en/newsletter/2010/07a.html [VERIFIED OPENED]; JCR's own statement that it "was registered as a 'Credit Rating Agency' with the Financial Services Agency in September 2010" at https://www.jcr.co.jp/en/service/company/regu/ [VERIFIED OPENED].

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **credit rating** — a graded opinion (e.g. AAA, BBB, B) on how likely a borrower is to repay a debt in full and on time.
- **credit rating agency (CRA)** — a company that researches borrowers and bonds and publishes credit ratings; the largest are S&P Global Ratings, Moody's and Fitch.
- **issuer-pays model** — the standard arrangement in which the borrower issuing a bond pays the agency to rate it (rather than investors paying for the research).
- **conflict of interest** — a situation where someone's duty (here, to give an accurate rating) clashes with their own or a client's financial interest (here, in a high rating and repeat business).
- **investment grade vs. speculative grade** — ratings of roughly BBB-/Baa3 and above are "investment grade"; lower ratings are "speculative grade," "high-yield," or informally "junk."
- **sovereign rating** — a credit rating on the debt of a national government.
- **downgrade / upgrade** — a move of a rating to a lower / higher grade, signalling higher / lower assessed risk of non-payment.
- **mortgage-backed security (MBS) / collateralized debt obligation (CDO)** — bonds built by pooling home loans (or other debts) and selling claims on the cash they produce; central to the 2008 crisis.
- **registration / designation** — official listing of a rating agency by a regulator (SEC "NRSRO" registration; ESMA registration; Japan's FSA registration, which replaced an older "designation" system) as a condition of its ratings being used for regulatory purposes.
- **supervision / examination** — ongoing regulator oversight of a registered agency, including periodic on-site or desk reviews of its controls, conflicts management and record-keeping.
- **structured finance** — bonds created by packaging and slicing pools of loans or other assets, as opposed to a plain bond issued by a single company or government.
- **settlement (legal)** — an agreement to resolve a lawsuit or investigation, usually by paying money, often without an admission of illegal conduct.

## Notes / cautions

- **Settlement figures are widely misquoted.** The S&P deal is **US$1.375 billion** with DOJ + 19 states + DC (federal penalty $687.5 m); the commonly cited "$1.5 billion" bundles in a separate $125 m CalPERS settlement. The Moody's deal is **"nearly $864 million"** with DOJ + 21 states + DC (federal penalty $437.5 m). Verify against the DOJ press releases, not news write-ups. Both DOJ pages block automated fetching — a human should open the live justice.gov URLs (or the /archives/ variants) to confirm before print.
- **"Big Three market share" varies by source, metric and year.** The precise, official, datable figure is the SEC's: the three large NRSROs held **94.15% of US ratings outstanding as of 31 December 2023**. Looser figures ("about 95%", "more than 90% of the global market") are common in press and academic work — attribute and date any such figure, and prefer the SEC number as the hard anchor.
- **Scope Ratings fine is 2024, not 2021.** ESMA fined Scope €2,197,500 on **22 March 2024**. The 2019 Fitch fine (€5,132,500, 28 March 2019) and the 2023 S&P fine (€1,110,000, 24 March 2023) are the other verified ESMA enforcement actions.
- **US sovereign downgrades — get the agency and notation right.** S&P: AAA→AA+ on 5 Aug 2011. Fitch: AAA→AA+ on 1 Aug 2023. Moody's: Aaa→Aa1 on 16 May 2025 (Moody's uses the "Aaa/Aa1" scale; S&P and Fitch use "AAA/AA+"). As of August 2026 none of the three rates US debt at its top grade.
- **France downgrades are recent (Sept-Oct 2025).** Fitch AA-→A+ on 12 Sep 2025; S&P AA-/A-1+→A+/A-1 on 17 Oct 2025. Moody's still rated France above single-A as of the last checks — do not write "all three" for France. Reuters covered both but reuters.com is not machine-fetchable; France 24, CNBC, Bloomberg and Euronews carried the same facts.
- **Japan regime date.** The FSA registration system took effect **1 April 2010** under the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act, replacing a ~20-year "designation" system; five agencies were registered by 30 Sept 2010. JCR's corporate page says "September 2010" for its own registration — consistent.
- **SEC scope limit worth noting.** US law bars the SEC from regulating the *substance* of ratings or the methodologies; NRSRO registration is explicitly "not a … 'seal of approval.'" The EU and Japan regimes are similarly conduct- and disclosure-focused rather than merit-based.
- **China material is academically sourced only** — no regulator-primary URL was opened. Use it as colour, not as a hard anchor, or drop it in favour of the France case.
