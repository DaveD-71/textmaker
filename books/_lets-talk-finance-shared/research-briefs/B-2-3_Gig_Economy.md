## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

The "gig economy" here means paid work found through smartphone apps and websites — ride-hailing, food delivery, and online freelancing marketplaces — where each task or trip is a separate small job rather than an ongoing job with one employer. Workers value the flexibility to choose their own hours, but they carry risks that employees do not: income that rises and falls week to week, and usually no paid holiday, sick pay, employer pension contributions, or unemployment cover, because platforms classify them as self-employed. Courts, parliaments and regulators across Europe, North America and Asia are now fighting over whether these workers are really "employees" (or an in-between "worker" category) entitled to labour protections, and several have changed the law.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **UK: on 19 February 2021 the UK Supreme Court ruled unanimously (in _Uber BV and others v Aslam and others_, [2021] UKSC 5) that Uber drivers are "workers" — not self-employed contractors — and are therefore entitled to the national minimum wage and paid annual leave, with working time counting from when a driver has the app switched on, is in their licensed territory and is ready to accept trips.** The case had run through the Employment Tribunal, Employment Appeal Tribunal and Court of Appeal since 2016, and the Supreme Court upheld each. 2. **EU: Directive (EU) 2024/2831 on improving working conditions in platform work was adopted by the Council on 14 October 2024, signed 23 October, published in the Official Journal on 11 November 2024, and entered into force on 1 December 2024; Member States must transpose it into national law by 2 December 2026.** It creates a rebuttable legal presumption of an employment relationship when facts showing "direction and control" are present (shifting the burden of proof onto the platform), and it regulates "algorithmic management" — requiring human oversight of significant decisions such as account suspension or dismissal, banning certain data processing (e.g. emotional state, private conversations), and giving workers the right to an explanation of automated decisions. 3. **Spain: Royal Decree-Law 9/2021 of 11 May 2021 (the "Ley Rider" / "Riders' Law") was published in the Boletín Oficial del Estado on 12 May 2021 and took full effect on 12 August 2021.** It amends the Workers' Statute to create a legal presumption that couriers delivering for digital platforms are employees, not self-employed, where the company organises, directs and controls the work directly, indirectly or through an algorithm; it also gives worker representatives the right to be informed of the parameters and rules of the algorithms that affect working conditions. Eurofound records that it affected roughly 17,000 riders then working without employment contracts. Region: EU / Spain. 4. **United States / California: on 25 July 2024 the California Supreme Court unanimously upheld Proposition 22 as constitutional (_Castellanos v. State of California_).** Prop 22, approved by about 58% of California voters in November 2020 after app companies (Uber, Lyft, DoorDash and others) spent more than USD 200 million campaigning for it, keeps app-based ride-hail and delivery drivers classified as independent contractors — exempting them from California's AB5 employee test — while giving them a limited benefits package (guaranteed earnings of 120% of minimum wage for engaged time, a health-care stipend, and occupational-accident insurance) but not sick pay, unemployment insurance or workers' compensation. The Service Employees International Union and four drivers had challenged it. Region: United States (California). 5. **United States / California: AB5, signed 18 September 2019 and effective 1 January 2020, wrote the "ABC test" into California law** — a worker is an employee unless the hiring business shows the worker is (A) free from its control, (B) doing work outside its usual business, and (C) independently established in that trade. It codified and extended the California Supreme Court's 2018 _Dynamex_ decision and was aimed squarely at reclassifying gig drivers as employees; Prop 22 (anchor 4) was the industry's successful response. Region: United States (California). 8. **Japan: the Freelance Act — officially the "Act on Ensuring Proper Transactions Involving Specified Entrusted Business Operations" — took effect on 1 November 2024.** It covers business-to-business transactions with freelancers (defined as individual operators, or one-person companies, who employ no staff) in any sector, and requires the client to state the work, the fee and the payment date in writing/electronically, to pay within 60 days of receiving the work (30 days in re-subcontracting chains), and to have systems to prevent harassment and to give 30 days' notice before ending a continuing contract; it bans unjustified refusal of completed work and unilateral fee cuts. 9. **Japan: on 25 November 2022 the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Labour Relations Commission (Tokyo-to Rōdō Iinkai) ordered Uber Japan and related entities to bargain collectively with the Uber Eats Union, finding that Uber Eats delivery partners are "workers" (rōdōsha) under the Labour Union Act** despite having no employment contracts — reasoning that they work under the company's direction and control, that Uber unilaterally sets the terms, and that their pay is consideration for the provision of labour. Uber called the ruling regrettable for not accounting for "flexible and independent working styles" and, in December 2022, sought a re-examination by the Central Labour Relations Commission. ## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan has taken two distinct routes rather than a single "employee vs self-employed" reclassification. First, the **Freelance Act** ("Act on Ensuring Proper Transactions Involving Specified Entrusted Business Operations"), in force since **1 November 2024**, does not make freelancers employees but imposes fair-dealing duties on the companies that hire them: written statement of the work, fee and payment date; payment within **60 days**; measures against harassment; **30 days' notice** before ending a continuing contract; and a ban on unjustified rejection of completed work or unilateral fee cuts — enforced mainly by the **Japan Fair Trade Commission**. Second, on **25 November 2022** the **Tokyo Metropolitan Government Labour Relations Commission** found that **Uber Eats delivery partners are "workers" under the Labour Union Act** and ordered Uber to bargain collectively with the Uber Eats Union, on the grounds that couriers work under the company's direction and control and are paid for supplying labour; Uber sought a re-examination by the Central Labour Relations Commission in December 2022. ## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

1. **gig economy** — a labour market of short, one-off paid tasks or trips arranged through apps or websites, rather than ongoing jobs with a single employer
2. **platform work** — work found and organised through a digital platform (an app or website) that connects the worker with customers and sets many of the rules
3. **ride-hailing** — booking and paying for a car journey through an app (e.g. Uber, Grab, Bolt, DiDi)
4. **self-employed (independent contractor)** — someone who works for themselves, invoicing clients; they set their own hours but get no employer-provided benefits and less legal protection
5. **employee** — someone hired under a contract of employment, entitled to protections such as minimum wage, paid leave, sick pay and pension contributions
6. **worker status** — in UK law, an in-between category: more protected than the self-employed (minimum wage, paid holiday) but with fewer rights than a full employee
7. **misclassification (bogus / false self-employment)** — labelling someone self-employed when the nature of the work makes them really an employee, so they lose protections they should have
8. **employee benefits** — non-wage entitlements tied to a job: paid holiday, sick pay, parental leave, health cover, employer pension contributions
9. **pension contributions** — money paid regularly into a retirement fund; employees usually get an employer top-up, the self-employed must fund it entirely themselves
10. **income security** — having earnings that are predictable and sufficient enough to plan and live on, rather than swinging sharply from week to week
11. **algorithmic management** — using software to assign tasks, set pay, monitor performance and even deactivate accounts, with limited human involvement
12. **collective bargaining** — negotiation between an employer and a group of workers (usually through a union) over pay and conditions
13. **presumption of employment** — a legal starting assumption that a platform worker is an employee, which the company must produce evidence to rebut

## Notes / cautions

- **"Employee / worker / self-employed" categories differ by country — say which system you are in.** The UK has a three-way split (employee / "worker" / self-employed), and _Aslam_ placed Uber drivers in the middle "worker" tier — minimum wage and paid holiday, but not the full employee package. Spain, California and Japan's Labour Union Act work with a two-way split (employee vs self-employed / independent contractor), so a ruling there is "employee or not". The EU Directive uses a presumption of "an employment relationship" but leaves the precise national categories to each member state. Do not imply a single global definition.
- **Size-of-the-gig-economy figures are contested and definition-dependent — always attribute and date.** The ILO's "five-fold in a decade" is a count of _platforms_ (≈142 in 2010 to ≈777 in 2020), not of workers. The OECD's "1–3% of total employment" (2019 working paper) is a cautious estimate of main-job platform work; broader "ever done any platform work" surveys produce much higher percentages. Platform-industry and consultancy estimates are higher still. Prefer the ILO and OECD framings and label each.
- **EU headcount discrepancy.** The Council's October 2024 press release says "more than 28 million" people work via platforms in the EU; the European Parliament's April 2024 newsroom item and much later commentary use "around 40 million" (a projection for 2025). Both trace to Commission impact-assessment work at different dates. If you cite a number, pick one source and give its date; safest is "tens of millions."
- **Japan Uber Eats decision — two dates in circulation.** The Tokyo Metropolitan Government Labour Relations Commission's order was publicly issued and reported on **25 November 2022** (Kyodo); JILPT's commentary gives the decision date as **4 October 2022**. Use "November 2022" in running text, or write "issued 25 November 2022" and footnote the 4 October decision date. Also note the case is **not final** — Uber asked the Central Labour Relations Commission to re-examine it in December 2022; as of this brief no reversal or final resolution has been confirmed, so phrase it as "a labour-board ruling, under appeal", not settled law.
- **Prop 22 vs AB5 sequence matters.** AB5 (2020) tried to make gig drivers employees; the companies funded Prop 22 (Nov 2020) to carve themselves out; a lower court struck Prop 22 down in 2021; the Court of Appeal reversed; the California Supreme Court upheld Prop 22 on 25 July 2024. Present it as a back-and-forth that the platforms ultimately won _in California_, not as the US position — most US states never adopted an AB5-style test.
- **Spain "Riders' Law" is narrow.** It creates the employee presumption specifically for **delivery/distribution couriers**, not for ride-hailing drivers or online freelancers; the algorithm-transparency duty is broader. Enforcement has been uneven and some platforms restructured rather than hire couriers directly — worth a one-line caveat if the reading leans on Spain.
