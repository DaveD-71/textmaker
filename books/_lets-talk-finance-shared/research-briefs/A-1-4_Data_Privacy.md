# Research Brief — Book A 1.4 Data Privacy and Protection

_Research compiled 2026-08-28. Every URL below was opened during research unless marked otherwise. All figures should be re-checked against the primary source at drafting time._

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

How personal financial data — account details, transaction histories, credit records, identity documents — is protected by law, and what happens when it leaks. The reading looks at three things at once: the rules that data-protection authorities enforce (and the fines they impose), the rules governing moving personal data across borders, and the underlying tension between organisations wanting to use data (for credit scoring, fraud detection, personalised services, AI) and individuals' right to have it protected. Japan is treated as one case among several, alongside the EU, the United States, and other regions.

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan's Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI, Act No. 57 of 2003) was one of Asia's earliest national privacy laws. A landmark 2015 amendment took full effect on 30 May 2017 and established the Personal Information Protection Commission (PPC), an independent supervisory authority, and built in a requirement to review the Act roughly every three years. The amendment enacted in 2020 took full effect on 1 April 2022, strengthening individuals' rights, making breach reporting to the PPC mandatory in serious cases, and tightening rules on transferring personal data outside Japan (opt-in consent with disclosure of the destination country's regime, or an equivalent protection framework with the overseas recipient). On the cross-border side, the European Commission's Implementing Decision (EU) 2019/419 of 23 January 2019 recognised Japan as providing "adequate" protection, and the PPC made a reciprocal finding for the EU — allowing personal data to flow both ways without additional safeguards, the first such mutual arrangement the EU concluded. Verifiable facts: APPI = Act No. 57 of 2003; 2019/419 dated 23 January 2019 (EUR-Lex, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32019D0419; PPC, https://www.ppc.go.jp/en/).

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **personal data** (also "personal information") — information relating to an identified or identifiable person
- **data breach** — a security incident exposing personal data to unauthorised access, loss, or disclosure
- **data protection authority (DPA)** / **regulator** — the body that enforces privacy law (e.g. Ireland's DPC, Japan's PPC, California's CPPA)
- **consent** — a person's freely given, informed agreement to a specified use of their data
- **cross-border data transfer** — moving personal data from one country/jurisdiction to another
- **adequacy decision** — an official finding that another country's data protection is strong enough to allow data to flow there freely
- **standard contractual clauses (SCCs)** — pre-approved contract terms used to legitimise transfers where no adequacy decision applies
- **regulatory fine / administrative penalty** — a monetary sanction imposed by a DPA for breaking the rules
- **encryption** — encoding data so it is unreadable without a key; a common protective measure
- **breach notification** — the legal duty to report a data breach to the regulator and/or affected individuals
- **data minimisation** — collecting and keeping only the personal data actually needed
- **credit bureau / credit reporting agency** — a company that compiles individuals' credit histories (e.g. Equifax)

## Notes / cautions

- **Verify every fine and breach figure against a primary or authoritative source before publishing — these numbers are widely misquoted.** In particular: the Meta fine is EUR 1.2 billion (not "USD 1.3 billion," a figure that appeared in some early headlines); the Equifax settlement is "up to USD 700 million" total with "up to USD 425 million" for consumers — avoid the bare "USD 575 million" or "USD 700 million" without the "up to" qualifier; the Capital One OCC fine is USD 80 million (a separate USD 190 million class-action settlement also exists — do not conflate).
- **FTC.gov pages returned HTTP 403 to the research tool.** The Equifax facts were verified via the CFPB's archived joint press release, which carries the same figures. The FTC pages remain the canonical citation and should be opened manually to confirm.
- **UNCTAD's legislation tracker (unctad.org) and the European Commission's adequacy-decisions overview page both blocked the research tool (403).** The "144 countries" figure is from IAPP (opened successfully); the Japan adequacy decision was verified directly on EUR-Lex.
- **China PIPL and India DPDP:** effective/enactment dates were verified only via secondary legal explainers and Wikipedia during this research. Open a government or established-outlet source before citing exact dates.
- **The "cumulative GDPR fines" figure moves constantly** and differs by source (DLA Piper ~EUR 5.88bn as of Jan 2025; CMS ~EUR 6.11bn as of 1 Mar 2026). State the as-of date with any figure, or use it only directionally ("more than EUR 5 billion in fines since 2018").
- **The UK's EU adequacy decision was time-limited and set to lapse / be reviewed around 27 December 2025** — check its current status (it may have been renewed or amended) before making a present-tense claim. Current month is August 2026.
- Keep the data-use-versus-protection tension concrete for learners: credit scoring, fraud detection, AML/identity checks, and AI model training are the everyday reasons financial firms want broad data access; breaches, profiling, and surveillance access by foreign governments (the issue behind the Meta transfer case) are the countervailing risks.
