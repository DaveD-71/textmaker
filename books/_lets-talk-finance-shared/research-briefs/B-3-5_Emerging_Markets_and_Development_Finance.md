# Research Brief — Book B 3.5 Emerging Markets and Development Finance

Prepared 2026-09-10 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts verified against at least two independent sources; where an IMF/World Bank page blocked automated fetch, news co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

"Emerging markets" are middle- and lower-income economies that are growing and opening to global finance but are not yet rich or fully stable — a group that ranges from India, now the world's fourth-largest economy, to countries that regularly need rescue loans. Development finance is the money that flows to these economies to fund growth and to bridge crises: private investment and bank lending, money sent home by migrant workers, loans from richer governments (bilateral lenders such as China, Japan and the Gulf states), and loans from institutions many countries own together (multilateral lenders such as the IMF and the World Bank). The topic's structure is problem then responses: when global conditions turn — a stronger dollar, higher US interest rates, a commodity shock — capital can leave an emerging economy quickly, and this article walks through the tools used to prevent or repair the damage.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The split screen (the opening comparison).** In 2025 India became the world's fourth-largest economy, with nominal GDP of about US$4.19 trillion, edging past Japan, and it remained the fastest-growing major economy (projected growth around 6.2-6.5% in 2025-26). Over the same period dozens of other developing economies were in or entering IMF programmes after currency falls and heavy debt-service costs. Same global conditions, opposite trajectories. Region: India / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — CNBC ("India set to be the world's fourth-largest economy," citing IMF April 2025 World Economic Outlook, US$4.187tn vs Japan's US$4.186tn) and Gulf News / NITI Aayog statements; growth-rate range corroborated by IMF WEO coverage across multiple outlets. **Flag:** the "fourth-largest" ranking is close and could shift year to year; date it to 2025 and cite the IMF WEO vintage.

2. **The problem: capital flow reversals ("sudden stops").** When the US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate 11 times from March 2022 (about +5.25 percentage points), higher dollar returns pulled investment capital back toward the United States and away from emerging markets. In the 2013 "taper tantrum," emerging-market bonds lost more than 10% of their value as capital fled; in 2022-23 most emerging markets weathered the Fed's tightening better because many had raised their own interest rates early and built up larger foreign-exchange reserves. Region: global / US / emerging markets. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Brookings ("How have Fed interest rate hikes affected other national economies?") and the Federal Reserve Board's FEDS Notes ("U.S. Interest Rates and Emerging Market Currencies: Taking Stock 10 Years After the Taper Tantrum"); corroborated by the Dallas Fed and Al Jazeera.

3. **Response — the IMF.** The IMF lends to member countries in balance-of-payments trouble, usually through a short Stand-By Arrangement or a multi-year Extended Fund Facility, with policy conditions attached ("conditionality"). Pakistan received a 37-month Extended Fund Facility of about **US$7 billion** (SDR 5,320 million), approved 25 September 2024, following a smaller roughly US$3 billion Stand-By Arrangement in 2023. Region: Pakistan / South Asia. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — IMF press release PR-24343 ("IMF Executive Board ... Approves 37-month Extended Arrangement," imf.org) and Voice of America / Deccan Herald reporting of the US$7 billion approval; the 2023 SBA is widely documented.

4. **Response — the IMF's reserve top-up (SDRs).** On 23 August 2021 the IMF made its largest-ever allocation of Special Drawing Rights — **SDR 456.5 billion, about US$650 billion** — distributed to all members in proportion to their IMF quotas to boost global reserves during COVID. About **US$275 billion** of it went to emerging market and developing economies. SDRs are a reserve asset countries can hold or exchange for hard currency, not a loan. Region: global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — IMF ("Special Drawing Rights" topic page and press release PR21248, imf.org) and Devex ("IMF approves $650B Special Drawing Rights allocation"); the US$275 billion EMDE share is stated on the IMF's own page.

5. **Response — stacked support: Egypt, 2024.** In March 2024 Egypt's IMF programme was enlarged from US$3 billion (agreed December 2022) to **US$8 billion**; in return Egypt let its pound float — it fell about 38% — and raised interest rates by 600 basis points in one step. Days earlier, the United Arab Emirates had agreed the "Ras El-Hekma" deal: about **US$35 billion** for development rights to a stretch of Mediterranean coast, including roughly US$24 billion in fresh foreign-currency deposits plus the conversion of about US$11 billion in existing UAE deposits. IMF money, Gulf bilateral money and a currency float arrived within the same fortnight. Region: Egypt / MENA / UAE. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Al Jazeera ("Egypt strikes expanded $8bn deal with the IMF," 6 March 2024) and The National / WSLS-AP (currency float ~38%, 600bp hike); the Ras El-Hekma structure corroborated by Gulf News and Reuters coverage.

6. **Response — the World Bank and regional development banks.** The World Bank Group lends for long-term development: its IDA arm gives grants and near-zero-interest credits to the poorest countries, while its IBRD arm lends to middle-income ones at close to market rates; regional banks such as the Asian Development Bank and the African Development Bank do the same within their regions. These are the institutions that fund the roads, power and schools behind "development" rather than crisis rescue. Region: global / multilateral. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — World Bank IDA/IBRD descriptions (worldbank.org) and OECD development-finance profiles; the IDA-vs-IBRD split is standard, widely documented. **Ledger note:** the World Bank's and ADB's FY2025 headline commitment figures (US$118.5 billion / US$29.3 billion) are **primary** evidence in Book A 3.2 Infrastructure Investment — do **not** reuse those numbers here; describe the *role* (concessional vs near-market lending) instead.

7. **Bilateral lenders — China.** China has become the largest single official bilateral creditor to the developing world: it is the top bilateral creditor in 53 countries, holds roughly a quarter of developing countries' external bilateral debt (more than half for the poorest and most vulnerable), and is owed on the order of **US$1.1 trillion** in outstanding principal. Its lending has shifted from Belt and Road infrastructure toward "rescue lending" — emergency loans to countries already in financial distress. Region: China / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — AidData figures reported by Newsweek ("How China Became the World's Largest Debt Collector," ~US$1.1tn outstanding, top bilateral creditor in 53 countries) and NBC News ("Chinese lending shifts from Belt and Road to rescue finance"); corroborated by the Lowy Institute and France 24. **Flag:** China does not publish loan-by-loan data, so all totals are researcher estimates with ranges (AidData: ~US$1.1tn outstanding; ~US$1.34tn lent 2000-2021) — present as "estimated at around."

8. **Remittances — the quiet giant of development finance.** Money sent home by migrant workers reached about **US$685 billion** to low- and middle-income countries in 2024 — more than foreign direct investment and official aid to those countries combined. India was the top recipient at about **US$129 billion**, followed by Mexico, China, the Philippines and Pakistan. Remittances are private, go straight to households, and tend to be more stable than investment flows during a downturn. Region: global / India. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — World Bank Migration and Development Brief figures reported by Human Progress ("Remittances to Poor Countries Reached $685 Billion in 2024," India ~US$129bn, "exceeded the combined total of net FDI and ODA") and the UN DESA World Economic Situation and Prospects briefing.

9. **Japan as a creditor (short paragraph, not the subject).** Japan is one of the world's largest bilateral development lenders through the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA), which extends yen loans for infrastructure across Asia and beyond, operates in more than 150 countries, and commands financial resources of roughly JPY 1 trillion (about US$8.5 billion); about 58% of Japan's 2024 ODA was loans rather than grants. Japan is also a founding member and — with the United States — the largest shareholder of the Asian Development Bank. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — JICA / Japan MOFA descriptions and OECD "Development Co-operation Profiles: Japan"; ADB "Japan and ADB" partnership pages (adb.org); JICA scale figures via Devex ("The yen has fallen, but JICA has grown").

## The Japanese angle (short paragraph — Japan is a lender here, not the subject)

In this topic Japan appears on the creditor's side of the table. Through the Japan International Cooperation Agency it is one of the world's largest bilateral development lenders, extending long-tenor yen loans for ports, railways, power plants and water systems across Asia and, increasingly, Africa; it operates in more than 150 countries with financial resources of roughly JPY 1 trillion, and about 58% of its 2024 official development assistance took the form of loans rather than grants. Japan is also a founding member and, jointly with the United States, the largest shareholder of the Asian Development Bank, and it has positioned its lending around "quality infrastructure" — an implicit contrast with the faster, less transparent Belt and Road lending that made China the developing world's biggest official creditor. For a Japanese reader, the point is not that Japan needs development finance but that Japan helps supply it, and that how much of that supply comes as concessional multilateral money versus bilateral loans with strategic strings attached is itself one of the live questions in the field.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **emerging market** — a developing economy that is growing and integrating with global finance but is not yet high-income or fully stable
- **development finance** — the mix of public and private money that funds growth and crisis support in developing economies
- **balance of payments** — a record of a country's transactions with the rest of the world; a "balance-of-payments crisis" is running short of foreign currency to pay for imports and debt
- **capital flows / capital flight** — investment money moving into a country, and moving out rapidly
- **sudden stop** — an abrupt halt or reversal of foreign capital inflows to an economy
- **the IMF (International Monetary Fund)** — the institution that lends foreign currency to member countries in payments trouble
- **conditionality** — the policy changes a borrower must make in return for an IMF loan
- **Stand-By Arrangement / Extended Fund Facility** — a short-term / multi-year IMF lending programme
- **Special Drawing Rights (SDRs)** — an international reserve asset created by the IMF and allocated to member countries
- **bilateral vs multilateral creditor** — a single lending government (bilateral) vs an institution many governments own (multilateral)
- **concessional lending** — loans at below-market interest rates or with long grace periods, for poorer countries
- **debt distress / debt sustainability** — being unable, or barely able, to service external debt
- **remittances** — money sent home by workers living abroad
- **foreign direct investment (FDI)** — investment to build or buy a lasting business stake in another country
- **official development assistance (ODA)** — government aid (grants and concessional loans) to developing countries
