# Research Brief — Book A 4.1 Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Regulations

Prepared 2026-09-11 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts checked against at least two independent sources; where a regulator/standard-setter page blocked automated fetch, news and law-firm co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Money laundering is moving the proceeds of crime — drug sales, fraud, corruption, sanctions evasion — through the financial system so the money looks legitimate when it comes out. Anti-money-laundering (AML) rules push the job of spotting it onto banks and other regulated businesses: they must identify their customers ("know your customer"), watch for unusual activity, report suspicious transactions to a government financial-intelligence unit, and screen against sanctions lists — and they are fined heavily, or prosecuted, when they do not. The global template is set by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF), a standard-setter whose "grey" and "black" lists give countries a strong incentive to comply.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The global standard-setter: FATF.** The Financial Action Task Force was set up by the G7 in 1989 and issued its "40 Recommendations" in 1990 (extended to terrorist financing in 2001). It has 40 member jurisdictions plus nine FATF-style regional bodies, and its standards shape AML law in more than 200 countries and territories. FATF assesses each country through peer "mutual evaluations" and maintains a black list ("high-risk jurisdictions subject to a call for action" — currently Iran, North Korea and Myanmar) and a grey list ("jurisdictions under increased monitoring"). Region: global standard-setter. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — FATF "History of the FATF" (fatf-gafi.org) and Wikipedia's FATF entry (aggregating FATF primary material), corroborated by multiple compliance-industry explainers on the 1989 founding, the 40 Recommendations and the two lists.

2. **The scale (order of magnitude only).** The most-cited estimate, originating with the IMF in the late 1990s and still used by the UN Office on Drugs and Crime, is that 2-5% of global GDP is laundered each year — very roughly US$800 billion to US$2 trillion. Region: global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — UNODC "Money Laundering" overview and repeated citations across FATF/IMF-linked material. **Flag:** this is an old, wide, order-of-magnitude figure, not a measured number; present it as "estimated at a few percent of world output" and attribute it to the UNODC/IMF, not as precise.

3. **A defining bank scandal: Danske Bank's Estonia branch.** Between roughly 2007 and 2015, about EUR 200 billion in largely non-resident, high-risk transactions — much of it linked to Russia and other former-Soviet states — flowed through the small Estonian branch of Denmark's largest bank. In December 2022 Danske Bank pleaded guilty in the United States and agreed to forfeit about US$2 billion (with a further US$413 million to the US Securities and Exchange Commission) for defrauding US banks about the state of its AML controls. Region: EU / Nordics-Baltics / United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — US Department of Justice press release ("Danske Bank Pleads Guilty to Fraud on U.S. Banks," 13 December 2022) and CNN Business / Bloomberg coverage; the SEC's separate US$413 million settlement is in the DOJ and Greenberg Traurig write-ups.

4. **A record US penalty: TD Bank, 2024.** On 10 October 2024, TD Bank — the 10th-largest bank in the US — pleaded guilty to Bank Secrecy Act program failures and to conspiracy to commit money laundering, agreeing to pay about US$3 billion in combined criminal and civil penalties (US$1.8 billion of it a DOJ criminal fine). It was the largest US bank ever to plead guilty to Bank Secrecy Act failures and the first to plead guilty to a money-laundering conspiracy; regulators also capped the growth of its US assets at about US$434 billion. Region: United States / Canada (parent). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — US DOJ ("TD Bank Pleads Guilty to Bank Secrecy Act and Money Laundering Conspiracy Violations") and CNBC / CBC News reporting of the ~US$3 billion total and the OCC asset cap.

5. **A new EU supervisor: AMLA.** As part of a 2024 overhaul, the EU created a central Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA), legally established in 2024 and based in Frankfurt, which began operating in 2025. A single EU AML rulebook (Regulation (EU) 2024/1624) applies from 10 July 2027, and from January 2028 AMLA will directly supervise around 40 of the highest-risk cross-border financial institutions — a shift from AML rules that each member state wrote and enforced on its own. Region: European Union. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — AMLA "Single Programming Document 2026-2028" (amla.europa.eu) and law-firm analyses (A&O Shearman, Moody's, Freshfields) all giving the Frankfurt seat, the 10 July 2027 rulebook date and the ~40-institution 2028 direct-supervision start.

6. **Japan's FATF result and response.** The FATF's fourth-round Mutual Evaluation Report on Japan, published 30 August 2021, found Japan's system was "delivering results" but placed the country in "enhanced follow-up" and told it to prioritise improvements. Japan then amended two key laws — the Act on Prevention of Transfer of Criminal Proceeds (adding AML duties for crypto-asset exchange providers, including the "travel rule") and the Act on Punishment of Organized Crimes — and was re-rated upward on several Recommendations in follow-up reports in 2022-23. Japan's financial-intelligence unit is JAFIC, inside the National Police Agency. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — FATF "Japan's measures to combat money laundering and terrorist financing" (Mutual Evaluation, 2021) and "Japan's progress ..." (Follow-Up, 2022), corroborated by Japan's Financial Services Agency ("JFSA's initiatives ... in response to FATF Fourth Round MER," fsa.go.jp) and Lexology's Japan AML overview.

7. **The core toolkit (definitional anchor).** Regulated firms must carry out customer due diligence (identify the customer and, for companies, the "beneficial owner" — the real person who controls it), apply enhanced checks to higher-risk customers such as "politically exposed persons," monitor transactions, file suspicious-transaction (or suspicious-activity) reports to the national financial-intelligence unit, and screen customers and payments against sanctions lists. FATF's 2022 strengthening of Recommendation 24 pushed countries toward central beneficial-ownership registers. Region: global mechanics. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — FATF Recommendations text as summarised across FATF material and compliance explainers; the 2022 Recommendation 24 change widely reported. **Note:** the EU's move to *public* beneficial-ownership registers was partly struck down by the Court of Justice of the EU in November 2022 on privacy grounds — a useful "tension" point if the Reading wants one.

8. **Crypto and the "travel rule."** FATF extended its standards to crypto-asset service providers in 2019 and requires them to pass on sender and recipient information with transfers (the "travel rule," Recommendation 16). Japan brought this into force via its 2022 APTCP amendment; the EU applies it through its 2023 Transfer of Funds Regulation. Region: global / Japan / EU. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — FATF virtual-asset guidance as reported across FATF updates and industry coverage; Japan's APTCP crypto amendment confirmed in the FATF follow-up material and JFSA notes.

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — mid-article, per the shape plan)

Japan's AML system is best understood through its 2021 FATF report card. After a peer review, the Financial Action Task Force concluded that Japan's controls were producing results but had real gaps, and it placed Japan in "enhanced follow-up" — a status requiring regular progress reports rather than a one-off pass. The government's response over the following two years was a burst of legislation: it amended the Act on Prevention of Transfer of Criminal Proceeds to bring crypto-asset exchanges fully into the AML regime, including the FATF "travel rule" that requires identifying information to travel with a transfer, and it tightened the Act on Punishment of Organized Crimes. Supervision is split — the Financial Services Agency oversees banks and exchanges, while suspicious-transaction reports flow to JAFIC, a financial-intelligence unit housed in the National Police Agency. For a Japanese reader, the useful framing is that AML is less a domestic policy choice than an international standard a trading nation has to keep pace with: falling behind FATF norms risks a poor evaluation, which raises the cost and friction of every cross-border payment Japanese banks make.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **money laundering** — moving the proceeds of crime through the financial system to make them appear legitimate
- **anti-money-laundering (AML)** — the framework of laws and controls meant to detect and prevent it
- **countering the financing of terrorism (CFT)** — the closely linked rules aimed at cutting off funding for terrorism
- **Financial Action Task Force (FATF)** — the intergovernmental body that sets the global AML/CFT standards
- **mutual evaluation** — FATF's peer review of a country's AML system
- **grey list / black list** — FATF's lists of countries under increased monitoring / high-risk countries facing countermeasures
- **know your customer (KYC) / customer due diligence (CDD)** — identifying and verifying who a customer is before and during a business relationship
- **beneficial owner** — the real person who ultimately owns or controls a company or account
- **politically exposed person (PEP)** — someone in a senior public role, treated as higher-risk for corruption
- **suspicious-transaction / suspicious-activity report (STR/SAR)** — a report a regulated firm files to the authorities about activity that may involve crime
- **financial-intelligence unit (FIU)** — the government body that receives and analyses those reports (Japan's is JAFIC)
- **sanctions screening** — checking customers and payments against government lists of barred people and entities
- **the "travel rule"** — the requirement that identifying information accompany a funds or crypto transfer
- **de-risking** — a bank exiting whole categories of customers or countries to avoid AML risk rather than manage it
