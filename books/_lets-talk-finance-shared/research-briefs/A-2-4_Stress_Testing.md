## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

A supervisory stress test is an exercise in which a central bank or regulator models a severe hypothetical shock — a deep recession, a market crash, a jump in unemployment, a property-price collapse — and calculates whether each large bank would still hold enough capital to keep lending and absorb the losses. Banks are judged against a minimum capital ratio (usually a Common Equity Tier 1, or CET1, "hurdle"): a bank that would fall below it must raise capital, cut dividends and buybacks, or shrink its balance sheet. The tests have limits — they only cover banks above a size threshold, and they model the scenarios the designers chose, so a real shock that was not in the scenario (as with the interest-rate losses that sank Silicon Valley Bank in 2023) can still be missed.

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan does not run a single headline pass/fail bank stress test the way the US Fed or the EBA do. Instead the Bank of Japan and the Financial Services Agency (FSA) build common macroeconomic scenarios and run macro stress tests across the banking system, with the aggregate results published twice a year in the BOJ's Financial System Report. The framework covers roughly 371 deposit-taking institutions — about 10 major banks (including the three G-SIBs), around 105 regional banks and around 256 shinkin banks. The April 2026 Financial System Report modelled a "tail event" as severe as the 2008 global financial crisis and a "tailored event" combining a geopolitical oil-price shock, weaker AI-driven growth expectations and a sharp rise in interest rates, and concluded that Japanese banks have "sufficient capital bases and stable funding bases to withstand various stress situations." Separately, the FSA launched a "supervisory simultaneous stress test based on common scenarios" for the largest banks in December 2020. ## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **stress test** — an exercise that models a severe hypothetical shock (deep recession, market crash) to check whether a bank would still have enough capital to absorb the losses and keep operating.
- **scenario** — the specific set of assumptions a stress test runs on (e.g. unemployment rising to 10%, house prices falling 30%); usually a "baseline" and one or more "adverse" or "severely adverse" scenarios.
- **adverse / severely adverse scenario** — the deliberately harsh hypothetical downturn a bank must be able to survive in the test.
- **capital ratio / CET1** — a bank's core loss-absorbing capital (Common Equity Tier 1) measured as a percentage of its risk-weighted assets; the main gauge in a stress test.
- **hurdle rate / minimum requirement** — the capital ratio a bank must stay above in the test; falling below it means the bank "fails."
- **capital shortfall / buffer** — the extra capital a bank must raise to reach the required level after projected stress losses.
- **systemically important bank (G-SIB)** — a bank large or interconnected enough that its failure would threaten the wider financial system, so it faces tougher rules and testing.
- **interest-rate risk** — the risk that changes in interest rates cut the value of a bank's assets (e.g. long-dated bonds lose value when rates rise) or squeeze its earnings.
- **resolution** — the official process for managing a failing bank (closing it, selling it, or winding it down) without a disorderly collapse or, ideally, a taxpayer bailout.
- **macroprudential** — regulation aimed at the stability of the financial system as a whole, not just individual firms.
- **tailoring / asset threshold** — rules that apply the strictest requirements (including full stress testing) only to banks above a certain size, easing them for smaller banks.
- **exploratory / climate scenario** — a stress exercise run to learn about a risk (e.g. climate change) rather than to pass or fail banks or set capital.

## Notes / cautions

- **Tie every headline number to a named year and exercise.** "US$75 billion across 10 of 19 banks" = 2009 SCAP only. "US$708 billion of losses, 32 banks, CET1 down 1.6 points" = the Fed's 2026 test. "US$550 billion, 22 banks, down 1.8 points" = the Fed's 2025 test. "~370 bps depletion, 64 banks" = the EBA's 2025 round (2023 was 479 bps). "14.2% to 10.8%, hurdle 6.9%, seven firms" = the Bank of England's 2025 test. Do not blend them.
- **The 2026 Fed headline improved mainly because of scenario design, not stronger banks.** The severely adverse scenario assumed a higher floor for long-term Treasury yields (about 2.3%, versus ~1.0% in 2025), which reduces modelled bond losses. CNBC/Reuters and Forbes commentary make this point; worth a sentence on "the result depends on the assumptions."
- **The SVB point is well documented and central to the "limits" part of the topic.** SVB was a state-chartered bank that had grown to roughly US$209–211 billion in assets, below the US$250 billion threshold that the 2018 EGRRCPA law and the Fed's 2019 tailoring rule use to trigger full enhanced standards and annual stress testing. Its losses came from long-dated bonds falling in value as rates rose in 2022–23 — an interest-rate/liquidity risk the annual scenarios did not capture for a bank of its type. Signature Bank failed two days later. Both are frequently cited as the second- and third-largest US bank failures (after Washington Mutual in 2008).
- **The ECB 2022 climate test was NOT pass/fail.** Say "a learning exercise" — no bank failed, no capital was required. Same caution applies broadly to "exploratory scenarios" (the Bank of England ran a Climate Biennial Exploratory Scenario reported in 2022).
