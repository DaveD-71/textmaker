# Research Brief — Book A 2.5 Sovereign Debt Management

Prepared August 2026. Compiled by research assistant; all URLs opened unless marked otherwise.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

How a national government actually borrows money: it issues bonds (and short-term bills), and a treasury or specialist debt management office decides how much to sell, at what maturities, through what method (auctions, syndications), and to whom. Two big choices shape the risk: whether to borrow in your own currency or a foreign one, and whether your lenders are domestic or foreign. When a government's debt becomes unpayable it must restructure — negotiate with creditors to stretch out maturities or cut the amount owed — and if talks fail or come too late, it defaults; recent cases are handled under the G20 "Common Framework."

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

Japan is the standout case and works well as the lead. Its general government gross debt is the highest of any major economy — about 206% of GDP in the IMF's April 2026 figures, and higher still (well over 230%) in earlier IMF vintages and on some gross measures — yet Japan has no debt crisis. The reason is who holds the debt: as of March 2026 the Bank of Japan alone held 47.9% of all Japanese Government Bonds (¥485 trillion), and foreign investors held just 8.1%, so roughly 92% of JGBs are financed at home by the central bank, Japanese banks, insurers and pension funds. That domestic, own-currency base means low yields, no foreign-currency mismatch and no rollover panic — the opposite of an emerging-market sovereign borrowing in dollars. The catch is the interest bill: having ended negative rates and yield curve control in March 2024, the Bank of Japan has been raising its policy rate and, from August 2024, cutting its monthly JGB purchases by about ¥400 billion a quarter, so the government's debt-service cost is set to climb as maturing low-coupon bonds are refinanced at higher rates. The debt management office has responded by keeping the average maturity of outstanding JGBs long (about 9 years 6 months) and by trying to broaden the investor base. ## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **sovereign debt** — money owed by a national government
- **government bond** (in the UK, *gilt*; in the US, *Treasury*; in Japan, *JGB*) — a tradable IOU the government sells to borrow
- **Treasury bill** — a short-term government IOU (under one year), sold at a discount
- **debt-to-GDP ratio** — total government debt measured against the size of the economy
- **gross debt vs net debt** — total borrowing vs borrowing minus the government's own financial assets
- **maturity profile** (also *average maturity*, *redemption profile*) — how the repayment dates of the debt are spread out over time
- **domestic vs external debt** — debt owed to lenders inside the country vs lenders abroad
- **local-currency vs foreign-currency debt** — debt the government must repay in its own money vs in dollars, euros, etc.
- **debt management office (DMO)** — the government agency that plans and runs the borrowing
- **bond auction** — the main way governments sell new bonds, to competing bidders
- **rollover risk / refinancing risk** — the danger of not being able to borrow again (or only at high cost) when old debt comes due
- **sovereign default** — a government failing to pay its debts on time
- **debt restructuring** — renegotiating debt: longer maturities, lower interest, or a cut in the amount owed (a *haircut*)
- **haircut** — the reduction in the face value or present value of a bond that creditors accept in a restructuring
- **Common Framework** — the G20's 2020 process for coordinating debt relief for low-income countries
- **original sin** — the inability of most developing countries to borrow abroad in their own currency

## Notes / cautions

- **Which debt measure, and which vintage.** Debt-to-GDP numbers vary a lot depending on (a) *gross* vs *net* (Japan's net debt is far below its gross debt, because the government and its agencies hold large financial assets), (b) *general government* (central + local + social security) vs *central government only*, and (c) which IMF WEO edition. The figures in this brief for Japan (~206% gross, 2025) are from the **IMF WEO April 2026 DataMapper**, general government gross debt. Older WEO editions and many secondary trackers still show Japan near **235-255%** — a large gap, likely from GDP revisions/rebasing and data updates.
