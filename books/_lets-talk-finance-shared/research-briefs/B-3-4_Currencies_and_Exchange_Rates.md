# Research Brief — Book B 3.4 Currencies and Exchange Rates

Prepared 2026-09-10 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts verified against at least two independent sources; where a government/central-bank page blocked automated fetch, news co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

An exchange rate is just the price of one country's money in terms of another's, and like any price it moves — sometimes gradually, sometimes fast. What makes it distinctive is that a single number cuts two ways at once: when a currency falls, the country's exporters and its tourism industry gain, while its importers, its consumers, and anyone who owes money in a foreign currency lose. The topic's job is to replace the everyday assumption that "a weak currency is bad" (or "a strong currency is good") with the more accurate picture — a fall or rise in a currency is a shift in a relative price that helps some people and hurts others — and then to show the hard choice governments face: you can manage your exchange rate, or set interest rates freely for your own economy, or let money move across your borders without limit, but not all three fully at the same time.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The weak yen, 2022-2024 (the opening scenario).** The Japanese yen fell from about 115 per US dollar in early 2022 to about 160-162 per dollar by late June and early July 2024 — its weakest level since 1986, roughly a 38-year low. The main driver was a widening interest-rate gap: the US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate to around 5.25-5.5% to fight inflation while the Bank of Japan held its rate near zero, so investors moved money out of yen and into higher-yielding dollars. Region: Japan / United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — NBC News ("Yen weakens past 160 against the dollar") and CNBC currency coverage, both dating the ~38-year low and citing the rate-gap cause; corroborated by contemporaneous Reuters/Malay Mail reporting of Japan's "fresh warning against sharp yen falls," June 2024. **Flag:** the yen weakened further in 2025-26; anchor the Reading on the dated 2022-24 episode and note the situation kept moving rather than chasing the latest figure.

2. **Japan's currency interventions.** To slow the yen's fall, Japan's Ministry of Finance ordered the Bank of Japan to buy yen and sell US dollars from its foreign-exchange reserves: about JPY 2.8 trillion in September 2022 (Japan's first yen-buying intervention since 1998), about JPY 6.3 trillion in October 2022, and a confirmed JPY 9.7885 trillion (about US$62 billion) between 26 April and 29 May 2024. Intervening to support your own currency means spending down reserves — the reverse of a country that intervenes to hold its currency *down*. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Japan's Ministry of Finance intervention records as reported by CNBC (the JPY 9.7885 trillion / US$62.25 billion April-May 2024 figure, "first ... since October 2022") and NBC News; the 2022 amounts corroborated across Reuters and Nikkei coverage. **Note:** MOF's own intervention-data page (mof.go.jp) is the primary source; it was not opened directly this pass.

3. **The two-sided effect of a weak currency (the trade-off at the centre of the topic).** A weaker yen lifts the yen value of the overseas earnings that big Japanese exporters such as carmakers report at home, and it made Japan cheaper for foreign visitors — inbound tourism hit a record of about 36.9 million visitors in 2024, with record spending. At the same time it raised the yen cost of imported energy, food and materials, and Japanese real wages (pay adjusted for inflation) fell year-on-year for about two years running through 2022-24 as prices rose faster than wages. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Japan National Tourism Organization / Japan Tourism Agency figures as reported by Nikkei Asia and Kyodo (record 2024 visitor numbers and spending); Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare monthly labour statistics as reported by The Japan Times and Reuters (consecutive real-wage declines). **Flag:** pick one clean, dated pair — "record inbound tourism in 2024" against "real wages fell through 2022-24" — rather than piling up sub-figures.

4. **The US dollar's special position.** The dollar made up about 58% of the world's allocated foreign-exchange reserves in 2024 (down from about 71% in 2001 but still by far the largest share), and roughly 88% of all foreign-exchange trades had the dollar on one side. Most oil and other commodities are priced in dollars. This lets the United States borrow more cheaply and in its own currency — an advantage sometimes called an "exorbitant privilege." Region: United States / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — IMF COFER data as summarised by the Atlantic Council ("Does the dollar's declining share in global reserves matter?", ~58% allocated share, down from ~71% in 2001) and IMF COFER dataset notes; the ~88%-of-FX-trades figure from the BIS Triennial Central Bank Survey 2022, widely cited. **Note:** the reserve share drifts a percentage point or two each quarter, mostly on exchange-rate moves — use "about 58%" with a year, not a precise number.

5. **Euro-dollar parity, July 2022.** On 12-13 July 2022 the euro fell to parity with the US dollar — one euro worth one dollar — for the first time since 2002, as investors bet that Russia cutting gas to Europe would push the euro area into recession while the European Central Bank still lagged the Fed on rate rises. A weaker euro made imported energy (priced in dollars) even dearer for European buyers, feeding record euro-area inflation. Region: European Union / euro area. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Euronews and Al Jazeera ("Euro hits US dollar parity for first time in 20 years," 12 July 2022); corroborated by the Christian Science Monitor and Fortune.

6. **An emerging-market case: Argentina, December 2023.** Days after taking office, President Javier Milei devalued the Argentine peso by more than 50% in one step — from about 400 to about 800 pesos per US dollar — as part of a shock stabilisation plan. Argentina's annual inflation, already 211% in 2023, stayed very high through 2024 (about 118%) but monthly inflation fell steadily; the poverty rate jumped to about 53% in the first half of 2024 before easing. It is a clear example of a government choosing a large, deliberate devaluation and accepting the short-term pain. Region: Argentina / Latin America. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — France 24 ("Argentina devalues currency by 50% in 'shock' measure," 13 December 2023) and Buenos Aires Herald / Al Jazeera (2023 inflation 211.4%, 2024 inflation 117.8%, poverty ~53%).

7. **What defending a fixed rate can cost: the Swiss franc, January 2015.** For three and a half years the Swiss National Bank had capped the franc at 1.20 per euro, buying euros without limit to hold the line. On 15 January 2015 it abandoned the cap with no warning; the franc jumped by as much as 30% against the euro within minutes, hitting exporters, triggering losses at banks and brokers worldwide, and later contributing to record losses at the SNB itself. Region: Switzerland / euro area. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — CNBC ("Swiss franc soars, stocks tank as euro peg scrapped," 15 January 2015) and ABC News ("Swiss central bank shocks traders by uncapping franc"); corroborated by FX Markets' one-year retrospective.

8. **The carry trade (optional mechanism, keep light).** Because Japanese interest rates were so low for so long, investors borrowed yen cheaply and put the money into higher-yielding assets abroad — the "yen carry trade." When the yen suddenly strengthened in 2024, some of that trade was unwound quickly, and the selling rippled through global markets. Region: Japan / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — widely reported across Reuters, the Financial Times and the IMF's 2024 *Global Financial Stability Report* commentary. **Ledger flag:** the sharp one-day Nikkei 225 fall of 5 August 2024 (-12.4%) is **primary** evidence in Book A 2.1 Regulatory Responses to Market Volatility — do **not** reuse that statistic here. Refer to the carry-trade *mechanism* and a "sharp global market reaction" in one sentence at most, without the Nikkei figure.

## The Japanese angle (this is the lead — per the batch plan)

The clearest recent lesson that "a weak currency is bad" is too simple comes from Japan. Between early 2022 and mid-2024 the yen fell from about 115 to about 161 per US dollar, a level not seen since 1986, mainly because the Bank of Japan kept interest rates near zero while the US Federal Reserve raised its rate above 5% to fight inflation. Japanese exporters and the tourism industry did well out of it: the weaker yen inflated the home-currency value of profits earned abroad, and it helped drive a record of roughly 36.9 million foreign visitors in 2024. But households felt the other side. Imported energy and food cost more in yen, and real wages — pay after inflation — fell year-on-year for about two years running. The government's response is itself instructive: it did not try to reverse the fall, only to slow it, ordering the Bank of Japan to buy yen with dollars from the national reserves — about JPY 2.8 trillion in September 2022 (the first such move since 1998), more in October 2022, and a confirmed JPY 9.79 trillion between April and May 2024. Supporting your own currency this way means spending reserves, and it works only at the margin against the underlying interest-rate gap. From here the article can widen out to the general principle — an exchange rate is a relative price, and moving it helps some and hurts others — and then to the harder choice every country faces: manage the exchange rate, run monetary policy for domestic goals, or allow free capital flows, but not all three at once.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **exchange rate** — the price of one country's currency in terms of another's
- **appreciation / depreciation** — a rise / fall in a currency's value against another currency (market-driven)
- **devaluation / revaluation** — a deliberate downward / upward move in a currency that a government manages or pegs
- **floating exchange rate** — an exchange rate set by supply and demand in the market
- **fixed (pegged) exchange rate** — an exchange rate a government holds at or near a set level
- **currency peg / cap / floor** — a specific level a central bank commits to defend
- **foreign-exchange (FX) reserves** — a country's holdings of foreign currencies and gold, used partly to intervene in currency markets
- **currency intervention** — a government buying or selling its own currency to influence the exchange rate
- **reserve currency** — a currency that governments widely hold in their reserves and use for trade and borrowing (chiefly the US dollar)
- **carry trade** — borrowing in a low-interest-rate currency to invest in a higher-yielding one
- **real wages** — wages adjusted for inflation; a measure of actual purchasing power
- **capital flows / capital controls** — money moving across borders for investment, and government limits on that movement
- **safe-haven currency** — a currency investors buy in times of stress (the US dollar, Swiss franc, yen)
- **policy trilemma ("impossible trinity")** — the rule that a country cannot at once have a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and an independent monetary policy
