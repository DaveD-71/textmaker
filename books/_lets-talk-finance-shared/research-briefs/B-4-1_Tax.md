# Research Brief — Book B 4.1 Tax: How Governments Raise Money

Prepared 2026-09-11 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts checked against at least two independent sources; where an OECD/government page blocked automated fetch, secondary and advisory-firm co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Almost everything a government does is paid for by tax, and the mix of taxes it uses — income tax, payroll or social-security contributions, a consumption tax such as VAT, corporate tax, property tax — shapes who actually carries the cost. Some taxes are progressive (they take a larger share of income from higher earners), others are regressive (they take a larger share from lower earners), and the "who really pays" question often differs from who writes the cheque: a company remits corporate tax, but the burden falls partly on its workers, shareholders and customers. The topic is a plain guide to where public money comes from, how the mix varies across countries, and the recent international effort to stop large companies routing profits to the lowest-tax jurisdictions.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **How much governments take: the tax-to-GDP ratio.** Across the OECD, total tax revenue averaged about 33.9% of GDP in 2023, ranging from about 17.7% in Mexico to about 43.8% in France. The gap between the highest- and lowest-taxing OECD countries was the narrowest since at least 2000. Region: OECD. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — OECD *Revenue Statistics* 2024/2025 (oecd.org) and independent summaries (statsandgraphs, taxspoc), all giving the ~33.9% average and the Mexico-to-France range for 2023.

2. **The main revenue sources (structural anchor).** For a typical OECD country the largest slices of the tax take are social-security contributions and personal income tax (each around a quarter of revenue), followed by taxes on goods and services including VAT (around a fifth), then corporate income tax (around a tenth) and property taxes (a few percent). The exact shares vary widely by country. Region: OECD. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — OECD *Revenue Statistics* tax-structure tables, reproduced across OECD material and tax-policy explainers. **Flag:** give this as approximate shares and name the OECD as the source; the composition shifts year to year and country to country.

3. **Two different models: the US and the EU.** The United States has no federal value-added tax and leans heavily on personal income tax and payroll taxes; sales taxes are levied by states at relatively low rates. Most EU countries run high standard VAT rates — from 17% in Luxembourg to 27% in Hungary — that raise a large, stable share of revenue. This is the clearest illustration that countries at similar income levels choose very different tax structures. Region: United States / European Union. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — OECD *Revenue Statistics – United States* country note (no federal VAT; income/payroll heavy) and EU VAT-rate tables (European Commission / Tax Foundation), Hungary 27% being the EU's highest standard rate.

4. **A resource economy adding broad taxes: the UAE.** Gulf states historically funded themselves from oil revenue and levied almost no tax on residents. The United Arab Emirates introduced a 5% VAT in 2018 and a 9% federal corporate tax from June 2023 (on business profits above AED 375,000; 15% for large multinationals), while still charging zero personal income tax. It shows that a country's tax structure follows its revenue base — and changes when that base looks less secure. Region: Gulf (UAE). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — UAE Ministry of Finance announcement of the corporate tax (mof.gov.ae) and the Washington Institute ("The UAE Formally Ceases to be a Tax-Free Haven"); the 2018 VAT and 9% rate are consistently reported.

5. **The global minimum corporate tax ("Pillar Two").** Around 140 countries agreed in 2021 to a 15% minimum effective tax rate on the profits of multinational groups with annual revenue above EUR 750 million, so that shifting profit to a very-low-tax country no longer avoids tax — the shortfall is "topped up" elsewhere. The rules took effect from 1 January 2024 in the EU (22 of 27 member states by 2025), the UK, Japan and South Korea, among others; the OECD expected about 90% of in-scope multinationals to be covered by 2025. The United States moved to withdraw its support in January 2025, creating uncertainty. Region: global / OECD-G20. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — OECD "Minimum Tax Implementation Handbook (Pillar Two)" and Tax Foundation / A&O Shearman trackers, consistent on the 15% rate, the EUR 750 million threshold, the 2024 start and the 2025 US reversal.

6. **Japan's consumption tax: small steps, big politics.** Japan introduced a consumption tax at 3% in April 1989, then raised it to 5% (April 1997), 8% (April 2014) and 10% (October 2019, with a reduced 8% rate kept for food and newspapers). Each increase was politically damaging for the government that enacted it, yet the consumption tax is now Japan's single largest source of national tax revenue, needed to help pay rising social-security costs for an ageing population. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Nippon.com ("The Political History of Japan's Consumption Tax") and the Tokyo Foundation's consumption-tax history archive, plus Stripe's rate summary; the 1989/1997/2014/2019 dates and rates are uncontested.

7. **Japan's reliance on social-security contributions.** Because the working-age population is shrinking, Japan leans heavily on social-security (pension and health) contributions levied on wages, alongside the consumption tax, rather than raising income-tax rates. This narrows the effective tax base to people in work and adds to the cost of employing them. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — OECD *Revenue Statistics – Japan* country note (high social-security-contribution share) and Japan Ministry of Finance tax-structure material; the ageing-population framing is standard. **Note:** Japan's pension contribution rate (fixed at 18.3% of salary) is primary evidence in Book A 3.4 Pension Reform — reference the *reliance on contributions* here without re-using that specific figure.

8. **Who really pays (incidence).** The person or company that hands the tax to the government is often not the one who bears its economic cost. VAT is collected from businesses but paid by final consumers, and takes a larger share of a low earner's income than a high earner's (regressive on income, though roughly proportional to spending). Corporate income tax is legally paid by companies but its burden is shared, in disputed proportions, among shareholders, workers and customers. Region: general mechanics. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — standard public-finance treatment across OECD, IFS and Tax Policy Center material on tax incidence.

## The Japanese angle (mid-article, per the shape plan)

Japan's tax story is dominated by one instrument and one problem. The instrument is the consumption tax, introduced at 3% in 1989 and raised in three steps to 10% by October 2019 (with food kept at 8%). Every increase cost the governing party politically — two of the rises are associated with election setbacks — and yet the consumption tax is now Japan's largest single national tax, because it is the most reliable way to raise revenue from a population in which the share of retirees keeps growing. The problem is that an ageing society needs ever more spending on pensions and health care while the number of working-age taxpayers falls, so Japan also leans hard on social-security contributions levied on wages, rather than on higher income-tax rates. The result is a tax system that weighs relatively heavily on consumption and on employing workers, and still does not cover government spending — a large share of the budget is financed by borrowing, which is the subject of the next topic.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **tax-to-GDP ratio** — total tax revenue as a share of the size of the economy
- **tax base** — the set of income, transactions or property a tax is charged on
- **direct vs indirect tax** — a tax on income or wealth vs a tax on spending or transactions
- **value-added tax (VAT) / consumption tax** — a tax charged at each stage of production but ultimately borne by the final buyer
- **personal income tax** — tax on individuals' earnings, usually at progressive rates
- **social-security / payroll contributions** — compulsory payments on wages that fund pensions, health care and unemployment benefits
- **corporate income tax** — tax on company profits
- **progressive / regressive tax** — a tax taking a larger / smaller share of income as income rises
- **tax incidence** — who ultimately bears the economic cost of a tax, as opposed to who remits it
- **withholding** — collecting tax at source (an employer deducts income tax from pay before the worker receives it)
- **tax expenditure** — revenue a government forgoes through deductions, exemptions and reduced rates
- **base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS)** — multinationals moving reported profit to low-tax jurisdictions to cut their tax bill
- **global minimum tax ("Pillar Two")** — the agreed 15% minimum effective rate on large multinationals' profits
