## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Green finance is money raised specifically to fund environmentally beneficial projects — mainly through green bonds (a normal bond, but with the proceeds earmarked for things like renewable energy, clean transport, or energy efficiency, and reported on afterward) and related tools such as sustainability-linked loans/bonds, where the borrower's cost changes if it hits or misses a stated environmental target. Standard-setters (ICMA's Green Bond Principles) and regulators (the EU's Green Bond Standard, various national taxonomies) exist because "green" is otherwise just a label an issuer chooses for itself, which creates a real risk of greenwashing — claiming an environmental benefit that a label doesn't actually deliver.

1. **The world's first green bond was issued by the European Investment Bank (EIB) on 5 July 2007** — the "Climate Awareness Bond" (CAB), a roughly €600 million issue earmarking proceeds exclusively for renewable-energy and energy-efficiency projects, with reporting by investment rather than by sector, a first at the time. **The World Bank issued the first bond to actually carry the "green bond" label in November 2008**, in partnership with SEB, using the Norwegian research centre CICERO to give the market's first "second opinion" certifying the projects' greenness and building in impact reporting — the template that later became the industry norm. 2. **ICMA's Green Bond Principles (GBP), first published in 2014, are the market's voluntary reference standard** and rest on four pillars, unchanged in substance since 2014: (i) use of proceeds — funds go to defined green project categories; (ii) process for project evaluation and selection; (iii) management of proceeds — tracked separately from the issuer's general funds; (iv) reporting — on both allocation (where the money went) and impact (what it achieved). 3. **The EU's European Green Bond Standard (Regulation (EU) 2023/2631, the "EuGB") was published in the Official Journal on 30 November 2023, entered into force 20 December 2023, and has applied since 21 December 2024.** It is voluntary — an issuer may call a bond a "European Green Bond" only by meeting its rules — and requires that **at least 85% of proceeds fund activities aligned with the EU Taxonomy**, with a "flexibility pocket" allowing up to 15% for activities that contribute to the EU's environmental objectives but are not yet covered by finalised Taxonomy technical screening criteria; an ESMA-registered external reviewer must verify use of proceeds before and after issuance. It was explicitly designed to curb greenwashing by giving the market one gold-standard, legally defined label. Region: European Union. 9. **A concrete, well-documented greenwashing controversy: Enel's 2019 "SDG-linked bond" (a roughly €2.5 billion sustainability-linked bond from the Italian utility) was publicly called "greenwashing" by Nuveen, one of the largest green-bond investors.** The bond's coupon would step up by 25 basis points if Enel failed to reach 55% renewable generation capacity by end-2021 — no earmarked green projects, just a financial penalty tied to a company-wide target. A Nuveen representative argued that Enel had, in effect, "paid for an option on not delivering their renewables goals," rather than issuing a real green bond that funds direct transition projects; the case became a widely cited reference point for the broader debate over whether sustainability-linked structures (as opposed to use-of-proceeds green bonds) create real incentives or just a cost of borrowing. 10. **The core definitional distinction that separates a labelled green bond from an ordinary bond funding the same kind of project**: a green bond legally commits the issuer to (a) spend the proceeds only on defined green categories, (b) track that spending separately, and (c) report on it — none of which an ordinary bond requires, even if an ordinary bond happens to fund a wind farm or an EV factory. The green label is a promise about use of proceeds and transparency, not a difference in what is physically built. This rests on the same ICMA GBP four-pillar structure verified in anchor 2 above (no separate sourcing needed — it is a direct restatement of that structure, not a new factual claim).

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

Japan gave the world its first sovereign transition bond. In February 2024 the government sold two tranches of what it calls Japan Climate Transition Bonds — a JPY 800 billion 10-year tranche on 14 February, followed by a JPY 800 billion 5-year tranche on 27 February, JPY 1.6 trillion combined in that first month — the opening instalment of a programme designed to raise roughly JPY 20 trillion over about ten years for the country's "green transformation" (GX) strategy, decarbonising heavy industry, energy, and transport while keeping Japan globally competitive. What makes the design distinctive is how the bonds get repaid: rather than relying on general tax revenue, Japan plans to fund redemption from carbon-pricing income collected outside the normal budget process — a fossil-fuel levy on importers and domestic extractors starting in FY2028, and Japan's emissions trading scheme, which becomes mandatory for large emitters from FY2026 though full allowance auctioning for power-sector firms does not begin until FY2033 — so the sectors being regulated are, over time, also expected to help finance the transition bonds that fund the shift away from fossil fuels. On the disclosure side, Japan's Ministry of the Environment has kept its domestic Green Bond Guidelines (first issued March 2017, updated 2020 and 2022) aligned with ICMA's international principles, and the Tokyo Stock Exchange has run a dedicated platform since January 2018 where bond issuers can voluntarily post their use-of-proceeds statements and post-issuance reporting for investors to check. Together, the GX transition bonds and the Ministry of the Environment/TSE framework put Japan among the more developed sovereign markets for this kind of finance, even though — as elsewhere — most green finance in Japan is still issued by corporations and public agencies rather than the sovereign itself.
