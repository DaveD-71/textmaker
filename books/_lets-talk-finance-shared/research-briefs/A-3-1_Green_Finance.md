## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Green finance is money raised specifically to fund environmentally beneficial projects — mainly through green bonds (a normal bond, but with the proceeds earmarked for things like renewable energy, clean transport, or energy efficiency, and reported on afterward) and related tools such as sustainability-linked loans/bonds, where the borrower's cost changes if it hits or misses a stated environmental target. Standard-setters (ICMA's Green Bond Principles) and regulators (the EU's Green Bond Standard, various national taxonomies) exist because "green" is otherwise just a label an issuer chooses for itself, which creates a real risk of greenwashing — claiming an environmental benefit that a label doesn't actually deliver.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The world's first green bond was issued by the European Investment Bank (EIB) on 5 July 2007** — the "Climate Awareness Bond," funding renewable-energy and energy-efficiency projects. The World Bank issued the first bond to actually carry the "green bond" label in 2008, using an outside reviewer (CICERO) to certify the projects and reporting on impact afterward — the template later formalised industry-wide. Region: global / EU origin. Source: European Investment Bank, "15 years of EIB green bonds" — https://www.eib.org/en/press/all/2022-308-15-years-of-eib-green-bonds-leading-sustainable-investment-from-niche-to-mainstream [VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET, exact date and description matched by two independent search results].

2. **ICMA's Green Bond Principles (GBP), first published in 2014, are the market's voluntary standard-setter reference** and rest on four pillars: (i) use of proceeds — proceeds must go to defined green project categories; (ii) process for project evaluation and selection; (iii) management of proceeds — tracked separately from the issuer's general funds; (iv) reporting — on both allocation (where the money went) and impact (what it achieved). Updated most recently in June 2025. Region: global standard-setter. Source: International Capital Market Association (ICMA), Green Bond Principles page — https://www.icmagroup.org/sustainable-finance/the-principles-guidelines-and-handbooks/green-bond-principles-gbp/ [VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET, four-pillar structure and 2014 origin corroborated by a second independent source, ACT Wiki treasurers.org].

3. **The EU's European Green Bond Standard (Regulation (EU) 2023/2631, the "EuGB") entered into force 20 December 2023 and has applied since 21 December 2024.** It is voluntary — issuers can call a bond an "EuGB"/"European Green Bond" only if they meet its rules — and requires that (with up to 15% flexibility for activities not yet formally covered) 100% of proceeds fund activities aligned with the EU Taxonomy, verified before issuance and again after full allocation by an external reviewer registered with the EU securities regulator, ESMA. It was explicitly designed to curb greenwashing by giving the market one gold-standard label. Region: European Union. Source: EUR-Lex, "European green bond standard" summary of Regulation (EU) 2023/2631 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/european-green-bond-standard.html [VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET, dates and 15%-flexibility figure corroborated across three independent search results, including a German regulator (BaFin) explainer].

4. **Global cumulative green bond issuance had reached about US$3.8 trillion by mid-2025**, and the wider "GSS+" sustainable debt market (green, social, sustainability, and sustainability-linked bonds combined) stood at roughly US$6.2–8.1 trillion depending on which Climate Bonds Initiative report vintage is used (figures rose through 2025 as more issuance was recorded). Annual aligned GSS+ issuance topped US$1 trillion for a third straight year in 2025. Region: global. Source: Climate Bonds Initiative, "Sustainable Debt Global State of the Market" series (2025 editions) and press releases — https://www.climatebonds.net/data-insights/publications/sustainable-debt-global-state-market-2025 [VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET; see Notes/cautions — this figure moves between report vintages and needs one final pinned number before drafting].

5. **Japan issued the world's first sovereign climate transition bonds on 14 February 2024** — two tranches of JPY 800 billion each (5-year and 10-year), totalling JPY 1.6 trillion (about US$10–11 billion at the time), under the government's "GX Economy Transition Bond" programme. The full programme targets JPY 20 trillion in issuance over roughly ten years (from FY2023), intended to crowd in over JPY 150 trillion of combined public and private "green transformation" (GX) investment. Region: Japan. Source: Japan Ministry of Finance, "Japan Climate Transition Bonds" — https://www.mof.go.jp/english/policy/jgbs/topics/JapanClimateTransitionBonds/index.html [VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET, issuance date/amount/tranche structure corroborated by three independent search results including a specialist climate-finance outlet].

6. **Repayment of Japan's GX transition bonds is designed to come from carbon-pricing revenue, not general taxes**: income from an emissions-trading scheme (auctioning from FY2026 under the GX-ETS) and a fossil-fuel levy on importers (from FY2028) is earmarked to redeem the bonds. This "pay for the transition, then have the transition pay you back" repayment design is a genuinely distinctive feature versus other sovereigns' green/transition bonds. Region: Japan. Source: Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) transition-finance materials, corroborated by UNFCCC secretariat presentation "Overview of Japan's GX Policy and Carbon Pricing" (April 2026) — https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/Session_02_01_Japan_GX-ETS.pdf [VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET].

7. **Japan's Ministry of the Environment published Green Bond Guidelines in March 2017**, updated them in 2020 (widened to cover green loans and sustainability-linked loans), and issued combined Green Bond and Sustainability-Linked Bond Guidelines in July 2022, keeping Japan's domestic rules aligned with ICMA's international principles. Separately, the Tokyo Stock Exchange (via Japan Exchange Group) launched a dedicated disclosure platform for green and social bonds listed on its TOKYO PRO-BOND Market on 22 January 2018 — issuers voluntarily post use-of-proceeds statements, external reviews, and post-issuance reporting in one place. Region: Japan. Sources: Japan's Green Finance Portal (Ministry of the Environment), "Domestic Policy Related to Green Bonds Issuance" — https://greenfinanceportal.env.go.jp/en/bond/related_info/related_policies.html ; Japan Exchange Group, "Outline of the Platform," ESG Bonds (TPBM) — https://www.jpx.co.jp/english/equities/products/tpbm/green-and-social-bonds/index.html [both VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET].

8. **Chile issued Latin America's first sovereign green bond in 2019** — a US$1,418 million 2050-maturity dollar tranche plus an €861 million euro tranche, part of a US$2.5 billion-equivalent placement; the dollar tranche alone drew roughly US$6.7 billion of orders, about 12.8 times the amount on offer. Chile went on to issue the world's first sovereign sustainability-linked bond in 2022, and by late 2025 was the largest emerging-market sovereign issuer of labelled sustainable debt (green, social, sustainability, and sustainability-linked combined), with about US$56 billion outstanding — ahead of Mexico's roughly US$29 billion. Region: Latin America / emerging markets. Sources: Climate Bonds Initiative blog, "Chile: First Sovereign Green Bond in the Americas" — https://www.climatebonds.net/news-events/blog/chile-first-sovereign-green-bond-americas-strong-investor-demand-come-says-finance-minister-larra%C3%ADn ; World Bank Treasury, "Labeled Sustainable Bonds – Market Update," Q4 2025 — https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/0cbf29fe7e18a8d8a64b67ad2d0a6a7c-0340012026/original/WBG-TRE-Labeled-Sustainable-Bonds-Market-Update-2025-Q4.pdf [both VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET].

9. **A concrete greenwashing-risk case: Greece's Public Power Corporation (PPC) missed its own 2022 sustainability-linked bond target** (a 40% cut in greenhouse-gas emissions from a 2019 baseline, largely derailed by the energy shock from the Russia–Ukraine war), triggering the bond's contractual penalty — a 25-basis-point step-up in the coupon rate from March 2023. This is the mechanism sustainability-linked bonds use instead of "use of proceeds" rules: investors are paid more if the issuer fails its own climate target, which critics (including europe's securities regulator, ESMA) say can let an issuer treat the penalty as just a cost of borrowing rather than a real incentive to change behaviour, especially since most such bonds are callable before the target date. Region: Europe (Greece), general SLB-market context. Source: coverage aggregated in S&P Global Market Intelligence and academic literature on SLB "structural loopholes"; original PPC step-up reported by Environmental Finance / trade press. [VERIFIED VIA SEARCH SNIPPET; the underlying PPC bond terms and step-up should be traced to PPC's own investor-relations disclosure or a named news outlet before drafting — see Notes/cautions.]

10. **The core definitional distinction that separates a labelled green bond from an ordinary bond funding the same kind of project**: a green bond legally commits the issuer to (a) spend the proceeds only on defined green categories, (b) track that spending separately, and (c) report on it — none of which an ordinary bond requires, even if an ordinary bond happens to fund a wind farm or an EV factory. The green label is a promise about use of proceeds and transparency, not a difference in what is physically built. Region: conceptual, applies globally. Source: same ICMA GBP four-pillar structure as anchor 2.

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

Japan gave the world its first sovereign transition bond. On 14 February 2024 the government sold two tranches of what it calls Japan Climate Transition Bonds — JPY 800 billion each, one due in five years and one in ten, JPY 1.6 trillion in total — the opening instalment of a programme designed to raise JPY 20 trillion over about ten years for the country's "green transformation" (GX) strategy, decarbonising heavy industry, energy, and transport while keeping Japan globally competitive. What makes the design distinctive is how the bonds get repaid: rather than relying on general tax revenue, Japan plans to fund redemption from carbon-pricing income — an emissions-trading scheme that begins auctioning permits in FY2026, and a levy on fossil-fuel importers starting FY2028 — so the polluters being regulated are, in effect, also financing the transition bonds that help pay for the shift away from fossil fuels. On the disclosure side, Japan's Ministry of the Environment has kept its domestic Green Bond Guidelines (first issued March 2017, updated 2020 and 2022) aligned with ICMA's international principles, and the Tokyo Stock Exchange runs a dedicated platform, launched in January 2018, where bond issuers can voluntarily post their use-of-proceeds statements and post-issuance reporting for investors to check. Together, the GX transition bonds and the TSE/Ministry of the Environment framework put Japan among the more developed sovereign markets for this kind of finance, even though — as elsewhere — most green finance in Japan is still issued by corporations and public agencies rather than the sovereign itself. Verifiable facts + URLs: Japan MOF, "Japan Climate Transition Bonds" — https://www.mof.go.jp/english/policy/jgbs/topics/JapanClimateTransitionBonds/index.html ; Japan Green Finance Portal, domestic green bond policy — https://greenfinanceportal.env.go.jp/en/bond/related_info/related_policies.html ; JPX ESG Bond Platform outline — https://www.jpx.co.jp/english/equities/products/tpbm/green-and-social-bonds/index.html ; UNFCCC secretariat, GX-ETS/carbon-pricing overview (April 2026) — https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/Session_02_01_Japan_GX-ETS.pdf
