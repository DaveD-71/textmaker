# Research Brief — Book A 4.3 Wealth Inequality and Redistribution Policies

Prepared 2026-09-11 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts checked against at least two independent sources; where an OECD/central-bank page blocked automated fetch, secondary and data-aggregator co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Wealth inequality is the gap in what people *own* — property, savings, businesses, pensions — which is far wider than the gap in what they *earn*, because wealth compounds and passes between generations. Governments try to narrow it with a redistribution toolkit: progressive income tax, taxes on capital gains and inheritance, cash transfers and public services, and, more rarely, an annual tax on net wealth itself. The topic is how inequality is measured, which tools actually move the numbers, and why some tools — annual wealth taxes especially — have mostly been tried and abandoned, even as a new push to tax the very richest is back on the international agenda.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **Global wealth concentration (the opening comparison).** The World Inequality Report 2022 found the richest 10% of the world's adults own about 76% of all household wealth, while the poorest 50% own about 2%. In average terms the bottom half holds roughly US$4,100 per adult against about US$771,300 for the top 10%. Wealth is distributed far more unequally than income. Region: global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — World Inequality Report 2022 executive summary (wir2022.wid.world) and Oxfam's and the European Commission's summaries of it, all giving the 76% / 2% split.

2. **A rich-country example: the United States.** Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts data show the wealthiest 1% of US households owned about 30.9% of roughly US$162 trillion in total household wealth in 2024, while the bottom 50% owned about 2.5% — down from around 3.4% in 1989. Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Federal Reserve DFA data as presented by Visual Capitalist ("U.S. Wealth Held by the Bottom 50%, 1989-2024") and the St. Louis Fed's "State of U.S. Household Wealth"; the FRED series WFRBST01134 is the underlying source.

3. **What redistribution actually does to the numbers.** Inequality is usually summarised by the Gini coefficient (0 = everyone equal, 1 = one person owns everything). Across OECD countries, taxes and cash transfers cut the Gini of working-age household income by roughly a quarter to a third on average — with high-transfer systems such as France, Belgium and the Nordics reducing it most, and the United States, South Korea and Chile reducing it least. Redistribution works, but by very different amounts depending on policy design. Region: OECD. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — OECD Income Distribution Database framing (market-income vs disposable-income Gini), widely reproduced across OECD "Society at a Glance" material and secondary analyses. **Flag:** the exact "quarter to a third" is a round summary of a range that differs by country and year — attribute to the OECD and don't over-specify.

4. **Annual wealth taxes: mostly tried and dropped.** The number of OECD countries levying an annual tax on individuals' net wealth fell from 12 in 1990 to just three by 2020 — Norway, Spain and Switzerland. Common reasons for repeal: they raised little revenue, were hard to value and enforce, and prompted wealthy residents to leave or restructure. Switzerland's is the durable exception — long-established, levied by the cantons, and raising a meaningful share of revenue. Region: OECD / Europe. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — OECD Tax Policy Studies, "The Role and Design of Net Wealth Taxes in the OECD" (2018, oecd.org) and Tax Foundation / Economics Observatory summaries, all giving the "12 in 1990 → 3" figure.

5. **The new international push: a minimum tax on billionaires.** For its 2024 G20 presidency Brazil commissioned economist Gabriel Zucman to design a coordinated minimum tax on the ultra-rich. His blueprint proposes that individuals worth more than US$1 billion pay at least 2% of their wealth in tax each year; applied to the roughly 3,000 known billionaires it would raise an estimated US$200-250 billion a year. Zucman's report estimates billionaires currently pay an effective rate equivalent to about 0.3% of their wealth. In July 2024, G20 finance ministers issued a joint declaration — a first — to cooperate on taxing ultra-high-net-worth individuals, though it is non-binding and the US and Germany were among the holdouts. Region: global / Brazil (G20). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Zucman's "A blueprint for a coordinated minimum effective taxation standard for ultra-high-net-worth individuals" (gabriel-zucman.eu) and NPR / ICIJ coverage of the G20 declaration and the 2% / US$200-250 billion / 0.3% figures.

6. **A high-inequality case: Brazil.** Brazil has long had one of the world's most concentrated income and wealth distributions; its 2023-24 tax changes moved to tax Brazilian residents' offshore investment funds and trusts and closed loopholes used by the wealthy, and it was Brazil's own high inequality that drove Lula's government to make taxing the ultra-rich the centrepiece of its G20 agenda. Region: Latin America (Brazil). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — coverage of Brazil's 2023 offshore-fund taxation law (Reuters, Gulf News) alongside the G20 material in anchor 5; Brazil's ranking among the most unequal large economies is standard in World Inequality Lab and World Bank data.

7. **Japan: low income inequality, but a tax "notch" at the top.** Japan's disposable-income inequality is moderate by rich-country standards, but its effective income-tax rate actually *falls* for the very highest earners, because financial income (capital gains and dividends) is taxed at a flat rate of about 20% while employment income is taxed progressively up to about 45%. The effect — nicknamed the "¥100 million wall" — is that people earning well above ¥100 million a year, who take more of their income as investment returns, can face a lower average rate than people earning somewhat less. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Japan Ministry of Finance PRI review "New Developments in the Taxation of Financial Income and Wealthy Individuals" (mof.go.jp) and Daiwa Institute of Research / PwC tax summaries describing the flat ~20% financial-income rate against progressive employment rates.

8. **Japan's response: a minimum tax on top earners.** From 2025, Japanese residents whose total annual income exceeds ¥330 million pay a minimum tax equal to 22.5% of the amount above that threshold (payable only where it exceeds their normal income-tax bill). It affects an estimated 200-300 people a year and raises perhaps ¥30-60 billion. From 2027 the threshold falls to ¥165 million and the rate rises to 30%. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Daiwa Institute of Research ("Minimum Tax of 22.5% on Top-Income Earners Introduced") and Grant Thornton Japan / Deloitte "Japan Highlights 2025," which give the ¥330 million / 22.5% design and the 2027 tightening to ¥165 million / 30%.

## The Japanese angle (mid-article, per the shape plan)

Japan is not among the most unequal rich countries on income, but its tax system has a well-known quirk at the very top. Employment income is taxed progressively, up to a combined national-and-local rate of roughly 45-55%, but income from selling shares or receiving dividends is taxed at a flat rate of about 20%. Because the highest earners take a larger share of their income as investment returns, the *average* tax rate can start falling once annual income climbs past about ¥100 million — the so-called "¥100 million wall." The government's answer, phased in from 2025, is a minimum tax: residents with total income above ¥330 million must pay at least 22.5% of the excess, with the threshold dropping to ¥165 million and the rate rising to 30% from 2027. It is a narrow measure — a few hundred taxpayers, tens of billions of yen — but it illustrates the topic's central difficulty: taxing the wealthy is less about headline rates than about how different kinds of income and assets are treated, and the wealthiest hold the kinds that are taxed most lightly.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **wealth (net worth)** — the value of what a person owns minus what they owe
- **wealth inequality vs income inequality** — the gap in assets held vs the gap in money earned in a year
- **Gini coefficient** — a single number from 0 to 1 summarising how unequally income or wealth is distributed
- **top 1% / bottom 50% share** — the fraction of total wealth or income held by the richest 1% / poorest half
- **progressive / regressive tax** — a tax that takes a larger / smaller share of income as income rises
- **redistribution** — using taxes and transfers to shift resources from higher-income to lower-income households
- **transfer payment** — a government payment to households (pensions, child benefit, unemployment benefit) not in exchange for goods or services
- **capital gains tax** — tax on the profit from selling an asset such as shares or property
- **inheritance / estate tax** — tax on wealth passed on at death
- **net wealth tax** — an annual tax on the total value of a person's assets above a threshold
- **effective tax rate** — the share of income (or wealth) actually paid in tax, after all deductions and preferential rates
- **capital flight** — wealthy individuals or their money leaving a jurisdiction to avoid tax
- **ultra-high-net-worth individual (UHNWI)** — a person with very large wealth (commonly US$30 million+, or "billionaire" for the G20 proposal)
