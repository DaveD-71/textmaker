## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

How the price of an asset — tulip bulbs, tech stocks, real estate, a cryptocurrency — can rise far beyond any reasonable estimate of its worth, and then collapse quickly once buyers stop believing the price will keep rising. The same ingredients recur across centuries: cheap or easy credit (leverage), herd behaviour (buying because everyone else is buying), and "this time is different" thinking that dismisses the lessons of earlier crashes. The aftermath — how long a market or an economy takes to recover — often matters more than the crash itself.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **Tulip mania, Netherlands, 1636–37.** Contract prices for the rarest Dutch tulip bulbs reached extraordinary levels in the Dutch Golden Age — by one account the most prized bulbs traded for roughly the price of a house on an Amsterdam canal — before collapsing at a bulb auction in Haarlem in the first week of February 1637, when bidders stopped showing up even as the asking price was repeatedly lowered. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Federal Reserve Bank of New York, "Crisis Chronicles: Tulip Mania, 1633–37," Liberty Street Economics blog; corroborated by History.com, "The Real Story Behind the 17th-Century 'Tulip Mania'," and the University of Oxford Faculty of History. **Flag: this is historical scholarship on a pre-modern, pre-regulator event, not contemporary reporting — treat exact guilder figures as illustrative, not precise.**

2. **Wall Street crash of 1929 (US).** The Dow Jones Industrial Average peaked at 381 in September 1929, fell through Black Thursday (24 Oct) and Black Tuesday (29 Oct), and kept falling until it bottomed at 41.22 in July 1932 — a peak-to-trough decline of about 89%. The Dow did not return to its 1929 peak until November 1954, roughly 25 years later. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — "Stock Market Crash of 1929," Federal Reserve History (federalreservehistory.org, a Federal Reserve System publication); corroborated by History.com, "Stock Market Crash of 1929."

3. **Dot-com crash, US, 2000–2002.** The Nasdaq Composite index peaked at 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000, then fell roughly 78% to about 1,114–1,140 by October 2002, wiping out an estimated US$5 trillion in market value; the index did not reclaim its March 2000 high until April 2015 — 15 years later. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Wikipedia "Dot-com bubble" (aggregating primary market data) and Goldman Sachs, "The Late 1990s Dot-Com Bubble Implodes in 2000" (goldmansachs.com/our-firm/history); figures consistent across both.

4. **Japan — Nikkei 225 peak, 29 December 1989.** Japan's Nikkei 225 stock index touched an intraday high of 38,957.44 and closed at 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989, the last trading day of the decade, at the height of Japan's "bubble economy." It took 34 years and roughly 2 months for the index to reclaim that closing level, finally doing so in February 2024. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Nippon.com, "Nikkei Index Sets First Record High Since 1989"; corroborated by Yahoo Finance/Reuters wire coverage of the February 2024 record ("Japan's Nikkei stock index breaks its 1989 record and surges to an all-time high") and Business Standard's syndication of the same Reuters report.

5. **Japan — commercial land prices, six major cities.** According to the Japan Real Estate Institute, an index of commercial land prices across six major Japanese cities (central Tokyo's 23 wards, Yokohama, Nagoya, Kyoto, Osaka and Kobe) fell by about 87% from its peak; the price of commercial land kept falling for 15 years after the 1990–91 peak, not turning up again until 2005. A separate, broader estimate puts the fall in Japan's urban land prices generally at over 80% between 1991 and 2002. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS — but see caution below]** — Nippon.com, "Heisei Blues: The Post-Bubble Struggles of Japan's Financial Sector" (citing Japan Real Estate Institute data), corroborated directionally by multiple independent retrospectives citing "over 80% between 1991 and 2002." **Flag: the 87% (six-city commercial) and 80%+ (broader urban, 1991–2002) figures describe overlapping but not identical measures — see Notes/cautions.**

6. **GameStop "meme stock" short squeeze, US, January 2021.** GameStop (GME) shares rose from about US$17.25 on 4 January 2021 to an intraday high of US$483 on 28 January 2021 — a rise of well over 1,000% in under a month — driven by coordinated retail buying organised on the Reddit forum r/WallStreetBets against hedge funds that had bet the price would fall (a "short squeeze"). By 29 January 2021, short-sellers had lost an estimated nearly US$20 billion on the position. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — CNBC, "GameStop jumps another 90% above $140, but short sellers aren't backing down" and CNBC, "GameStop short sellers are still not surrendering despite nearly $20 billion in losses this month" (both cnbc.com, January 2021); corroborated by the Cato Institute's Cato Journal essay "The GameStop Episode: What Happened and What Does It Mean?"

7. **Terra/Luna crypto collapse, May 2022.** The cryptocurrency LUNA and its linked "algorithmic" stablecoin UST (designed to track US$1 without holding real dollar reserves) collapsed within days in May 2022: LUNA fell from about US$87 on 5 May 2022 to a small fraction of a cent by 13 May 2022, and UST broke its dollar peg. More than US$40 billion in combined LUNA/UST market value was wiped out. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond, "Why Stablecoins Fail: An Economist's Post-Mortem on Terra" (Economic Brief, richmondfed.org); corroborated by Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance, "Anatomy of a Run: The Terra Luna Crash" (corpgov.law.harvard.edu).

8. **"This time is different."** The phrase is the title of a well-known 2009 academic history of financial crises by economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff, *This Time Is Different: Eight Centuries of Financial Folly* (Princeton University Press), which studied crises across 66 countries and eight centuries and found the same belief — that old warning signs no longer apply — recurring before nearly every one. Useful as a named, citable source for the "this time is different" idea rather than a floating cliché. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Princeton University Press book page (press.princeton.edu) and Harvard University's Kenneth Rogoff faculty publication page (rogoff.scholars.harvard.edu).

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

Japan's late-1980s "bubble economy" is one of the best-documented asset bubbles anywhere, and gives this topic real numbers rather than vague description. The Nikkei 225 stock index closed at 38,915.87 points on 29 December 1989 — the peak of a rally fuelled by cheap credit, rising land values used as loan collateral, and a widespread belief that Japanese asset prices would keep climbing. It took 34 years for the index to reclaim that level, finally doing so in February 2024. The property side of the bubble was, if anything, more extreme: the Japan Real Estate Institute's index of commercial land prices in six major cities (Tokyo, Yokohama, Nagoya, Kyoto, Osaka, Kobe) fell by roughly 87% from its peak, and prices kept falling for 15 years, not turning up again until 2005. The aftermath is often called Japan's "lost decade," although economists now generally agree it ran closer to two decades: banks were left holding huge volumes of bad loans secured against real estate that had lost most of its value, and the wider economy grew only slowly through the 1990s and 2000s. For a Japanese reader, the lesson is close to home rather than a foreign case study: it shows how long a bubble's aftermath can last, and why the Bank of Japan spent decades afterward focused on getting mild, steady inflation back rather than fighting it.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **asset bubble** — a rapid rise in an asset's price well beyond its underlying value
- **speculative mania** — a period when buying is driven by expected further price rises, not underlying value
- **herd behaviour** — investors following what others are doing rather than independent judgement
- **leverage** — using borrowed money to buy an asset, which multiplies both gains and losses
- **irrational exuberance** — excessive investor optimism that pushes prices beyond reasonable value (coined by Alan Greenspan, 1996)
- **"this time is different" thinking** — the recurring belief that old warning signs of a bubble no longer apply
- **bubble burst / pop** — the sudden, rapid fall in price once buyers stop believing in further gains
- **crash** — a sudden, sharp fall in asset prices across a market
- **correction** — a smaller, more routine price fall (commonly defined as a 10%+ drop), distinct from a crash
- **peak-to-trough decline** — the percentage fall from an asset's highest point to its lowest point afterward
- **short squeeze** — a rapid price rise that forces investors who bet on a fall (short-sellers) to buy back at a loss
- **algorithmic stablecoin** — a cryptocurrency designed to hold a stable value through an automated formula rather than by holding real reserves
- **market capitalisation** — the total value of all outstanding units of a stock or cryptocurrency
- **contagion** — the spread of a financial shock from one market or asset to others
- **bull market / bear market** — a sustained period of rising prices / sustained period of falling prices
