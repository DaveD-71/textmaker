# Research Brief — Book B 4.5 The Future of Money

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

"Money" has changed form many times — from coins, to paper notes, to numbers in a bank's computer — and it is changing again. Physical cash is fading fast in some countries and holding firm in others; most money is already private and digital (a bank deposit, not a banknote); and new forms are emerging on top of that: stablecoins issued by private companies, "tokenised" deposits that banks can move instantly, and central-bank digital currencies still mostly at the pilot stage. The topic is a short history of money's changing form and a sober look at what is actually arriving next — including a recent, striking reversal in the country that went furthest toward cashlessness.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The opening comparison: Sweden vs the cash-attached economies.** In Sweden, the share of people who had used cash for their most recent purchase fell from about 40% in 2010 to about 8% in 2022, and only around one in ten in-store purchases is now made with cash. In Germany and Japan, by contrast, cash is still used for a large share of everyday retail purchases. Same decade, opposite trajectories. Region: Sweden / Germany / Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Sveriges Riksbank "Payments Report 2024/2025" (cash-use figures) and Cash Essentials / GIS Reports coverage; the German and Japanese contrast is standard in Bundesbank and Bank of Japan payment surveys.

2. **Sweden reverses course.** Having led the world toward cashlessness, Sweden is now legislating to protect cash. In November 2024 the Swedish authorities sent every household a brochure ("If Crisis or War Comes") advising people to keep at least a week's worth of cash — a baseline of around SEK 1,000 (about US$100) per adult — and the Riksbank has backed a proposed legal obligation for shops selling essential goods to accept cash and for banks to maintain cash-handling services. Cash is now framed as civil-defence infrastructure and a resilience backstop, not a relic. Region: Sweden. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Cash Essentials ("Sweden Steps Up Efforts to Reverse the Decline in Cash Usage") and Cash Matters / Malay Mail-AFP, consistent on the household brochure, the ~SEK 1,000 advice and the accept-cash legislative proposal.

3. **The long chronology (structural anchor).** Money's form has evolved in stages: commodity money → minted coins → paper notes (first backed by gold or silver, later "fiat" backed only by trust in the state) → book-entry bank deposits → payment cards → mobile and QR-code payments → and now stablecoins, tokenised deposits and central-bank digital currencies. Each step kept the unit of account (the pound, the yen) but changed how value is stored and moved. Region: general history. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — standard monetary history across Bank of England, IMF and BIS explanatory material.

4. **Most money is already private and digital.** In modern economies the large majority of the money supply is not central-bank cash but commercial-bank deposits — money created when banks lend, existing only as ledger entries. Physical cash is a small and, in most rich countries, shrinking share. So "the future of money" is largely a question about what backs and moves those deposits, not about notes and coins. Region: general / advanced economies. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Bank of England "Money creation in the modern economy" and equivalent Fed/ECB material, widely cited for the "most money is bank deposits" point.

5. **Stablecoins have scaled — but mostly for trading so far.** Privately issued tokens pegged to a national currency (over 90% to the US dollar) reached a combined market value of about US$250 billion by mid-2025, dominated by Tether (about US$173 billion) and USD Coin (about US$74 billion). Reported transfer volume hit roughly US$33 trillion in 2025, though a large share of that is automated trading rather than payments. Their real-world use is growing for cross-border transfers and for dollar access in high-inflation economies. Region: global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Bloomberg ("Stablecoin Transactions Rose to Record $33 Trillion in 2025") and CoinLedger / Reap market-share data, consistent on the ~US$250 billion supply, the USDT/USDC split and the ~90%+ USD peg. **Flag:** the transfer-volume figure overstates payment use — say so.

6. **Tokenised deposits and "unified ledgers."** Banks and central banks are piloting putting deposits and securities on shared programmable ledgers so that payment and settlement happen together, instantly, around the clock — for example the BIS's cross-border experiments and large banks' internal deposit tokens already moving billions of dollars a day between corporate accounts. The aim is to modernise the plumbing of existing money rather than replace it. Region: global (BIS / major banks). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — BIS work on tokenisation and the "unified ledger" concept and reporting on bank deposit-token services (JPMorgan and others); described consistently across BIS speeches and financial press. **Flag:** keep this high-level; specific project names and volumes move quickly.

7. **The infrastructure of cash is thinning.** Even where people still want cash, the means to get it is shrinking: the UK lost roughly half its bank branches over the decade to the mid-2020s, and ATM numbers are falling across advanced economies. This makes cash harder to use, accelerates the shift, and concentrates the cost on older, rural and lower-income people — the reason regulators in several countries now legislate to guarantee cash access. Region: United Kingdom / advanced economies. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — UK branch-closure tallies (Which?, BBC) and central-bank "access to cash" reviews (Bank of England, Riksbank), consistent on the ~50% UK branch loss and falling ATM counts.

8. **Japan: the instructive laggard.** Despite rapid growth in QR-code and contactless payments, Japan's demand for physical currency is around 19-20% of GDP — the highest of any country the Bank for International Settlements tracks — and cash is still used in more than 40% of retail transactions. The reasons: near-zero interest rates mean holding cash costs nothing, an ageing population prefers tangible money, crime is low and trust is high. Japan issued its first redesigned banknotes in 20 years on 3 July 2024, and total cash in circulation (around JPY 129 trillion in 2023) only recently began to edge down. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — BIS currency-demand data as reported (Japan ~19% of GDP, "highest of any country" tracked) and Bank of Japan material on the July 2024 new banknote series and the first decline in cash in circulation in a decade.

## The Japanese angle (lead-ish or mid-article, per the shape plan)

Japan is the useful counter-example to the "cash is dying" story. QR-code wallets and contactless cards have spread quickly, and yet Japan's demand for physical currency is around 19-20% of GDP — the highest of any economy the Bank for International Settlements measures — and notes and coins are still used in more than four in ten retail purchases. Several things hold cash in place: with interest rates near zero for decades, there has been no cost to keeping money as cash rather than in a bank; the population is old and used to tangible money; crime is low and counterfeiting rare, so cash feels safe; and small shops long preferred it to card fees. Japan issued its first redesigned banknotes in twenty years in July 2024, and only very recently has the total amount of cash in circulation started to fall. Japan shows that whether a society goes cashless is not just a matter of technology being available — it depends on interest rates, demographics, trust and habit, which is why the same decade produced near-cashless Sweden and cash-heavy Japan.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **cash / physical currency** — notes and coins issued by a central bank
- **fiat money** — money that has value because a government declares it legal tender and people trust it, not because it is backed by a commodity
- **legal tender** — money that must be accepted to settle a debt
- **bank deposit (commercial-bank money)** — money held as a claim on a bank; most of the money supply
- **money supply** — the total stock of money in an economy (cash plus deposits, on various measures)
- **cashless society** — an economy where almost all payments are electronic
- **stablecoin** — a privately issued digital token designed to hold a fixed value against a currency
- **tokenisation** — representing money or an asset as a transferable digital record on a shared ledger
- **tokenised deposit** — a bank deposit issued in token form so it can be moved instantly and programmatically
- **central-bank digital currency (CBDC)** — a digital form of central-bank money for public use, mostly still at pilot stage
- **unified / programmable ledger** — a shared platform where money and assets can be exchanged and settled together
- **access to cash** — the availability of the branches, ATMs and services needed to obtain and deposit cash
- **unit of account** — the standard in which prices are quoted (the yen, the pound), unchanged even as money's form changes
