# Research Brief — Book A 4.2 Financial Literacy Programs

Prepared 2026-09-11 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts checked against at least two independent sources; where an OECD/government page blocked automated fetch, secondary and academic co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Financial literacy programs are organised efforts — school courses, adult workshops, national strategies, mass-media campaigns, one-to-one guidance — to raise people's ability to understand money and make sound financial decisions. Governments run or coordinate them because surveys keep finding large gaps in basic knowledge (inflation, interest, risk-spreading) that track with worse outcomes: costly debt, no emergency savings, falling for scams. The live debate is not whether the gaps exist but whether *teaching* closes them — early studies found weak, fading effects, while a large recent review finds programs do change behaviour when they are well designed and delivered near a real financial decision. This is a policy/programme topic, not a personal how-to guide.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **How financial literacy is measured internationally: the OECD/INFE survey.** The OECD's International Network on Financial Education runs a standardised survey; its 2023 round covered 39 countries and economies (20 of them OECD members) and produced an average financial-literacy score of 60 out of 100 (63 for OECD countries), combining questions on knowledge, behaviour and attitudes. Region: global / OECD. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — OECD "OECD/INFE 2023 International Survey of Adult Financial Literacy" (oecd.org) and national-bank summaries of it (Austria's OeNB, Luxembourg's letzfin), all giving the 39-economy scope and the 60/100 (63 OECD) averages.

2. **The classic "three questions."** The most-used quick measure, developed by economists Annamaria Lusardi and Olivia Mitchell, is three questions on compound interest, inflation and risk diversification (the "Big Three"). In the United States only about a third of adults answer all three correctly, and scores are lower for women, younger and lower-income respondents — a pattern repeated across many countries. Region: United States / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Lusardi-Mitchell work summarised by the Global Financial Literacy Excellence Center (GFLEC) and the FINRA Investor Education Foundation's National Financial Capability Study; the "~one-third answer all three" finding is widely reported.

3. **The skeptical evidence (case one of the comparison).** A widely cited 2014 meta-analysis by Daniel Fernandes, John Lynch and Richard Netemeyer found that financial-education interventions explained only about 0.1% of the variation in later financial behaviour, and that effects faded within roughly 20 months — feeding an argument that "just-in-time" guidance and better default rules might beat classroom teaching. Region: global (research). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer, "Financial Literacy, Financial Education, and Downstream Financial Behaviors," *Management Science* (2014), summarised across academic and policy literature.

4. **The more positive recent evidence (case two).** A 2022 meta-analysis by Tim Kaiser, Annamaria Lusardi, Lukas Menkhoff and Carly Urban, published in the *Journal of Financial Economics*, pooled 76 randomised experiments covering more than 160,000 people across 33 countries and found financial education has positive, economically meaningful effects on both knowledge and behaviour — "at least three times as large" as the earlier estimate, and comparable to educational interventions in other fields, with the strongest results for budgeting, saving and borrowing. Region: global (research). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban, *Journal of Financial Economics* 145(2), 2022, pp. 255-272 (also NBER Working Paper 27057); figures corroborated by the GFLEC meta-analysis page and RePEc listings.

5. **A large-scale school mandate: the United States.** Personal-finance education in US high schools is set state by state. The number of states guaranteeing every public-school student a standalone personal-finance course rose from a handful around 2020 to 25 by 2024 (covering about 53% of US public high-schoolers) and to about 30 states in the most recent count; California, the largest state, adopted a requirement in 2024. Studies of earlier state mandates find they improve young adults' credit scores and reduce reliance on high-cost borrowing. Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Next Gen Personal Finance "State of Financial Education" dashboard and press releases (ngpf.org, "25th state," "on track to ... 53% by 2030") and the National Endowment for Financial Education's 2024 legislative review; the credit-outcome research is from Urban/Brown and related studies.

6. **A national coordinating strategy: the UK.** The UK's Money and Pensions Service (a government-sponsored body) runs the "UK Strategy for Financial Wellbeing," a 10-year plan launched in 2020 with numerical goals — for example, two million more people saving regularly and two million fewer using credit for essentials by 2030 — and coordinates banks, charities and schools rather than delivering all teaching itself. Region: United Kingdom. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Money and Pensions Service / "UK Strategy for Financial Wellbeing" material as summarised by FinCap (fincap.org.uk) and MaPS publications; the 2020 launch and the "2 million" targets are widely cited.

7. **Japan's new dedicated body: J-FLEC.** Japan created the Japan Financial Literacy and Education Corporation (J-FLEC), authorised in February 2024, operational from April, and formally launched on 2 August 2024, as a public-private organisation jointly backed by the Financial Services Agency, the Bank of Japan and financial-industry groups. It absorbed the functions of the Bank of Japan-run Central Council for Financial Services Information and is charged with coordinating financial education nationwide — free of product-selling incentives. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Prime Minister's Office of Japan ("Launching Ceremony of the Japan Financial Literacy and Education Corporation," japan.kantei.go.jp) and the Bank of Japan release of 1 March 2024 (boj.or.jp); corroborated by a 2025 World Economic Forum piece on Japanese financial education.

8. **Japan's school context: the age-of-majority change.** Japan lowered the age of adulthood from 20 to 18 in April 2022, meaning 18-year-olds can now sign contracts (loans, credit cards, phone plans) without parental consent. High-school curriculum guidelines revised the same year added household financial planning and "asset formation" content to home-economics classes — the change that made school financial education a near-term priority rather than a long-term aspiration. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Japan's Ministry of Justice on the 2022 age-of-majority change and MEXT curriculum-guideline revisions, widely reported (Nippon.com, Japan Times); the link to school financial education is made in J-FLEC and FSA material.

## The Japanese angle (short paragraph, per the shape plan)

Japan spent decades with financial education handled quietly by a Bank of Japan-linked council; two changes made it a policy priority. First, in April 2022 the age of adulthood dropped from 20 to 18, so a first-year university student can now take out a loan or a credit card with no parental sign-off, and revised school curriculum guidelines added household budgeting and "asset formation" to home-economics lessons the same year. Second, in August 2024 the government launched the Japan Financial Literacy and Education Corporation (J-FLEC), a public-private body jointly backed by the Financial Services Agency, the Bank of Japan and industry associations, to coordinate teaching for schools and adults without the conflict of interest that comes when a bank or broker provides the "education." The push also connects to a separate policy goal — moving some of Japan's large pool of household cash and deposits into investment through the expanded NISA tax-free accounts — which raises the stakes on people understanding risk before they act. For a Japanese reader the question the topic poses is concrete: will a new agency and a school requirement actually change behaviour, given that the international evidence on whether financial education "works" is genuinely mixed?

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **financial literacy** — the knowledge and skills needed to make informed money decisions
- **financial capability / financial wellbeing** — broader terms covering not just knowledge but behaviour, confidence, and access to suitable products
- **the "Big Three" questions** — the standard three-question test of understanding of interest, inflation and risk diversification
- **compound interest** — interest earned on both the original sum and on interest already added
- **risk diversification** — spreading money across different assets to reduce the effect of any one loss
- **national financial-education strategy** — a government-coordinated multi-year plan involving many bodies
- **randomised controlled trial (RCT)** — a study that assigns people to a programme or a control group at random to measure its effect
- **meta-analysis** — a study that statistically combines the results of many earlier studies
- **"just-in-time" / "teachable moment" education** — guidance delivered right when a person faces a specific financial decision
- **default option / nudge** — a pre-set choice or small design change that steers behaviour without teaching
- **conflict of interest** — here, the risk that "education" provided by a seller promotes its own products
- **age of majority** — the age at which a person can legally enter contracts on their own (lowered to 18 in Japan in 2022)
