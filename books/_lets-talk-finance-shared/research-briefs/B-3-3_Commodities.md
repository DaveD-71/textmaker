# Research Brief — Book B 3.3 Commodities: Oil, Metals and Food

Prepared 2026-09-10 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch — primary sources opened directly where the site allowed automated fetch; noted per anchor where a government/IGO page blocked it and news co-sources were used instead).

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Why the prices of the raw materials the world runs on — crude oil, industrial and "critical" metals, and food staples such as wheat and cooking oil — swing so sharply, and who feels those swings. Two different stories sit inside one topic: a **supply shock**, where a war, an accident, or a production cut suddenly removes barrels or tonnes from the market (the 2022 oil and wheat spikes after Russia's invasion of Ukraine), and a **structural or demand-driven** price story, where prices climb over years because new supply cannot keep pace with growing use (copper for electrification) or because one country controls most of the supply (China and rare earths). For an economy that produces almost none of these things itself, such as Japan, the price on the world market is close to the whole story.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The 2022 oil supply shock.** Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 pushed Brent crude, the main international oil benchmark, to an intraday US$139.13 a barrel on 7 March 2022 — its highest level since 2008 — before it settled around US$120-128 a barrel that week. Region: global / Russia / OPEC+. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Nature *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* (peer-reviewed, citing the US$139.13 March 2022 high) and NAIC Capital Markets Bureau ("New Oil Price High – March 2022"), corroborated by Bloomberg's 7-8 March 2022 price coverage. **Flag:** exact settle prices differ by the hour and by source; use the US$139 intraday high (well attested) and describe the settle level as "around US$120-130," dated to the first week of March 2022.

2. **The largest strategic-reserve release in US history.** On 31 March 2022 the United States announced it would release 180 million barrels from its Strategic Petroleum Reserve — about 1 million barrels a day for six months — the biggest release since the reserve was created in 1975; other International Energy Agency members added a further 60 million barrels (later raised). It is the clearest recent example of governments holding physical stockpiles specifically to blunt a supply shock. Region: United States / IEA. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — US Department of Energy ("United States to Release ... Barrels of Oil From the Strategic Petroleum Reserve," energy.gov) and USAFacts analysis of the release; corroborated by CBS News and EIA "Today in Energy."

3. **The opposite shock: oil below zero, April 2020.** On 20 April 2020, at the depth of the COVID-19 demand collapse and with storage tanks nearly full, the expiring May futures contract for West Texas Intermediate (WTI), the US oil benchmark, settled at about **minus US$37.63 a barrel** — the first negative price since WTI futures trading began in 1983. Holders of the contract were, in effect, paying buyers to take oil off their hands because there was nowhere to put it. Region: United States / global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — US EIA ("Today in Energy," id=46336, "the first time in history that WTI prices became negative") and US CFTC interim report on 20 April 2020 NYMEX WTI trading (cftc.gov); corroborated by CNBC. **Flag:** CME's official settlement was -US$37.63; EIA rounds to -US$37.62. Use "about -US$37 a barrel."

4. **The 2022 food-price shock.** The UN Food and Agriculture Organization's Food Price Index — a basket tracking world prices of cereals, vegetable oils, dairy, meat and sugar — hit an all-time high of **159.3 points in March 2022**, up 12.6% in a single month and 33.6% higher than a year earlier, driven mainly by wheat and vegetable-oil prices after the invasion. Russia and Ukraine together supplied roughly 30% of world wheat exports, and Ukraine alone about half of world sunflower-oil exports. Region: global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — UN News ("Ukraine war drives international food prices to 'new all-time high'," news.un.org, quoting the 159.3-point March 2022 figure) and World Economic Forum ("These charts show food prices have increased sharply"); corroborated by NPR. **Note:** the FAO index fell back through 2023-24 as harvests and trade adjusted — a Reading should show the spike *and* the partial retreat, not just the peak.

5. **A targeted fix for a supply shock: the Black Sea Grain Initiative.** A deal brokered by the United Nations and Türkiye in July 2022 let Ukraine ship grain out of three Black Sea ports through a protected corridor; over the year to July 2023 it moved about **33 million tonnes** of grain and other foodstuffs to world markets before Russia withdrew from it on 17 July 2023. It shows that a supply shock in one commodity can be partly reversed by a specific logistical arrangement rather than only by higher prices drawing out new supply. Region: global / Ukraine / Türkiye. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Council of the EU ("Ukrainian grain exports explained," consilium.europa.eu) and the UN Office of the Black Sea Grain Initiative Coordinator (un.org); corroborated by CNBC and IFPRI.

6. **A metals market breaking down: LME nickel, March 2022.** On 8 March 2022 the price of nickel on the London Metal Exchange spiked past **US$100,000 a tonne** — a rise of more than 100% in about five hours — as fears about Russian supply collided with a large bet against the price by a major Chinese producer, Tsingshan Holding Group, that was forced to buy back ("short-covering"). The LME suspended nickel trading and then cancelled about **US$12 billion** of trades; UK courts later ruled the cancellation lawful. Region: United Kingdom (LME) / China. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Norton Rose Fulbright ("London Metal Exchange wins High Court battle over US$12 billion cancelled nickel trades") and the US Office of Financial Research working paper "Central Clearing and Trade Cancellation: The Case of LME Nickel Contracts on March 8, 2022" (financialresearch.gov); corroborated by Business Standard and Euronews.

7. **A structural metals story: copper and electrification.** LME copper reached a then-record **US$10,899 a tonne on 20 May 2024**, passing its March 2022 high, on expectations that demand from electric vehicles, power grids, renewable-energy equipment and data centres would outpace new mine supply for years. Unlike the oil and wheat spikes, this is a slow demand-growth story, not a war shock — and copper has set further records since (three-month LME copper broke US$11,200 a tonne in October 2025). Region: global (LME / COMEX). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — StoneX ("Copper Posts New Record Highs," giving the US$10,899/t 20 May 2024 figure vs the US$10,674/t March 2022 high) and Benchmark Mineral Intelligence ("LME copper three-month price climbs to record high"); corroborated by ADM Investor Services and Trading Economics. **Flag:** copper prices have kept moving; pick one clearly dated figure at drafting time rather than "the record," which keeps changing.

8. **Concentration risk: China and critical minerals.** China accounts for roughly **60% of world rare-earth mining and about 90% of rare-earth processing**, and about **98% of the world's refined gallium and germanium** (metals used in chips, fibre optics and defence electronics). From July 2023 China placed export-licensing controls on gallium and germanium, then extended similar controls to graphite (October 2023), antimony (August 2024), tungsten and tellurium (February 2025) and rare earths (2025). For these commodities the price risk is one government's policy, not weather or war. Region: China / global supply chains. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Center for Strategic and International Studies ("The Consequences of China's New Rare Earths Export Restrictions," csis.org) and Global Trade Alert's inventory of Chinese critical-raw-material export controls; corroborated by ORF America and a US International Trade Commission executive briefing on germanium and gallium.

9. **Japan — near-total import dependence.** Japan's primary energy self-sufficiency rate was **15.2% in fiscal 2023** (its highest since the 2011 earthquake, up from 12.6% in fiscal 2022); it imports close to 100% of its crude oil, coal and liquefied natural gas. Its food self-sufficiency rate on a calorie basis was **38% in fiscal 2023**, unchanged for three years and below the government's fiscal-2030 target of 45%. A weak yen through 2022-24 raised the yen cost of all of these imports at the same time world prices were rising. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — METI FY2023 Energy Supply and Demand Report (Preliminary), reported via Statista and OpenGov.jp (15.2% / 12.6% figures); The Japan Times ("Japan's food self-sufficiency rate remains at 38%," 10 October 2025) and Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (maff.go.jp) for the 38% calorie-basis figure. **Note:** the weak-yen effect is developed fully in the next topic, B 3.4 Currencies and Exchange Rates — here it is only the amplifier on imported commodity costs, worth one sentence, not the teaching point.

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead or a mid-article turn)

Japan is close to a textbook "price-taker" in commodities: it produces almost none of the oil, gas, coal, metals or feed grain it uses, so when a world price moves, Japan pays the new price. In fiscal 2023 its energy self-sufficiency rate was 15.2% — a post-2011 high, but still meaning about 85% of primary energy was imported — and its food self-sufficiency rate on a calorie basis was 38%, unchanged for three years. That leaves the country doubly exposed. In 2022 the same event, Russia's invasion of Ukraine, pushed up the world price of crude oil, LNG and wheat all at once, and it happened while the yen was weakening sharply against the dollar, so the yen cost of each imported cargo rose twice over — once for the commodity, once for the currency. Japan's usual buffers are the ones every importer uses: long-term supply contracts, part-ownership of overseas oil and gas fields, government and commercial stockpiles (Japan holds well over 200 days of oil reserves between the state and industry), and, for food, a high tariff wall and support for the few staples it does grow. Rice is the striking counter-example: Japan is nearly self-sufficient in rice, yet a domestic supply squeeze still sent retail rice prices up sharply in 2024-25 — a reminder that being your own supplier reduces exposure to world prices but does not remove price risk altogether.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **commodity** — a raw material or basic good (oil, wheat, copper) that is traded in bulk and is largely interchangeable between suppliers
- **benchmark price** — a widely quoted reference price for a commodity (Brent and WTI for crude oil)
- **spot price vs futures price** — the price for immediate delivery vs an agreed price for delivery on a future date
- **supply shock** — a sudden fall in the available supply of a commodity, pushing its price up quickly
- **demand shock** — a sudden change in how much of a commodity buyers want (the 2020 collapse in oil demand)
- **OPEC+** — the group of major oil-exporting countries (OPEC plus partners such as Russia) that coordinate production levels
- **strategic reserve / stockpile** — a store of a commodity held by a government to release during a shortage (the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve)
- **price-taker** — a buyer or country too small in a market to influence the price, so it simply pays the going rate
- **self-sufficiency rate** — the share of a country's consumption of energy or food that is produced domestically
- **critical minerals / rare earths** — metals essential to modern technology whose supply is concentrated in a few countries
- **export controls** — government limits on selling a good abroad, used here as a lever on commodity supply
- **hedging** — using financial contracts to lock in a future price and reduce exposure to price swings
- **pass-through** — how much of a commodity-price rise reaches consumers as higher retail prices
- **volatility** — the size and speed of price movements in a market
