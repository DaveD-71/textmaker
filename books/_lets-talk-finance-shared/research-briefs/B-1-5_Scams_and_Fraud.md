# Research Brief — Book B 1.5 Scams, Fraud and Financial Self-Defence

Prepared August 2026. Research only — no article drafted.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Fraud is now one of the most common crimes in many rich countries: criminals trick people into
handing over money or bank access through fake investments, phishing messages, impersonation
calls, romance approaches and "pig butchering" crypto schemes. Much of it is run at industrial
scale by organised crime, increasingly from fortified "scam compounds" in South-East Asia and,
more recently, other regions, often using trafficked workers. Governments, regulators, police and
banks are responding with fraud-reporting bodies, transaction friction (delays, warnings,
withdrawal limits), and — in the UK — a rule forcing banks to refund most victims of
bank-transfer scams; but reported-loss totals still understate the real problem because most
fraud is never reported.

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan has struggled for two decades with "special fraud" (tokushu sagi) — a family of scams that
includes the classic "ore ore" / "it's me" call, in which a fraudster poses as a relative in
trouble, and, more recently, fake police and fake official calls. It disproportionately hits
older people: in 2024 losses hit a record ¥72.15 billion (about US$480 million), up nearly 60% in
a year, and roughly 45% of individual victims were aged 75 or over (National Police Agency, 2024
figures, released 23 May 2025). Countermeasures are heavily focused on the elderly and on adding
friction to cash-out: many banks have for years suspended ATM transfers on accounts held by older
customers who have not used the function recently, some cap daily ATM withdrawals and transfers
(e.g. ¥500,000 / ¥1,000,000), and in 2025 the NPA proposed a ¥300,000 daily ATM limit for
account holders aged 75+; convenience-store staff are trained to question older customers buying
large numbers of prepaid cards. (Sources: Nippon.com citing the NPA, 17 June 2025; South China
Morning Post, 26 March 2025.)

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **fraud** — deceiving someone to get money or advantage unlawfully
- **scam** — a dishonest scheme to cheat people out of money (informal term for fraud)
- **phishing** — fake emails, texts or sites that trick you into revealing passwords or card details
- **authorised push payment (APP) fraud** — when a victim is tricked into sending a bank transfer
  themselves to a criminal (the payment is "authorised" by the victim)
- **impersonation** — pretending to be someone trusted (a bank, the police, a family member)
- **investment fraud** — persuading someone to put money into a fake or non-existent investment
- **romance scam** — building a fake romantic relationship online to extract money
- **pig butchering** — a hybrid romance-and-investment scam, often crypto, where the victim is
  "fattened up" with a relationship before being cleared out (from Chinese *shā zhū pán*)
- **reimbursement** — paying a victim back for money lost
- **scam compound / scam centre** — a guarded site, often in South-East Asia, where workers
  (frequently trafficked) are made to run online scams
- **money mule** — a person who moves criminal funds through their own bank account, wittingly or not
- **cooling-off period / transaction friction** — deliberate delays or warnings added before a
  payment goes through, to give a victim time to stop

## Notes / cautions

- **Reported-loss figures undercount reality.** Every headline total here (FTC, FBI, UK Finance,
  NPA, NASC, UNODC) is based on cases that were reported to that body. Most fraud is never
  reported — from embarrassment, or because victims do not know where to report. The FTC itself
  says its data capture only reported incidents. Present all totals as "reported losses", not
  "losses".
- **Different bodies, non-additive numbers.** The US FTC (US$12.5bn) and FBI IC3 (US$16.6bn)
  figures come from separate reporting systems with overlapping victims; do not add them or treat
  the gap as meaningful. Same caution for Japan's "special fraud" vs "SNS-type investment/romance
  fraud" — the NPA tracks them separately and they should not be summed without saying so.
- **Figures are annual and get revised.** 2024 full-year figures were mostly published in
  spring 2025; provisional and final versions differ slightly (e.g. Japan special-fraud victim
  counts appear as ~20,900–21,000 depending on the release). Some 2025 data now exists (Japan's
  Jan–Jul 2025 losses already exceeded the 2024 full-year record; UNODC's July 2026 region-wide
  estimate is for 2025). Current month is **August 2026** — a writer should note figures are the
  latest available full-year data and may be superseded.
- **Currency conversions** (yen/AUD to USD) move with exchange rates; give the local-currency
  figure first and treat USD as approximate.
- **The UK reimbursement rule is UK-only and recent** (from 7 October 2024). Do not imply other
  countries have the same mandatory-refund regime — most do not; elsewhere (including the US and
  Japan) whether a bank-transfer scam victim gets money back is far less certain, which is a good
  contrast point for the reading.
- **"Pig butchering" losses** in the US$75bn-over-four-years range circulate widely but trace to
  an academic estimate (Univ. of Texas), not an official body; attribute it as an estimate, or
  prefer the UNODC region-wide ranges.
- **FTC, FBI/IC3, UNODC and UK Finance pages could not be opened by our research tooling** (bot
  blocking / paywall), but all are reachable in a normal browser and are the correct primary
  citations; the figures above were cross-checked against established secondary reporting
  (SecurityWeek, CyberScoop, Which?, SCMP, Nippon.com, OCCRP). The writer should cite the primary
  page and verify the exact number in a browser.
