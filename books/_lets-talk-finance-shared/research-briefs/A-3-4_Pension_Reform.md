## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

How a country's public (and sometimes mandatory occupational) pension system pays for the retirement of an ageing population: whether it is pay-as-you-go (today's workers' contributions fund today's retirees) or funded (money is invested in advance), and the levers governments pull when the sums stop working — raising the retirement age, raising contribution rates, cutting the benefit formula, or widening who must pay in. This is the policy/system level, not an individual's personal choice about how to save for their own retirement.

## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

Japan is both the world's most-watched ageing case and a country still mid-reform. Its public system has two tiers: the National Pension (a flat-rate basic pension all residents aged 20–59 must join) and the Employees' Pension Insurance (an earnings-related top-up for company employees). Two long-running reforms defined the current shape of the system: a phased rise in the pension eligibility age from 60 to 65, legislated in 1994 and 2000, which only finished for men's earnings-related benefit in 2025 (women's finishes in 2030); and a phased rise in the combined EPI contribution rate from 13.58% to a fixed 18.3% of salary, completed in 2017 and unchanged since. A built-in "macroeconomic slide" mechanism, introduced in the 2004 reform, automatically trims how much pension benefits rise with prices or wages, based on life expectancy and the shrinking number of contributors — a direct, mechanical response to Japan's demographics. Those demographics keep worsening: the share of the population aged 65 or older hit a record 29.4% in 2025 (about 36.19 million people), among the highest shares of any large country, while the total fertility rate fell to a record-low 1.15 in 2024 and annual births dropped below 700,000 for the first time. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's own 2024 five-yearly financial check projects the pension replacement rate will fall from 61.2% (fiscal 2024) to 50.4% (fiscal 2057) under its cautious growth scenario — still above the legally required 50% floor, but a genuine decline. The government's most recent policy response, a law passed in June 2025, does not touch the retirement age or contribution rate; instead it widens who must join the system, scrapping an income threshold and lowering a company-size threshold so an estimated 1.8 million more part-time workers are drawn into the Employees' Pension Insurance.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **pay-as-you-go (PAYG) system** — today's workers' contributions directly fund today's retirees' pensions
- **funded pension** — a system where money is invested in advance to pay future benefits
- **public pension** — a state-run retirement scheme, as distinct from a private/occupational one
- **statutory retirement age** (also *pensionable age*, *full retirement age*) — the age at which a full pension can first be claimed
- **contribution rate** — the share of salary paid into the pension system, usually split between employee and employer
- **replacement rate** — pension income as a share of a worker's earnings (or a household's income) before retirement
- **old-age dependency ratio** — the number of people over a set age (e.g. 65) per 100 people of working age
- **defined-benefit pension** — a pension that promises a set payout, usually based on salary and years worked
- **defined-contribution pension** — a pension whose eventual payout depends on how much was paid in and how it performed
- **pension deficit** (also *funding gap*) — the shortfall between what a pension system owes and what it can pay
- **automatic adjustment mechanism** — a built-in rule that changes benefits or contributions without a new law each time (Japan's version is called the "macroeconomic slide")
- **trust fund depletion** — the point at which a pension system's reserve is used up
- **phase-in** (of a reform) — introducing a change gradually, often by birth-year cohort, rather than all at once
- **pension reform** — a change to the rules governing a public or mandatory pension system
- **occupational pension** — a pension tied to employment, such as Japan's Employees' Pension Insurance
