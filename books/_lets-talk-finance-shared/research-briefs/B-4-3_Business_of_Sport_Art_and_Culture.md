# Research Brief — Book B 4.3 The Business of Sport, Art and Culture

Prepared 2026-09-11 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts checked against at least two independent sources; where a report or federation page blocked automated fetch, news and industry co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Sport, art and culture look like leisure, but they are also industries — and they are funded in two very different ways. At one end, elite football, major US sports leagues, blockbuster film and streaming, and the top of the art market run as commercial businesses on media rights, ticketing, sponsorship, transfers and sales, and attract billions in outside investment. At the other, orchestras, opera houses, public museums and most non-blockbuster culture cannot cover their costs from tickets and depend on government subsidy or private philanthropy to survive. The topic compares the two models, explains the structural reason live performing arts struggle to pay for themselves, and looks at what changes when sovereign wealth funds and streaming platforms move in.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The commercial engine: football media rights.** In December 2023 the English Premier League sold its UK live-broadcast rights for a record GBP 6.7 billion (about US$8.45 billion) over four years from the 2025-26 season — the largest domestic sports-media deal in UK history — with up to 270 matches shown live each season, split mainly between Sky and TNT Sports. Media rights are the single biggest revenue source for top European football. Region: United Kingdom / Europe. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — SportsPro and Bloomberg ("Premier League sells UK rights ... for £6.7 billion") and the Premier League's own announcement, consistent on the GBP 6.7 billion figure, the four-year term and the 2025-26 start.

2. **The scale at the top: Real Madrid's billion.** In the 2023-24 season Real Madrid became the first football club to record more than EUR 1 billion of revenue in a single year (Deloitte Football Money League), reaching about EUR 1.05 billion and later EUR 1.16 billion; the next-largest clubs (Manchester City around EUR 838 million, Paris Saint-Germain around EUR 806 million) trail well behind. Region: Europe. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Deloitte UK press room ("Real Madrid break billion Euro revenue record") and Real Madrid's own statement, plus Goal.com's Money League breakdown.

3. **Outside capital: Gulf sovereign funds in sport.** Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund bought Premier League club Newcastle United in October 2021 for about GBP 305 million (later raising its stake to 85%), took ownership of four Saudi Pro League clubs in June 2023 (signing players such as Cristiano Ronaldo on a deal worth around US$550 million), and has reportedly put around US$5 billion into the breakaway LIV Golf circuit. The fund states three aims at once: financial return, "soft power," and building domestic entertainment under its Vision 2030 plan. Region: Gulf (Saudi Arabia). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Play the Game ("The Public Investment Fund's growing sports portfolio") and Golf Channel / Gulf News, consistent on the Newcastle price, the June 2023 four-club takeover and the ~US$5 billion LIV figure.

4. **The subsidy-dependent side: performing arts can't pay their way from tickets.** US orchestras, on average, receive more of their revenue from donations than from ticket sales — around 43 cents of every dollar from contributions against about 40 cents from tickets (League of American Orchestras) — and many European orchestras get more than half their budget from government. Because the US gives little public support, American concert tickets average close to US$80, which limits audiences. Region: United States / Europe. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — League of American Orchestras data as cited by Symphony.org and the Stanford Economic Review, and comparative pieces on European government funding exceeding 50% of orchestra budgets.

5. **Why: "cost disease."** The economist William Baumol pointed out that a live performing art cannot raise its productivity the way a factory can — a string quartet still needs four musicians for the same length of time as in 1800 — so as wages rise across the economy, the *relative* cost of live performance keeps climbing. This is the structural reason orchestras, opera and theatre need subsidy or philanthropy indefinitely, not just in a bad year. Region: general economics. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — "Baumol's cost disease" is standard in cultural economics, described across academic and policy sources on arts funding.

6. **Big differences in public support: Germany vs the US.** Germany funds culture heavily through a decentralised system of state and city budgets — on the order of billions of euros a year — which sustains a dense network of publicly owned theatres, opera houses and orchestras. The US National Endowment for the Arts, by contrast, is about 0.004% of the federal budget (roughly US$200 million), and American culture relies instead on earned income and tax-deductible private donations. Region: Germany / United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — NEA "International Data on Government Spending on the Arts" and comparative coverage (Alternet, The Thought), consistent on Germany's far higher per-capita public spending and the NEA's tiny budget share. **Flag:** per-capita comparison figures vary by year and method; use "roughly an order of magnitude more per person" rather than a precise multiple.

7. **The art market: commercial, concentrated, and shrinking.** Global art sales were an estimated US$57.5 billion in 2024, down 12% year-on-year — a second straight annual fall, with the steepest declines at the high end (auction sales down 25%) while the low end grew. The US accounted for 43% of sales by value, the UK 18% and China 15%. Region: global (US / UK / China lead). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Art Basel & UBS Global Art Market Report 2025 as reported by Art Basel, CNN and The Art Newspaper, consistent on the US$57.5 billion figure, the 12% fall and the 43/18/15 country split.

8. **Japan: a small art market, a huge cultural export.** Japan's domestic art market is small by global standards (roughly 1% of world sales). But its animation industry generated a record of about JPY 3.3 trillion (around US$21 billion) in 2023, up 14%, and for the first time on a full-year basis overseas revenue (about JPY 1.72 trillion) exceeded domestic revenue (about JPY 1.62 trillion) — anime is Japan's most successful cultural business, and it is primarily an export. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Association of Japanese Animations "Anime Industry Report" as reported by Screen Daily ("record $22bn in 2023") and Japan Today / The Business Standard (JPY 3.3 trillion, overseas surpassing domestic).

## The Japanese angle (mid-article, per the shape plan)

Japan is a useful case because its cultural economy is lopsided in an instructive way. Its fine-art market is small — around 1% of global sales — and its domestic classical-music and theatre institutions rely on the same mix of public money and corporate support as elsewhere. But Japan has one cultural industry that is a genuine commercial powerhouse: animation. In 2023 the anime industry earned a record of roughly JPY 3.3 trillion (about US$21 billion), and for the first time across a full year, revenue from overseas — streaming licences, merchandise, films, events — exceeded revenue from Japan itself. Anime works as a business for reasons that do not apply to a live orchestra: it can be reproduced and distributed at almost no extra cost, sold into every market at once, and monetised through characters and merchandise for decades. Japan's football league, the J.League, sits in between — commercially run, but dependent on a large broadcast-rights partnership to fund itself, in a market far smaller than England's. The contrast inside one country mirrors the topic's central split: culture that scales through reproduction and rights can be a big business; culture that must be performed live, each time, generally cannot pay for itself.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **media / broadcast rights** — the payments a league or event receives for letting broadcasters and streamers show its content
- **gate revenue / box office** — income from ticket sales
- **sponsorship** — payment by a brand to be associated with a team, event or venue
- **transfer fee** — the payment one club makes another to sign a player under contract
- **franchise (US sports)** — a league-licensed team; franchise "value" is what an ownership stake would sell for
- **sovereign wealth fund** — a state-owned investment fund (here, buying sports assets)
- **"soft power"** — influence a country gains through culture and reputation rather than force or money
- **subsidy / grant-in-aid** — government money given to support an activity that does not cover its own costs
- **philanthropy / endowment** — private charitable giving; an invested fund whose returns support an institution
- **earned income vs contributed income** — money an arts body makes from tickets and sales vs money it receives as gifts and grants
- **"cost disease" (Baumol)** — the tendency for the relative cost of labour-intensive services such as live performance to rise over time
- **intellectual property (IP) / licensing** — the rights to a character, story or brand, and the fees charged to use them
