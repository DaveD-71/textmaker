# Research Brief — Book B 1.4 Housing Markets and Affordability

_Prepared August 2026. Research only — not a draft._

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

House prices are set by demand (population, incomes, credit conditions, interest rates, expectations) meeting a supply of housing that is often slow to respond because of land constraints and land-use rules. This unit looks at why housing has become less affordable relative to incomes across much of the rich world since the 1990s, the choice between renting and buying, and whether a home is best seen as an investment or simply as shelter. It also surveys the policy levers governments pull — zoning and planning reform, rent regulation, public housing, tax settings — and why homes hold value in some places (most of the Anglosphere) but lose it in others (much of Japan).

## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan is the rich world's clearest counter-example to "houses always go up." The building and the land are valued separately, and the structure is widely treated as a wasting asset: the tax code assigns a wooden house a 22-year depreciable life, lenders and appraisers discount older buildings steeply, and a detached house is often assumed to have little or no building value after 20–30 years, with resale priced mainly off the land. That norm is reinforced by a large flow of new construction — about **800,000 housing starts in FY2023** (MLIT) despite a shrinking population — and by comparatively light, nationally-set zoning that makes it easy to knock down and rebuild. The flip side is the **akiya** problem: the 2023 Housing and Land Survey counted about **9 million vacant homes, a record 13.8% of the stock**, heavily concentrated in rural and shrinking areas, with roughly 3.9 million simply abandoned. Verifiable facts + urls: MLIT FY2023 housing starts (`https://www.patiencerealty.com/post/japan-nationwide-fy-2023-housing-starts-decline-for-second-consecutive-year`, primary series MLIT/e-Stat `https://www.e-stat.go.jp/en/stat-search/files?page=1&layout=dataset&toukei=00600120`); vacancy figures (`https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h01987/`, primary: Statistics Bureau Housing and Land Survey 2023).

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **house price-to-income ratio** — average home price divided by average household income; a standard affordability gauge (OECD indexes it to 2015 = 100)
- **affordability** — the relationship between housing costs (purchase or rent) and household income
- **mortgage** — a loan secured against property; the borrower loses the home if repayments stop
- **zoning / land-use rules / planning permission** — government rules on what may be built where and how densely
- **upzoning** — relaxing zoning to allow taller or denser buildings on a site (e.g. Auckland 2016)
- **housing supply / housing stock** — the total number of dwellings; how responsive new building is to prices is the "supply elasticity"
- **housing starts** — the number of new dwellings on which construction has begun in a period
- **rent control / rent cap / rent regulation** — legal limits on rent levels or rent increases (e.g. Berlin's Mietendeckel)
- **depreciation** — the fall in an asset's value as it ages and wears out; in Japan applied heavily to house structures
- **land value vs building value** — a property's price split between the plot and the structure on it
- **public housing / social housing** — government-built or government-subsidised rental or sale housing (e.g. Singapore's HDB flats)
- **real vs nominal prices** — "real" prices are adjusted for general inflation; "nominal" are not
- **negative equity** — when the outstanding mortgage exceeds the home's market value

## Notes / cautions

- **House-price data is revised and index-dependent.** The BIS "selected series," OECD analytical series, and national indices (e.g. NZ's REINZ House Price Index, Sweden's HOX/Valueguard, Statistics Canada's NHPI) differ in coverage, method (repeat-sales vs median vs hedonic) and base period. Always name the specific series and the exact quarter, and state whether figures are real or nominal.
- **BIS reclassified several economies as "advanced" in January 2026** (Czechia, Hong Kong, Israel, Korea, Singapore), which changes the composition of BIS aggregate figures over time.
- The **OECD price-to-income figures in anchor 2 could not be opened directly** (oecd.org blocks automated fetch); they are consistent across several independent write-ups of the OECD series but must be confirmed on the OECD page or in the Affordable Housing Database (HM1.2) before the number goes in print. Same caution for the **RBNZ** figures (anchors 3–4) and the **Auckland upzoning** study numbers (anchor 6): the substance is well established, but open the primary pages to lock exact wording and values.
- **Japan's "22-year" figure is a tax-depreciation convention, not a physical lifespan.** MLIT-cited research puts the actual average life of a demolished wooden house closer to ~65 years. The depreciation-to-zero pattern is strongest in weak rural markets; in central Tokyo wards, land values have risen and older houses retain substantial value through the land. Frame carefully.
- **"Prices doubled since 2000" needs a country and a series.** New Zealand and Canada more than doubled (indeed more than tripled in nominal terms in Canada 2005–2022); Australia's real rise over ~2000–2022 is more like 50–70% depending on series. Pick the country and cite its central bank or statistics office.
- Rent control is genuinely contested in the literature; present Berlin as a concrete legal case (struck down on jurisdiction, not merits) rather than as settled evidence for or against.
