## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

How the roads, bridges, power grids, ports, and water systems a country depends on actually get funded. Governments have never been the sole builder — a persistent "funding gap" between what is invested and what is needed is filled by a mix of public budgets, private capital, multilateral development banks, and public-private partnerships (PPPs), in which a private company designs, builds, finances, and operates a public asset — often a toll road, airport, or power plant — in exchange for revenue (tolls, fees, or government payments) over a fixed concession period before ownership reverts to the state. A second, related problem in wealthy countries is that infrastructure built decades ago is now ageing and needs costly repair or replacement, competing for funding with new projects.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **Global funding-gap estimate.** The Global Infrastructure Hub (GI Hub), a G20 initiative, forecasts total global infrastructure investment *need* of US$94 trillion by 2040 (US$97 trillion including universal access to water and electricity), but at current spending trends only about US$79 trillion will actually be invested — leaving an unfunded gap of roughly US$15 trillion (some GI Hub materials round this to US$18 trillion). Closing the gap means raising global infrastructure investment from about 3% of world GDP to roughly 3.5%. Region: global. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — GI Hub (gihub.org, "Global infrastructure investment need to reach USD97 trillion by 2040") and World Economic Forum ("The world is facing a $15 trillion infrastructure gap by 2040," weforum.org/stories/2019/04/infrastructure-gap-heres-how-to-solve-it/), both citing the same GI Hub Global Infrastructure Outlook dataset.

2. **United States — the largest single-country gap.** The GI Hub projects the US will have the single largest national infrastructure funding gap in the world, at roughly US$3.8 trillion by 2040. Separately, the American Society of Civil Engineers' 2025 Infrastructure Report Card gave US infrastructure an overall grade of C (the highest since ASCE's report card began in 1998, up from C- in 2021) but still found a US$3.7 trillion ten-year gap (2024–2033) between the US$9.1 trillion needed to bring all categories to a state of good repair and the roughly US$5.4 trillion likely to actually be spent. Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — for the GI Hub figure: gihub.org and tralac.org (tralac trade law centre), both citing GI Hub's Global Infrastructure Outlook; for the ASCE figure: asce.org ("ASCE Report Card Gives U.S. Infrastructure Highest-Ever C Grade," 25 March 2026) and Government Market News / Engineering News-Record, independently reporting the same US$3.7 trillion figure.

3. **European Union — infrastructure investment gap.** The European Investment Bank (EIB) has separately estimated the EU's shortfall between actual and required infrastructure investment (energy, transport, telecoms, water) at roughly €335 billion a year over a five-year horizon, with more than 30% of that gap in energy infrastructure. A broader EU-wide estimate from the think tank Bruegel put the annual investment shortfall for climate, digital, and resilience goals (infrastructure-heavy but wider in scope) at least €481 billion a year through 2030. Region: European Union. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — the €335bn EIB figure is referenced consistently across CaixaBank Research ("Infrastructure in the European Union and the Juncker Plan") and the Global Infrastructure Investors Association (giia.net); the €481bn Bruegel figure is corroborated by Bloomberg ("EU Faces €481 Billion Annual Shortfall on Strategic Investments," 23 January 2024) and Bruegel's own published report (bruegel.org, "Accelerating strategic investment in the European Union beyond 2026").

4. **Japan — ageing bridges and tunnels.** Japan built most of its expressways, bridges, and tunnels during the high-growth era of the 1950s–1970s. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) projects that by 2033, about 60% of Japan's roughly 730,000 road bridges will be 50 years old or older, and about 40% of road tunnels will be 50 years or older — a threshold after which deterioration accelerates sharply and major repair or replacement becomes necessary. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Nikkei Asia ("Aging infrastructure a major roadblock to Japan's future," asia.nikkei.com/spotlight/datawatch/) and a second, independent report citing the same MLIT data (Shot Peener Magazine, "Japan's Infrastructure Situation and Peening"), both attributing the 60%/40% figures to MLIT.

5. **Japan — public-works budget holds steady above JPY 6 trillion.** Japan's FY2025 (April 2025–March 2026) government draft budget allocated JPY 6,085.8 billion (about JPY 6.09 trillion) to public-works-related spending (公共事業関係費) — the twelfth consecutive fiscal year the figure has stayed above JPY 6 trillion — of which roughly JPY 4.07 trillion was earmarked for "national resilience" (disaster-prevention and ageing-infrastructure) projects. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — Nikkei xTECH ("公共事業費6兆円維持し防災新事業、25年度政府予算案を読み解く") and a second independent Japanese source, Sōgō Shikaku Navi (arc-navi.shikaku.co.jp), both citing the same JPY 6,085.8 billion / twelfth-consecutive-year figure from the Ministry of Finance/MLIT budget documents.

6. **Japan — long-run maintenance bill.** Separate MLIT-based estimates project Japan's cumulative infrastructure maintenance and renewal spending could reach roughly JPY 71.6–76.1 trillion over the 30 years to the mid-2050s if repairs continue on the current reactive basis, versus a lower total if "preventive maintenance" (fixing problems before they become critical) is adopted broadly; MLIT itself has estimated that shifting from post-failure repair to preventive maintenance could cut lifetime maintenance and renewal costs by roughly 30–50% for some asset categories. Region: Japan. **[SINGLE SOURCE ONLY — flag for manual spot-check]** — figure surfaced via aggregated secondary reporting (Lundgreen's Investor Insights, citing MLIT projections); the underlying MLIT white paper (白書) was not independently opened in this pass because direct URL fetching is blocked. 7. **Emerging-market PPP case — Kenya's Nairobi Expressway.** A 27-kilometre elevated toll road connecting Jomo Kenyatta International Airport to Nairobi's Westlands district, built under a Public-Private Partnership on a Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) model between the Kenyan government and the Chinese contractor China Road and Bridge Corporation (CRBC). The project cost about US$668 million, financed roughly 80% by CRBC and 20% by the Kenyan government (a debt-to-equity structure of about 75:25 on the private portion). CRBC operates and collects tolls under a concession originally reported as 27 years (some sources cite a 30-year concession agreement signed 15 October 2019) before ownership transfers to the Kenyan government. The road opened in July 2022 and was Kenya's first tolled expressway. Region: Sub-Saharan Africa (Kenya). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — cost and financing split corroborated by AidData/china.aiddata.org (citing CRBC regulatory filings: total cost RMB 4.602 billion / US$668 million, PPP/BOOT basis, ~75:25 debt-to-equity) and independent news coverage (South China Morning Post, "China-backed Nairobi Expressway opens in Kenya," and allafrica.com/Daily Nation, "Nairobi Expressway to cost Sh7bn more"). Note: concession length is reported inconsistently across sources (27 years vs. 30 years) — flag for the drafter to pick one figure and, if needed, phrase generally ("around three decades").

8. **Multilateral development bank scale — World Bank.** The World Bank Group committed a record US$118.5 billion in loans, grants, equity investments, and guarantees to partner countries and private businesses in fiscal year 2025 (year ended 30 June 2025), and separately mobilized US$69.9 billion of private capital alongside that lending — illustrating the "blended finance" role MDBs play in drawing private money into projects, including infrastructure. Region: global (MDB). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — World Bank Group's own Annual Report 2025 / IFC summary (ifc.org, worldbank.org) and independent reporting citing the same US$118.5 billion figure appears consistently across multiple aggregator and NGO-monitoring sources (e.g. Bretton Woods Project analysis of the FY2025 Annual Meetings).

9. **Multilateral development bank scale — Asian Development Bank.** The Asian Development Bank (ADB) committed a record US$29.3 billion from its own resources in 2025, a 20% increase year-on-year, boosted by a further US$14.7 billion mobilized from partners; of ADB's own commitments, US$5.5 billion went directly to private-sector projects, and roughly US$9.7 billion went to South Asia — the largest single regional allocation. Region: Asia-Pacific (MDB). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — ADB's own press release (adb.org, "ADB Scaled Up Operations to $29.3 Billion in 2025") corroborated independently by The Financial Express (Bangladesh) and Devdiscourse, both reporting the same US$29.3 billion / 20% year-on-year figures.

10. **Institutional-investor capacity.** Pension funds and insurance companies in OECD and G20 countries collectively hold assets that could, within current investment regulations, support up to roughly US$11.4 trillion allocated toward infrastructure — far more than currently invested — illustrating why governments and multilateral banks are trying to design PPP and blended-finance structures that make infrastructure attractive enough for this private, long-horizon capital to actually flow in. ## The Japanese angle (1 paragraph — this can be the lead)

Japan built the backbone of its modern infrastructure — expressways, bridges, tunnels, and water systems — in a concentrated burst during the high-growth decades of the 1950s to 1970s, largely as direct public works rather than private concessions, and that tradition of state-led construction still shapes the country's budget today: the FY2025 government draft budget kept public-works-related spending above JPY 6 trillion (JPY 6,085.8 billion) for the twelfth consecutive year, with a growing share directed at "national resilience" — disaster prevention and the repair of ageing assets — rather than new construction. The reason for that shift is straightforward: much of what was built fifty or sixty years ago is now reaching the end of its designed life at the same time. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism projects that by 2033 about 60% of Japan's roughly 730,000 road bridges and about 40% of its road tunnels will be 50 years old or older, a threshold past which deterioration accelerates and full-scale repair or replacement becomes unavoidable. Unlike the market-fragmentation funding gaps discussed elsewhere in this topic, Japan's challenge is less about finding money to build something new and more about finding money — and skilled labor, given the country's shrinking working-age population — to keep what already exists safe and functional, a maintenance bill that some projections put in the tens of trillions of yen over the coming decades if the country continues to repair infrastructure only after problems appear rather than on a preventive schedule.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

1. **public-private partnership (PPP)** — an arrangement where a private company finances, builds, and/or operates a public infrastructure asset, usually in exchange for revenue over a fixed period
2. **infrastructure funding gap** — the difference between the infrastructure investment a country or region needs and what it is actually projected to spend
3. **multilateral development bank (MDB)** — an international institution (e.g. the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank) owned by many governments that lends money for development projects, including infrastructure
4. **concession** — the legal right, usually time-limited, for a private company to build and/or operate a public asset and collect revenue from it
5. **Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT)** — a PPP model in which a private company builds and runs an asset for an agreed period, then hands ownership to the government
6. **toll / user-pays financing** — funding a project through direct charges to the people who use it, rather than general taxation
7. **project finance** — lending secured against the future revenue of a specific project rather than the borrower's general balance sheet
8. **blended finance** — combining public or philanthropic money with private capital to make a project attractive enough for private investors
9. **state of good repair** — the standard infrastructure condition needed for safe, reliable, efficient use (used by US infrastructure assessors)
10. **capital expenditure (capex) vs. maintenance/renewal spending** — money spent building new assets vs. money spent keeping existing assets working
11. **debt-to-equity ratio** — the proportion of a project's financing that comes from borrowed money versus the investors' own capital
12. **institutional investor** — a large organization (pension fund, insurance company, sovereign wealth fund) that invests pooled money, including in long-term infrastructure assets
13. **preventive maintenance** — fixing or servicing infrastructure on a planned schedule before it fails, generally cheaper over time than repairing after failure
14. **resilience / disaster-prevention infrastructure** — infrastructure spending aimed at withstanding natural disasters (earthquakes, floods) rather than only expanding capacity
15. **private capital mobilization** — the amount of private-sector money an MDB or public program succeeds in attracting alongside its own lending
