# Research Brief — Book A 2.3 Insurance Market Regulation

Prepared 2026-08-31. Every URL below was opened during research unless explicitly marked [COULD NOT OPEN]. Where a government primary source blocked automated access or sat behind a paywall, a substitute primary/official source or an established outlet is given and flagged.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

Insurance market regulation is how governments and insurance supervisors make sure that an insurer will still be able to pay claims years into the future, when the premiums have already been spent. The main tools are the same everywhere in outline: insurers must hold technical reserves (money set aside for claims they know are coming) plus a layer of extra capital calculated from the risks they carry, they must report regularly to a supervisor who can step in early if the cushion shrinks, and if an insurer fails anyway a policyholder protection fund pays at least part of what was promised. The detail differs sharply by region — the EU runs one harmonised risk-based regime (Solvency II), the US supervises insurers state by state, Japan uses a solvency margin ratio backed by a protection corporation, and the IAIS is now rolling out a first global capital standard — so an insurer authorised in one market normally cannot write business in another without meeting that market's rules too.

1. **The EU's Solvency II regime has applied since 1 January 2016. 3. **The IAIS (International Association of Insurance Supervisors), established in 1994, is the global standard-setter for insurance supervision; its members are supervisors from more than 200 jurisdictions representing about 97% of the world's insurance premiums.** Region: global. [VERIFIED OPENED] IAIS, "What we do" https://www.iais.org/about-the-iais/what-we-do/ ("Established in 1994… over 200 jurisdictions, constituting 97% of the world's insurance premiums").

4. **On 5 December 2024 the IAIS adopted the Insurance Capital Standard (ICS) as a group-wide global capital requirement for Internationally Active Insurance Groups (IAIGs). It is a risk-based measure calibrated to a 99.5% confidence level over one year — the same "1-in-200" bar as Solvency II. There are currently 59 IAIGs across 18 jurisdictions. The ICS was a decade in the making (four public consultations, six field-testing exercises); jurisdictional implementation assessments begin in 2027.** Region: global. [VERIFIED OPENED] IAIS news release, "IAIS adopts Insurance Capital Standard…", dated 5 December 2024 https://www.iais.org/2024/12/iais-adopts-insurance-capital-standard-and-other-enhancements-to-its-global-standards-to-promote-a-resilient-insurance-sector/ (59 IAIGs, 18 jurisdictions; decade of development; assessments from 2027). 99.5%/one-year calibration confirmed in the IAIS press-release PDF https://www.iais.org/uploads/2024/12/IAIS-Press-Release-IAIS-adopts-Insurance-Capital-Standard-and-other-enhancements-to-its-global-standards-to-promote-a-resilient-insurance-sector.pdf [VERIFIED OPENED].

5. **In the United States insurers are supervised state by state, coordinated through the NAIC. The NAIC's Risk-Based Capital (RBC) system — the life formula adopted 1992, implemented 1993 — sets capital "action levels": below a 200% ratio the regulator can require a remediation plan or take other action, and below 70% the regulator is obliged to take control of the company. RBC was created after a wave of failures: a 1992 GAO report counted 176 life and health insurer insolvencies in 1975–1990.** Region: US. ## The Japanese angle (1 short paragraph)

Japan's Financial Services Agency (FSA) supervises insurers using a solvency margin ratio, introduced as a common life/non-life standard in FY1996: a ratio of 200% or more is treated as sound, and once it falls below 200% the FSA applies "prompt corrective action" that escalates as the ratio drops. The system was tested hard when the 1980s bubble burst and left life insurers with guaranteed policy yields they could no longer earn: Nissan Mutual Life failed on 25 April 1997 — the first postwar failure of a Japanese life insurer — and a further wave followed, including Chiyoda Mutual Life and Kyoei Life within days of each other in October 2000 and Tokyo Mutual Life in March 2001 (about ¥980 billion in liabilities, the seventh insurer to fail since the war). In December 1998 Japan set up compulsory policyholder protection corporations for life and for non-life insurers; the Life Insurance Policyholders Protection Corporation of Japan funds a rescuer that takes over a failed insurer's policies, or takes them over itself, originally guaranteeing roughly 90% of policy reserves for most life policies. From the fiscal year ending 31 March 2026 Japan has begun replacing the old solvency margin ratio with an economic-value-based regime (an ICS-aligned "ESR" with a 100% minimum). Verifiable facts + urls: FSA solvency margin standard and 200% trigger — https://www.fsa.go.jp/en/refer/ins/capital.html [VERIFIED OPENED]; December 1998 creation of the (non-life) Policyholders Protection Corporation and the 100%-then-80% post-insolvency cover — https://www.sonpo.or.jp/en/regulations/002.html [VERIFIED OPENED]; Nissan Mutual Life 25 April 1997, "first since the postwar period" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nissan_Life (citing The Japan Times / Financial Times) [VERIFIED OPENED].

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **insurer (insurance company)** — a company that, in exchange for premiums, promises to pay money if a specified loss or event happens.
- **premium** — the price the policyholder pays the insurer for cover, usually monthly or yearly.
- **policyholder** — the person or business that owns an insurance policy and is entitled to its benefits.
- **claim** — a policyholder's formal request for the payment the insurance policy promises.
- **solvency** — the state of having enough assets to cover all liabilities; for an insurer, being able to pay expected future claims.
- **technical reserves / provisions** — money an insurer sets aside now to pay claims it expects to arise from policies already sold.
- **capital requirement** — a minimum amount of extra financial resources, above reserves, that a regulator makes an insurer hold as a buffer against unexpected losses.
- **risk-based capital** — a capital requirement whose size is calculated from the specific risks a firm carries, so riskier firms must hold more.
- **solvency margin ratio** — Japan's measure of an insurer's financial strength: its capital buffer divided by a measure of its risks, expressed as a percentage (200% is the intervention trigger).
- **Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR)** — the EU's main capital requirement under Solvency II, set so the insurer could withstand a loss expected only once in 200 years.
- **supervisor / regulator** — the public authority (e.g. EIOPA-coordinated national bodies, the NAIC's member states, Japan's FSA, the UK's PRA) that licenses insurers and monitors their soundness.
- **prompt corrective action / early intervention** — powers that let a supervisor step in before an insurer fails, e.g. demanding a recovery plan when the capital buffer falls below a set level.
- **guaranty association / policyholder protection fund** — an industry-funded scheme that pays at least part of policyholders' claims when an insurer becomes insolvent.
- **insolvency / failure** — when an insurer cannot meet its obligations and is put into a wind-down or rescue process.
- **standard-setter** — an international body (here the IAIS) that writes common rules or principles which national regulators then choose to adopt.

## Notes / cautions

- **Keep the capital idea plain.** Solvency II's SCR and the IAIS ICS are technically complex. For B1+/B2 readers the safe framing is: "insurers must hold enough money — reserves plus an extra capital cushion — to keep paying claims even after a very bad year (roughly a once-in-200-years loss)." Do not go into internal models, risk modules or the matching adjustment.
- **Dates to state precisely.** Solvency II applies "since 1 January 2016"; the revised Solvency II framework applies "from 30 January 2027" (agreed 2024) — it is not yet in force, so write it as upcoming. The IAIS ICS was "adopted on 5 December 2024" with jurisdictional assessments "from 2027" — adoption is not the same as being in force in every member country. NAIC Risk-Based Capital: life formula "adopted 1992, implemented 1993."
- **The Japanese life-insurer failures are ~25 years old.** They are good historical colour for "what happens when an insurer fails," but must be dated clearly (1997–2001) and framed as a past episode tied to the post-bubble economy, not a current event. Japan's system today (solvency margin ratio, protection corporations, and the new ESR regime from FY ending March 2026) is the live picture.
- **Japan's new ESR regime.** It took effect for the fiscal year ending 31 March 2026, so as of August 2026 insurers are reporting under it for the first time. The old "200% solvency margin ratio" is being superseded by an economic-value ESR with a 100% minimum. If the reading mentions the 200% figure, either attribute it to the pre-2026 system or note that the standard is changing. Try to cite an FSA English-language release on the new regime rather than a consultancy note.
- **AIG framing.** AIG's near-collapse was caused by credit-default swaps written by a financial-products unit, not by ordinary insurance underwriting; its insurance subsidiaries were separately regulated and largely solvent. Useful as "even a giant insurer group can threaten the financial system," but don't imply that normal life or property insurance caused it. The US$85 billion figure is the initial Fed loan (16 Sept 2008); the ~US$182 billion figure is the eventual total Fed + Treasury commitment, and taxpayers ultimately made a small positive return.
