# Research Brief — Book B 4.2 Government Debt and Deficits

Prepared 2026-09-11 (local session with working WebFetch/WebSearch). Facts checked against at least two independent sources; where an official page blocked automated fetch, news and research-institute co-sources are named.

## What the topic covers (2-3 sentences, plain)

A **deficit** is the gap in one year between what a government spends and what it raises in tax; **debt** is the accumulated total of past deficits; and **debt-to-GDP** measures that stock against the size of the economy. Governments run deficits routinely, and can carry high debt for decades — but the bond market sets a shifting, sometimes sudden, limit, demanding higher interest to keep lending when it doubts a government's plans. This topic is the political economy of deficits: why they are so hard to close, what a loss of market confidence looks like in real time, and why some highly indebted countries (Japan) borrow cheaply while others face a crisis at much lower debt levels.

## Candidate facts / anchors (6-10)

1. **The dated event: the UK "mini-budget," September 2022.** On 23 September 2022 the UK government announced about GBP 45 billion of tax cuts with no stated funding plan. Government bond ("gilt") prices fell at their fastest rate in decades, the pound hit a record low of about US$1.035, and forced selling by pension funds using "liability-driven investment" strategies threatened a wider crash. On 28 September the Bank of England launched an emergency operation to buy long-dated gilts "at whatever scale is necessary" (authorised up to GBP 65 billion). The finance minister was sacked on 14 October and Prime Minister Liz Truss resigned on 20 October, after 49 days in office — the shortest tenure of any UK prime minister; almost all the measures were reversed. Region: United Kingdom. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — NIESR ("The Truss/Kwarteng Mini Budget: One Year On") and EFG International / Financial Study Association accounts, consistent on the ~GBP 45 billion, the US$1.035 low, the 28 September BoE intervention and the up-to-GBP 65 billion authorisation; the "44/49 days" and reversal are widely reported.

2. **Deficit vs debt vs debt-to-GDP (definitional anchor).** The deficit is a yearly flow; the debt is the stock that flow adds to; the debt-to-GDP ratio compares that stock to annual national output, because a bigger economy can carry more debt. A government can shrink its debt-to-GDP ratio either by running surpluses or by growing (or inflating) GDP faster than the debt. Region: general mechanics. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — standard public-finance definitions across IMF, OECD and national-treasury material.

3. **Even the United States has been downgraded — three times.** Standard & Poor's cut the US credit rating from AAA to AA+ in August 2011 during a debt-ceiling standoff; Fitch did the same in August 2023; and on 16 May 2025 Moody's cut the US from Aaa to Aa1 — the last of the three major agencies to act — citing "large and growing" deficits, with total federal debt near US$40 trillion. The US still borrows easily because Treasuries are the world's benchmark safe asset, but the direction of travel is clear. Region: United States. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — MUFG and CSIS analyses of the May 2025 Moody's downgrade and CNBC/Axios coverage, all giving the Aa1 rating, the 16 May 2025 date, and the S&P-2011 / Fitch-2023 sequence.

4. **The euro area's rules and their limits.** The EU's Stability and Growth Pact set reference values of a 3%-of-GDP deficit and 60%-of-GDP debt back in 1997; the rules were suspended during COVID (2020-2023) and a reformed version took effect in 2024, replacing rigid targets with country-specific multi-year spending paths. France and several other members were placed under the EU's "excessive deficit procedure" in 2024. Region: European Union. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — European Commission material on the reformed economic-governance framework (2024) and news coverage of the 2024 excessive-deficit-procedure decisions; the 3%/60% reference values are longstanding. **Flag:** the reformed rules are new and their bite is not yet tested — describe as "revised in 2024," not as settled practice.

5. **An emerging-market case: Kenya, 2024.** Kenya's 2024 Finance Bill sought to raise roughly US$2.6 billion in new taxes, partly to meet targets under an IMF programme, at a time when interest payments alone were consuming about 37% of government revenue. Mass protests — in which dozens of people were killed — led President William Ruto to withdraw the bill on 26 June 2024 and turn instead to spending cuts. It is a stark illustration that deficit reduction is a political problem before it is an arithmetic one. Region: Sub-Saharan Africa (Kenya). **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — France 24 and Al Jazeera / NBC News coverage of the bill's withdrawal, the ~US$2.6 billion target and the "37% of revenue on interest" figure.

6. **Rising interest costs everywhere.** As central banks raised rates from 2022, the cost of servicing government debt climbed sharply across advanced economies. In the United States, net interest on the federal debt reached roughly US$880 billion in fiscal 2024 — more than the defence budget — and is projected to keep rising. Region: United States / advanced economies. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — US Congressional Budget Office / Treasury figures widely reported (Peterson Foundation, CBO) showing FY2024 net interest around US$880 billion, exceeding defence outlays. **Flag:** exact figure varies slightly by source and definition (net vs gross interest); use "roughly US$880 billion, more than defence spending," dated to fiscal 2024.

7. **Japan: the highest debt, the lowest worry.** Japan's general government gross debt is well over 200% of GDP — the highest of any large advanced economy — yet Japanese government bond yields have stayed very low and there has been no crisis of confidence. The reasons: the debt is overwhelmingly held domestically, the Bank of Japan owns roughly half of it, and decades of deflation kept interest rates near zero. The political consequence is that there has never been sustained pressure to close the deficit. Region: Japan. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — IMF and Ministry of Finance debt data, widely reported; the "domestic-held, BOJ owns ~half, ultra-low yields" explanation is standard. **Ledger note:** the precise figure (~206% of GDP, IMF WEO Apr 2026), the BOJ's ~48% holding and the ~8% foreign share are **primary** evidence in Book A 2.5 Sovereign Debt Management — this brief should use a rounded "well over 200%" and focus on the *political economy* (why deficits persist, the reluctance to raise the consumption tax), not the debt-management mechanics.

8. **Why deficits persist (the political-economy core).** Spending cuts and tax rises both concentrate visible pain on identifiable groups now, while the benefit — lower future borrowing costs — is diffuse and years away. Ageing populations push up pension and health spending automatically. And as long as bond markets keep lending cheaply, the discipline that would force a change does not arrive — until, as in the UK in 2022, it arrives all at once. Region: comparative. **[VERIFIED VIA 2+ SEARCH SNIPPETS]** — standard political-economy-of-deficits treatment across IMF, OECD and academic public-finance literature.

## The Japanese angle (short paragraph, per the shape plan)

Japan is the puzzle that makes this topic interesting. Its government debt is well above 200% of GDP — comfortably the highest in the rich world, and more than double the level that helped trigger crises in Greece or the UK. Yet Japanese government bonds still pay very low interest, and there has been no run on them. The explanation is structural: the debt is held almost entirely by Japanese institutions and households, the Bank of Japan itself owns around half of it, and a quarter-century of deflation kept the whole interest-rate structure near zero. The political consequence is that Japan has never faced the market pressure that forces other governments to act. Raising the consumption tax has been so costly at the ballot box that it has happened only three times in 35 years, spending on an ageing population keeps rising, and each downturn brings another stimulus package. Japan shows both that a country can carry extraordinary debt for a long time if the conditions are right — and that "the conditions are right" is not a plan.

## Terminology likely needed (candidate New terms)

- **budget deficit / surplus** — spending exceeding revenue in a year / revenue exceeding spending
- **primary deficit** — the deficit excluding interest payments on existing debt
- **government (sovereign) debt** — the total a government owes, mostly through bonds it has issued
- **debt-to-GDP ratio** — government debt measured against the size of the economy
- **government bond** — a tradable IOU issued by a government (gilts in the UK, Treasuries in the US, JGBs in Japan)
- **bond yield** — the effective interest rate a bond pays, which rises as its price falls
- **fiscal consolidation / austerity** — cutting the deficit through spending cuts or tax rises
- **debt ceiling** — a legal cap on total borrowing (used in the US), separate from the budget itself
- **credit rating (sovereign)** — an agency's assessment of a government's likelihood of repaying
- **"bond vigilantes" / market discipline** — investors demanding higher yields to punish a government seen as fiscally reckless
- **liability-driven investment (LDI)** — a pension-fund strategy that amplified the UK's 2022 gilt sell-off
- **debt monetisation** — a central bank effectively funding the government by buying its bonds
- **rollover risk** — the danger that a government cannot refinance maturing debt on acceptable terms
