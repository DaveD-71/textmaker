# Let's Talk Finance — Shared House Style

Applies to **both** books: *Let's Talk Finance* (Book A, rebuild of the existing 20 topics) and *Let's Talk Finance 2* (Book B, 20 new topics). Derived from the completed *Let's Talk: Investor Relations* resource and `Book_Draft_And_Edit_Process_Guide.md`. See `../Let's Talk Finance/PROJECT-PLAN.md` for scope, decisions, and phases.

---

## 1. Audience and level

- **Learner:** an educated adult **based in Japan**, primarily a Japanese first-language speaker, interested in current finance and the economy for professional or general reasons. Not necessarily a finance specialist.
- **CEFR: B1+ / B2.** One step easier than the IR resource (which was B2–B2+). This governs sentence structure and abstraction, not only word choice.
- **Purpose:** develop professional/academic English through substantial finance content, and generate genuine discussion. The reading must inform the discussion, not replace a textbook.

### 1a. Write from the Japanese reader's vantage point

**The subject of each topic is the financial topic, presented with a global perspective — not any one country.** These books are written *for* a Japanese reader, so that reader should recognise their own context inside the article, and it can be a natural entry point. But the article is about *cryptocurrency regulation* or *inflation*, covered across several regions (typically some mix of the US, EU/euro area, UK, Japan, and one or more of other Asia / Latin America / emerging markets), with **no single country dominating the word count or the structure**. Aim for at least three regions in a Reading, and do not let Japan be named far more often than the others.

The IR resource is the closest model: it uses Japanese companies freely (Toyota, Nintendo, TSE rules) alongside many others, and keeps a global focus. Do the same here. The difference from IR is only vantage point and audience — write so a Japanese reader is not treated as an outsider looking in — not "make it about Japan".

Two failure modes seen in the prototype passes, both to avoid:
- **Non-Japanese default, Japan as an aside.** "People often ask why central banks do not aim for zero inflation" assumes the reader's normal experience is meaningful inflation. For this audience it is not.
- **Over-correction into a Japan-centred article.** Opening on Japan, structuring the whole piece around Japan's timeline, naming Japan in every paragraph. That is equally wrong — too narrow, and not the point of the book.
The target is the middle: a global topic, Japan as one well-understood case among several.

- **Do not treat a non-Japanese situation as the default and Japan as the exception.** The failed prototype line — "People often ask why central banks do not aim for zero inflation" — assumes a reader whose normal experience is meaningful inflation. A Japanese reader's normal experience for ~25 years was near-zero inflation and deflation, and a central bank *trying to raise* inflation. Frame the topic the way this reader actually meets it, then widen out.
- **Know the Japanese baseline for each topic and state it where it changes how the reader should understand the issue:** the "lost decades" and deflation; the Bank of Japan's 2% target (2013) and its 2024 exit from negative rates; the weak yen and its effect on imported food and fuel; the 2025 rice-price shock; Japan's early crypto-exchange registration after Mt. Gox (2016–17) and Coincheck (2018); an ageing population and pension pressure; a high household savings rate held largely in cash and deposits; NISA tax-free investment accounts; keiretsu and cross-shareholdings; the Tokyo Stock Exchange's governance reforms; consumption-tax history; lifetime-employment norms under strain. Not every topic needs a Japan paragraph — include the baseline where its absence would leave the reader with the wrong mental model.
- **Still teach the international picture, as the IR book does.** Every topic should include at least one clearly labelled non-Japan example (US, EU, UK, other Asia, emerging markets). The goal is a Japanese reader who can also discuss how other countries handle the same issue.
- **Examples and scenarios** in Readings and questions may lean toward contexts a Japanese professional recognises (a trading company, a bank, a manufacturer, a listed firm under governance pressure, a household deciding what to do with cash savings), while staying company-neutral per §6.
- **Discussion questions** may address the reader directly as someone in Japan ("Should the Bank of Japan…", "Advise someone in Japan on…") where that is the natural framing.
- **Tone:** informed and matter-of-fact about Japan, never lecturing the reader about their own country, and never implying Japan is behind or unusual in a negative way. Where Japan did something first or differently (early crypto rules, long fight against deflation), say so plainly.

### What B1+/B2 means in practice (vs the IR articles)

| Dimension | IR (B2–B2+) | These books (B1+/B2) |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence length | Frequent 30–40-word sentences with 2–3 subordinate clauses | Mostly 15–25 words. One subordinate clause is normal; two is the ceiling; three is a rewrite. |
| Connectives | "which is why", "even as", "in service of", stacked "moreover" | Prefer "because", "so", "but", "for example", "this means". Keep "however" and "as a result" for genuine contrast/consequence. |
| Abstraction | Can open on an abstract principle | Lead with a concrete case, number, or situation. State the principle after the reader can see it. |
| New terms | Term used, defined 1–2 sentences later | Define the term in the same sentence it first does real work, or the sentence before. |
| Idiom / metaphor | "red line", "hold together at once", "run the company" | Allowed sparingly and only if transparent. Avoid finance-desk slang. |
| Passive voice | Common | Prefer active. Passive only when the actor is genuinely unknown or unimportant. |
| Paragraph | 5–7 sentences | 3–5 sentences, one idea each. |

### Paragraph writing (learning carried over from the IR project, 2026-08-25)

During IR development the user flagged two recurring paragraph faults. Both apply here from the start.

1. **A paragraph break must mark a genuine shift** — a new stage in the argument, a move to a different country or case, a turn from problem to response. Do **not** break a paragraph mid-idea just to make it shorter. Every paragraph is one complete unit of thought with real internal unity; if two consecutive paragraphs are still developing the same point, they are one paragraph. (The B1+/B2 target of 3–5 sentences is a guide, not a reason to split a unified idea.)
2. **No single-sentence "moral" or "kicker" ending.** A Reading must not close with a lone summarising sentence standing apart as its own paragraph ("Good regulation means keeping both in balance."). The final paragraph carries a substantive point of its own and is a full paragraph. Ending on a specific fact, a real trade-off, or a concrete open question is fine; ending on a detachable aphorism is not. This holds even though we otherwise avoid repeated patterns — the fix for pattern-repetition is variety, not a tacked-on closing line.

Read each finished Reading the way a news editor would: paragraphs cohesive and self-contained, no filler, and the body never refers to itself, the classroom, the source page, or the article's usefulness.

### What does NOT change from IR

- The specialist finance terms a topic genuinely needs stay in — they are the teaching point. The simplification is in the sentences *around* them, and in explaining each term in plain English on first use.
- Factual density: still ≥2 verifiable anchors per Reading, still every anchor cited.
- Discussion still demands extended reasoning. Stems are simpler; one task-style question per topic is enough (IR aimed for at least one; we hold to exactly one guaranteed, more if natural).

## 2. Topic template — six subsections

Numbered **Part.Topic** (e.g. `2.4`), subsections numbered beneath (`2.4.1. Goal`, `2.4.2. Reading`, …). Headings are Heading 2 for the topic title, Heading 3 for each subsection.

| # | Subsection | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| .1 | **Goal** | One sentence. States the teaching aim. Copied verbatim into the teacher answer book's `**Aim:**` line. |
| .2 | **Reading** | Fills exactly one A4 page as a 2-column article beneath the Goal block — **no overflow** (§5). ≈ **440–475 words**, 3–6 inline `[N]` markers. ≥1 real company / regulator / market event / transaction / policy; ≥2 factual anchors (number, date, name, %, value). Short paragraphs. Company-neutral. Jurisdiction-specific rules labelled (§4). Understandable with no outside reading. No promotion, no overgeneralization. Ending specific to this article. |
| .3 | **Vocabulary Focus** | `**New terms:**` 4–6 subject-specific terms (comma-separated). `**Recycled terms:**` 3–5 from earlier topics in the same book (write `none yet` for the book's first topic). Must match this book's `control/vocabulary-map.md` and its `glossary.md` tags exactly. |
| .4 | **Reading Questions** | Exactly 5. Answerable from the text alone. Mix across the set: main idea, specific detail, cause/effect, inference, meaning-in-context, writer's purpose. |
| .5 | **Discussion Questions** | Exactly 5. Progress analysis → evaluation → application. **At least one** task-style stem: rank / advise / compare / diagnose / rewrite / design / prepare. None may require confidential or personal financial disclosure. |
| .6 | **Source Notes** | Numbered, matching the `[N]` markers in the Reading. Each: `Publisher: Title — URL` on the entry line, URL may wrap to its own indented line. Every URL checked live during drafting; must resolve to a stable public page. |

## 3. Facts, numbers, citations

- **Every** factual anchor in a Reading carries an inline `[N]` marker (superscript in the DOCX; plain `[N]` in the markdown source).
- Sources: regulators, central banks, standard-setters, official statistics agencies, company IR/press pages, established news outlets (Reuters, FT, Bloomberg, Nikkei, AP, national broadcasters). Not blogs, not aggregators, not undated explainer sites.
- Prefer primary over secondary (the regulator's own page over a news report of it) where both exist and both are reachable.
- Date any time-sensitive claim in the text ("in 2024", "as of mid-2026") so it can be re-checked.
- If a fact cannot be verified to a reachable source, it does not go in the Reading.
- Fictional or composite examples may appear only as secondary illustration, never as the primary evidence for a point, and must be visibly hypothetical.

### Currency and number style

- **Non-USD:** ISO 4217 code + space + number — `JPY 45,095.3 billion`, `EUR 1,155 million`, `DKK 290.403 billion`, `GBP 2.4 billion`.
- **USD:** `US$` symbol, no space — `US$6.395 billion`, `US$29 billion`.
- Spelled-out currency name as a plain noun is fine (`95 yen per share`, `a few dollars`); never as a code substitute (`Yen 45,095.3 billion` ✗).
- Percentages: `3.8%`, `up 4.4%`. Percentage points: write "percentage points" in full the first time, then "pp" if repeated.
- Large numbers: `billion` / `trillion` spelled out; group digits with commas.

## 4. Jurisdiction labelling

Many finance rules are country-specific. When a Reading names one, label it:

- U.S.: "the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)…", "under U.S. rules…"
- EU: "in the European Union…", "the EU's …"
- Japan: "in Japan…", "Japan's Financial Services Agency (FSA)…", "the Tokyo Stock Exchange…"
- UK: "in the UK…", "the UK's Financial Conduct Authority…"

Apply the **same** label to the **same** rule every time it appears across the book — inconsistent labelling of one rule across topics was a flagged IR defect. Where a concept is broadly international (AML/KYC, deposit insurance, capital requirements), say so, then give one labelled national example.

Because the reader is in Japan (§1a), **Japan does not always need the "in Japan…" label when it is the reader's own default context** — but naming the specific body (Bank of Japan, FSA, Tokyo Stock Exchange) is still expected. Non-Japan rules always get their country label.

## 5. Page layout (the binding length constraint — measured from shipped IR Articles)

- **A4. Each topic occupies exactly 2 pages.**
- **Page 1 — Reading page:** single-column header zone — `X.Y. Title`, then `X.Y.1. Goal` + the one-sentence goal, then the `X.Y.2. Reading` heading — then the Reading body in **2 columns** filling the rest of the page. `[N]` markers superscript.
- **Page 2 — Questions page:** single column throughout — `X.Y.3. Vocabulary Focus`, `X.Y.4. Reading Questions` (numbered 1–5), `X.Y.5. Discussion Questions` (numbered 1–5), `X.Y.6. Source Notes` (numbered; publisher + title, URL indented).
- **Part divider:** its own page — `Part N`, the Part title, a single-column intro of ~150–190 words.
- The Reading must not spill to page 2; the questions page must not spill to a third page. Enforced by the Phase 7 rendered-PDF page-fit check, not by word count alone. Word count (~440–475) is the working target while drafting.

## 6. Structure and variety (pre-empting IR-project defects)

- **Vary the opening.** IR had 8 of 20 topics opening "On [date], X happened". Across each book, spread openings among: a dated event, a concrete situation/scene, a number that surprises, a short comparison, a common misconception, a direct question. No opening pattern used by more than ~3 topics per book.
- **Vary the article shape.** Not every Reading is scenario → principle → trade-off → closing moral. Use also: data-led explainer, short chronology/timeline, two-case comparison, problem → responses. Assign a shape per topic in the `control/` plan before drafting the batch.
- **No generic endings.** The last sentence must be about *this* topic, not "good finance means…".
- **No cross-topic evidence reuse.** The same company case or statistic must not be the primary evidence in two topics of the same book (or, ideally, across both books). `_lets-talk-finance-shared/topic-ledger.md` + each book's `control/company-and-geography-audit.md` are the guard. Light one-sentence callbacks are fine and encouraged for cohesion; copy-pasted evidence sentences are not.
- **Geographic spread.** Rotate real-world examples across regions (Americas, Europe, Asia-Pacific, and others where natural). Don't let one country dominate a book's examples.

## 7. Consistency checks (run every batch, and at QA)

For every topic, these three must match **exactly** — check by direct comparison, not by trusting a previous pass:

1. The Reading's `**New terms:**` line
2. The teacher answer book's `**Target vocabulary:**` line for that topic
3. The glossary's `[Part.Topic]` tags on those terms

Plus:

- Each topic's `## Goal` sentence === the teacher book's `**Aim:**` line for that topic, word for word.
- Every teacher-book Reading answer is verified against the **final** article wording (not an earlier draft).
- The vocabulary map's Topic row === the article's Vocabulary Focus section.

## 8. Markdown source conventions

- One file per topic in `drafts/articles/`, named `P-T_Title_In_Snake_Case.md` (e.g. `1-1_Cryptocurrency_Regulation.md`), matching the IR project's file naming.
- `00_How_This_Resource_Is_Organized.md` is the front-matter file.
- Headings: `## X.Y. Title`, then `### X.Y.1. Goal` … `### X.Y.6. Source Notes`.
- Inline citations as literal `[1]`, `[2]` in the prose; Source Notes as a numbered list.
- Straight quotes and hyphens in the markdown source; smart punctuation is applied at DOCX build time via the reference doc / Pandoc, not hand-typed (a plain-ASCII source survives shell round-trips safely).
- One blank line between paragraphs; no hard-wrapping inside paragraphs.
