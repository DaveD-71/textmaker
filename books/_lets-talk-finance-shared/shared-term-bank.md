# Let's Talk Finance — Shared Term Bank

Aligned plain-English definitions for finance terms that recur across **both** books.
When a term appears in a Reading in either book, its glossary entry in that book must be
consistent with the wording here (not necessarily identical prose, but the same meaning,
scope, and B1+/B2 register). Populated as drafting proceeds.

Each entry: **Term.** One-sentence plain definition. Then why it matters / how it comes up.
No `[Part.Topic]` tags here — those live in each book's own `glossary.md`.

---

Populated 2026-09-12 after both books' `glossary.md` files were drafted (`qa-todo.md` item 6). Cross-checked every headword appearing in both glossaries: of 133 Book A entries and 122 Book B entries, only 7 headwords collide exactly. All 7 were verified consistent in meaning, scope and B1+/B2 register between the two books (each book illustrates with its own examples, which is expected and fine) — no drift found requiring a fix.

**Bank run.** A situation in which many depositors try to withdraw their money from a bank at the same time, usually out of fear the bank cannot repay them, which can turn even a fundamentally sound bank into a failed one. Both books use a real March 2023 US regional-bank case as the illustrating example (Book A: Signature Bank; Book B: Silicon Valley Bank — kept deliberately distinct per the item-1 cross-topic-evidence fix), and both connect it to deposit insurance and a central bank's lender-of-last-resort role as the standard defences against one.

**Central bank.** The public institution responsible for a country's money supply, monetary policy and currency, and the ultimate issuer of a nation's cash. Both books treat it as a recurring institution rather than a one-topic term, returning to its role in setting interest rates, targeting inflation, intervening in currency markets, buying government bonds, and standing behind the banking system as lender of last resort.

**Consumer protection.** Rules and duties that shift the risk of financial harm — fraud, unfair terms, excessive risk-taking — away from an individual customer and onto the institution or system best placed to prevent or absorb it. Book A uses it broadly across fintech, data-privacy and financial-inclusion contexts; Book B anchors it specifically to the UK's post-2024 bank-reimbursement rule for authorised-push-payment fraud victims. Both readings are instances of the same underlying principle.

**Exchange rate.** The price of one currency expressed in terms of another. Both books treat it as market-determined (set by supply and demand, or by relative interest rates) and both use it to explain why a government borrowing in its own currency, or a country whose currency moves sharply against the dollar, faces different risks than one that does not.

**Progressive / regressive tax.** A progressive tax takes a larger share of income or spending from higher earners than from lower earners; a regressive tax does the opposite. Both books use this distinction to explain a real tax-fairness debate — Book A via Japan's investment-income flat-tax carve-out that can lower the effective rate for the very highest earners; Book B via VAT's criticised regressive effect on low earners.

**Stablecoin.** A privately issued digital token whose value is pegged to a national currency, overwhelmingly the US dollar, so each unit is designed to stay worth about one unit of that currency. Both books flag the same underlying regulatory concern: a stablecoin is only as safe as the real assets actually backing it, which is why regulators in major jurisdictions require issuers to hold and report on those backing assets.

**Stock index.** A single number tracking the combined price movement of a defined basket of shares, used as shorthand for how a market (or a segment of one) is performing overall. Both books use a real, large single-day or single-year index move as their illustrating example (Book A: the Nikkei 225's record one-day points fall on 5 August 2024; Book B: the S&P 500's 2025 return) to show how one index figure can capture a market-wide event.
